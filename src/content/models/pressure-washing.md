Algae on the north side.  
Driveway that looks like a parking lot after a long winter.  
A property manager who needs the walkways and dumpster pads clean before the next inspection.

How long do you wait — and who shows up with a **real truck and a process**, not a Home Depot special and a Facebook quote?

There’s a lot of things you can put off.  
“The house looks filthy from the street” is not one of them when curb appeal, HOA letters, or commercial foot traffic are on the line.

That’s **pressure washing / soft wash**.

Not a neighbor with a consumer washer and a garden hose.  
Not pure **exterior painting** as the whole company (paint cousin — different ticket and warranty).  
Not pure **window cleaning** or **gutter** as the identity of the book (common attach — separate spines).

A **truck + equipment + marketing** exterior-cleaning company that sells residential curb-appeal jobs, wins commercial accounts that tiny washers can’t touch, and lives or dies on **whether you can fill the calendar when the weather cooperates — without racing every uninsured weekend warrior to the bottom**.

Simple on a whiteboard: dirty exteriors → wash → invoice → next lead.  
Brutal if you confuse “anyone can buy a washer” with “this scales itself.”

## What the business actually is

You’re a **mobile exterior cleaning** company — and the category secretly contains two related crafts that share a truck but not the same risk:

1. **High-pressure washing** — concrete, driveways, sidewalks, dumpster pads, fleet/equipment (force + water volume; surface-dependent)  
2. **Soft wash / low-pressure chemical wash** — house siding, roofs, softer surfaces where blasting destroys the asset and the brand (chemistry + dwell time + rinse)

Typical menu:

1. **Residential house wash** — soft wash siding / brick / stucco; often the marketing hook  
2. **Driveways / sidewalks / patios / decks** — pressure on hardscape; mix with house wash in one ticket  
3. **Roof soft wash** — algae / black streaks; specialty risk (ladders, runoff, manufacturer warranties)  
4. **Commercial exteriors** — HOAs, apartments, restaurants, retail centers, government, assisted living, schools (Brian’s amplified teaching leans hard here)  
5. **Fleet / truck / equipment wash** (when you run it) — B2B cadence; water/reclaim rules get real  
6. **Attach services** — gutters, windows, concrete sealing, graffiti, kitchen hoods / grease (system- and skill-dependent)  
7. **Storm / seasonal recovery** — pollen dumps, wildfire ash, spring “everything looks terrible” spikes

Typical loop:

1. **Lead** — Google / LSA, Facebook/Nextdoor, truck wraps, door hangers, property-manager / HOA / realtor referrals, brand call center or national accounts (if franchised), prior-year commercial renewals  
2. **Quote** — photos, sq footage / surface type, access, water source, runoff / HOA rules, soft-wash vs pressure decision  
3. **Book the truck** — weather window, crew, chemical load-out  
4. **Wash + document** — before/after, chemical used, areas completed, property protected  
5. **Collect / review** — card on site; curb appeal is the ad that keeps working  
6. **Chase the next commercial renewal** — the quiet half of a *company* vs a busy Saturday driveway guy

**Scope lock for this page:** branded (or independent) **pressure washing / soft-wash exterior cleaning** built on **truck + commercial-grade equipment**, lead flow, weather/season honesty, and (when you can win it) commercial accounts that amateurs can’t fulfill. Roof soft wash, gutters, and windows appear in many real books — call them out in diligence; don’t pretend every greenfield starts as a national-accounts machine.

**Out of scope (different models):**  
- Pure **exterior painting / staining** as the company identity  
- Pure **window cleaning** routes (adjacent — Brian has covered that spine separately)  
- Pure **gutter guard install** project company  
- Solo “I own a consumer washer and a Facebook ad” as the whole *company* story  
- Mobile **car detailing** as the only book (related water/labor muscle — different customer)

Brian’s published frame on the category:

- Recurring-revenue YT (Rolling Suds segment): power washing named as a recurring / commercial-repeat concept — **not** because every driveway is a subscription, because **commercial clients can renew** when you can do work tiny washers can’t touch  
- Easy-entry punchline he puts on the table: **anybody can go to Home Depot, buy a power washer, and start** — so why would a serious operator spend real money on a system? Because the *professional* game is different equipment, process, and commercial work  
- Process teaching he amplifies: commercial-grade truck setups + specific chemical wash process; anecdote of a ~**2,000 sq ft house** washed in roughly **~20 minutes** vs a competitor taking **1–2 hours** — speed/process as a competitive wedge, not a guarantee you’ll match it  
- Bigger focus he highlights: **commercial** — HOAs, government buildings, assisted living, restaurants, portfolios that consumer washers can’t bid honestly  
- Operator anecdote he aired (top franchisee friend, Nick Heye / “Nick Hay” in transcript): won a roughly **~$70k** commercial job where the next closest bid sat around **~$100k** — priced to win reputation and build a renewing base, not to maximize one invoice  
- David Barr interview (investor lens Brian hosted): power washing as a **services** bet in a high-rate world; Barr as a heavy *user* (dozens of QSR exteriors / drive-thrus on a **quarterly** cadence); commercial LTV can crush CAC when you don’t get rebid every quarter; industry **incredibly fragmented** and needs **professionalization**; residential as supplement, commercial as thesis  
- Pink’s window interview adjacency: soft wash / pressure wash shows up as stadium / commercial attach — and as work window cleaners **sub out** when man-hours are short  
- Hiring teaching: power washing as a named **mobile** business for staffing boards / crew roles — recruiting wet, ladder-comfortable people is part of the job  
- Named systems exist (Rolling Suds, Shack Shine, Green Earth Powerwashing, regional independents, PE-curious consolidators). Treat them as **proof the category franchises and places** — FranServe activity has ranked Rolling Suds near the top of placement dollars in recent cuts — **not** as the point of this page

Mobile-service staging notes apply the same ladder as junk / pest / lawn: 1 truck you’re the closer-dispatcher-backup tech → 2–3 with a lead washer → multi-crew + sales seat → multi-market only after density and weather math work.

## How money comes in

You sell **completed exterior cleans** — residential curb-appeal tickets and commercial account work — not “hours on a timesheet” the customer sees, and not a free “I’ll rinse your sidewalk if you leave a review” hobby.

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Booked demand in weather windows** — leads answered, quotes converted, rain days recovered  
2. **Average ticket / mix** — driveway-only vs whole-home soft wash vs commercial portfolio vs roof  
3. **Marketing CAC vs close rate** — this category is **marketing-heavy**; paid search and Facebook get loud fast  
4. **Truck + equipment utilization** — billable wash days vs idle CAPEX, repair downtime, and “wrong tip / clogged pump” wasted trips  
5. **Labor %** — crew burdened cost on wet, physical, seasonal work  
6. **Chemical / water / disposal / reclaim costs** — soft-wash chemistry and commercial reclaim rules aren’t free  
7. **Commercial retention / renewals** — Barr’s user thesis: do it right and they don’t rebid every quarter  
8. **Attach / upsell** — gutters, windows, sealing, next-season prebook — margin *or* chaos if every job becomes custom

Gross margin lives in **price − crew labor (burdened) − chemicals/water − fuel − payment fees (− commissions/spiffs)**.  
Then overhead: truck/equipment leases or notes, insurance (GL + auto + workers’ comp; pollution/runoff where required), marketing, shop/yard if you stage gear, software, and (if franchised) royalty + brand/MAP + tech fees.

Revenue mix usually looks like some blend of:

| Channel | What it feels like |
|--------|---------------------|
| **Residential house / driveway wash** | High volume marketing. Mid tickets. Easy-entry noise is loudest here. Weather-sensitive. |
| **Roof soft wash** | Higher ticket. Ladder + runoff + warranty risk. Not a beginner’s first week. |
| **HOA / multi-family / commercial exterior** | Bigger tickets, bid process, insurance requirements. Recurring *when won*. Equipment bar is real. |
| **Fleet / equipment wash** | B2B cadence. Water/reclaim compliance. Different ops rhythm. |
| **Seasonal / storm recovery** | Spikes that feel like permanent income until November. |
| **Attach (gutters / windows / sealing)** | Margin when staffed; dilutes focus when every crew becomes a different company. |

Industry shape (education only): public affiliate / Item 19-style chatter for one large power-wash system has framed **average residential jobs** roughly around the **~$500** band and **average commercial jobs** in the **low-to-mid thousands** (sometimes cited ~**$5k+** on mature affiliate books) — useful for intuition, not your forecast. Healthy operators watch **revenue per truck-day**, **marketing CAC by channel**, **weather utilization**, and **commercial renewal rate** as hard as “leads.” Private interest in exterior cleaning exists because dirty buildings don’t stop — not because every Facebook washer prints.

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Houses get dirty. Concrete stains. HOAs send letters. Restaurants need clean pads and sidewalks. QSR and retail exteriors get washed on a cadence whether the stock market is up or down.

When the economy is good, discretionary curb-appeal and “whole neighborhood looks great” work spikes.  
When the economy is tight, commercial maintenance and “the property has to look acceptable” work still creates trucks to fill — ticket mix and residential volume shift; demand doesn’t vanish.

That’s why Brian’s guests and segments put power washing in the **services / mobile exterior** family: the need is visible from the street; **your calendar and your equipment still can fail**.

The constraint isn’t “exteriors get dirty.”

It’s **marketing discipline + equipment reality + weather/season honesty + commercial fulfillment capability** in *your* metro.

Easy entry (consumer washer + Craigslist + a logo on a door) keeps competition permanently loud. Process, insurance, soft-wash competence, and commercial-grade trucks are how a *company* separates from a guy who rented a machine for the weekend.

## Micro: what actually drives one company

**Marketing engine (this category is loud)**  
Paid search, LSAs, Facebook, door hangers, wraps — everyone bids the same keywords in spring. Brand, before/after proof, and commercial relationships are the escape hatch. Without a real lead machine, a beautiful truck sits.

**Soft wash vs pressure (competence is the product)**  
Blasting siding like a driveway is how you buy callbacks, damaged substrates, and lawsuit fame. Soft-wash chemistry, dwell time, plant protection, and rinse discipline are the difference between “professional” and “we ruined the paint.” Amateurs undercutting you often skip this until someone files a claim.

**Equipment + truck as the factory**  
Commercial pumps, tanks, reels, hot-water options, surface cleaners, proper soft-wash setups — not a $199 consumer unit. Under-equip → you can’t win the jobs Brian’s teaching cares about. Over-equip → notes and repairs eat quiet Tuesdays and rain weeks. CAPEX is real; “asset-light” is a relative term here.

**Seasonality & weather (climate is a co-owner)**  
Sun Belt can lean longer. Northern / wet markets slam in spring–fall and face winter holes (plus rain-outs that don’t care about your ad spend). Owners who treat July like permanent income meet November as a cash crisis — unless fleet, indoor-adjacent, commercial contracts, or reserves were the plan *before* Labor Day.

**Commercial sales vs residential grind**  
Brian’s Rolling Suds framing and Barr’s user thesis both point the same direction: **commercial is where the company story lives** — HOAs, government, assisted living, restaurants, multi-site portfolios. Winning them needs insurance, documentation, bid literacy, and equipment that can finish on schedule. Residential fills gaps and builds reviews; it also invites the Home Depot crowd.

**Crew recruiting & retention**  
Wet, early, ladder-adjacent, chemical-handling, physical work. Brian’s hiring content casually uses power washing as the mobile example for crew roles for a reason — you need people who show up when it’s miserable and don’t treat soft wash like a toy. Demand without washers is a ringing phone and angry “they never came after the rain delay” reviews.

**Owner role (honest version)**  
Early stage: quoting, marketing CAC, riding jobs, hiring, weather triage, cash, and often backup washer.  
Not “I bought a logo and went fishing.”  
Later: sales seat + lead tech so you’re not every wand and every Google reply — still not passive.

**Systems**  
CRM / quoting, photo documentation, chemical logs, review engine, weather reschedule scripts, commercial contract renewals, equipment maintenance schedules. Guesswork doesn’t scale past one heroic truck.

- Commercial focus: HOAs, government, assisted living, restaurants — work “tiny power washers can’t even touch”  
- Bid anecdote (top franchisee friend): ~**$70k** won job vs ~**$100k** next bid — reputation / volume strategy, not a margin template  
- Barr thesis on Brian’s show: commercial repeat LTV vs CAC; quarterly QSR exterior user; fragmentation → professionalization opportunity; residential supplements  
- Recurring angle: commercial renewals and multi-visit contracts — **not** “every driveway is SaaS”

**Startup / investment shape (education — verify current FDD Item 7 / local quotes):**

- Buying an existing book can add customer-list cost — diligence weather dependence and how much revenue is real commercial renewals vs one-season Facebook fluff

**Operating intuition (industry education, not forecasts):**

- Residential tickets often discussed mid-hundreds; whole-home + roof climb; commercial jumps into thousands and five figures on portfolios  
- Public affiliate chatter for one system: residential averages roughly ~**$500**; commercial averages sometimes framed ~**$5k+** on mature books — verify locally  
- Item 19-style franchisee revenue dispersions in young exterior-cleaning systems can be **wide** (low six figures to seven figures in disclosed cohorts) — average ≠ median ≠ you  
- Rain-outs and short seasons destroy “annualized” math copied from Sun Belt Instagram  
- Marketing is a line item that can rival labor in spring; owners who underprice to “stay busy” fund their competitors’ education

Diligence questions that matter more than memorizing averages:

- What’s **revenue per truck per wash-day** last 12 months? Rain-out / idle %?  
- Mix: residential vs roof vs commercial vs fleet vs attach?  
- Marketing CAC by channel and close rate — spring vs off-season?  
- Soft-wash vs pressure competence — claim / callback history on siding and roofs?  
- Average residential ticket vs average commercial ticket? Renewal rate on commercial?  
- Equipment list (and age) — can you *actually* fulfill the commercial jobs you want to sell?  
- Labor % (burdened), overtime in peak weeks, injury rate?  
- Insurance / runoff / reclaim / HOA compliance posture for *this* metro  
- Off-season cash plan if northern / wet — fleet, commercial contracts, reserves, or owner draws that ignore November?  
- If franchised: royalty + brand + required local spend + software as % of gross; territory density; national-accounts reality vs brochure  
- Owner hours on the wand vs on sales/recruiting — is this a transferable company?  
- If buying a book: verified commercial renewals, equipment condition, and what’s *repeat* vs one-time spring fluff

What that means in plain English:

The *category* has durable, visible demand and can build commercial renewals when equipment, process, and sales work.  
A *specific book* can look slammed every May and still be broke — fat ad spend, rain weeks, cheap residential only, damaged siding claims, or an owner who never got off the wand.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

**1. Easy entry is the trap**  
Brian puts it on the table: anyone can buy a washer at Home Depot and *look* like a competitor by Monday. Trust, insurance, soft-wash competence, show-up rate, and commercial-grade fulfillment are the product — not the cheapest “I’ll blast your driveway Saturday” text. Simple to *start*. Hard to *build a company*.

**2. Marketing-heavy forever**  
Spring keywords and Facebook leads get expensive when every truck in town bids the same intent. Without referral density and commercial relationships, you fund a CAC war that amateurs don’t even know they’re fighting.

**3. Confusing a consumer washer with a business**  
A weekend machine and a hose is a side hustle. Commercial tanks, surface cleaners, soft-wash systems, hot water, reclaim — different CAPEX, different jobs you can win, different insurance conversation. Under-equip and you only compete where the price war is worst.

**4. Soft-wash / surface competence landmines**  
Wrong tip, wrong pressure, wrong chemistry, unprotected plants, stained oxidate — callbacks and claims. Roof soft wash without process is how you meet lawyers. “I pressure wash everything” is not a method.

**5. Seasonality & weather are co-owners**  
Rain-outs, short northern seasons, heat that cooks crews, pollen weeks that spike then vanish. Owners who annualize peak months meet winter (or monsoon season) as a cash crisis.

**6. Residential price war with uninsured amateurs**  
They undercut. You carry commercial auto, workers’ comp, chemical liability, and real equipment notes. Racing them to the bottom is how professionals go broke while Instagram still looks busy.

**7. Commercial is the opportunity — and the capability test**  
Brian’s teaching loves HOA / government / assisted living / restaurant work. Winning it needs bid skill, insurance, documentation, and trucks that finish. Selling commercial you can’t fulfill creates reputation death faster than no marketing at all.

**8. Equipment downtime & maintenance**  
Pumps, reels, engines, tips, heaters — the factory breaks. No backup plan means sold jobs become review bombs. Lease payments continue during shop weeks.

**9. Crew scarcity in peak weeks**  
Everyone wants washers in May. Training soft-wash correctly is slower than hiring a strong back. Peak-season no-shows turn weather windows into missed revenue you don’t get back.

**10. Runoff / environmental / HOA / municipal rules**  
Soap to storm drains, reclaim requirements, HOA chemical rules, apartment quiet hours. Amateurs ignore this until a property manager or city doesn’t. Compliance isn’t optional paperwork in serious commercial work.

**11. Working capital on a “simple” wash story**  
Truck note, insurance, chemicals, spring ad spend, and payroll burn while weather and learning curves hit. Undercapitalized launches stall when CAC and rain hit the same month.

**12. Owner still on the wand (or still the only closer)**  
If the company only works when you’re the tech, the estimator, and the Google-reply department, you own a job with a wrap — same staging truth as junk and lawn.

**13. Sales engine that outruns fulfillment**  
Ads can fill a calendar fast. If equipment, soft-wash QA, and crews can’t keep up — or rain stacks the backlog — you bought churn and chargebacks with a truck payment attached.

**14. Attach-service distraction**  
Gutters, windows, sealing, roofs — profitable *when* you have process. Every new service is a new way to damage property and dilute the crew. Focus is a strategy.

**15. Franchise stack (if you go that route)**  
Royalty + brand fund + local ad minimums + tech fees + rules + territory vs playbook, truck specs, national accounts, and peer network. Worth it only if the system fills commercial-capable trucks and trains process — not because the wrap looks good on Instagram. Young systems can grow outlet counts fast; diligence Item 19 cohort quality and Item 21 franchisor health like an adult.

**16. Buying a tired spring book without renewal math**  
Customer lists look like assets until you discover last year’s revenue was one-time Facebook driveways and a commercial account that already switched. Verify renewals and equipment — not “we’ve washed 10,000 houses historically.”

**17. Semi-absentee fantasy**  
Manager salary on a thin, seasonal book is how shops bleed. Semi-absentee usually needs a mature multi-truck machine with real commercial renewals — not a greenfield hope or one hot May.

**18. Confusing “houses get dirty” with “easy money”**  
Timeless, visible demand is real. Your P&L still needs operators who can win the marketing war, the weather war, and the easy-entry price war at the same time.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means pressure washing / soft wash is a **real truck + equipment + marketing business** where **easy entry creates permanent noise** — and scale only shows up when process, commercial fulfillment, and seasonality discipline actually work.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re wired to sell *and* fulfill — quotes, crews, and wet quality control  
- Marketing CAC puzzles don’t scare you (this category won’t let you ignore them)  
- You’re willing to learn soft-wash chemistry and surface limits — not just “more PSI”  
- Commercial account hunting (HOAs, PMs, multi-site) sounds exciting once one truck is dialed  
- Truck-by-truck scale in a loud, easy-entry market sounds like a fight worth picking  
- If northern / wet: you can stomach (and plan for) off-season and rain-out cash holes

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want passive / semi-absentee from month one  
- You refuse to market hard or recruit people who work outside in misery  
- You’re allergic to chemicals, ladders, runoff rules, and “you etched my concrete” realities  
- You thought Home Depot equipment *was* the business plan  
- You’re undercapitalized for a real truck, insurance, spring CAC, and weather reserves  
- You want pure paint / roofing *project* energy every week and hate weather-gated field days  
- You’re looking for a craft job with a logo (you *being* the only washer forever)

Franchise vs independent is a separate call.  
Same category either way: generate leads → quote surfaces correctly → wash without destroying the asset → collect → earn the commercial renewal → escape the amateur price war on trust and capability.

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “houses get dirty.”

You’re buying the right to run a **truck + equipment + marketing machine** that turns visible exterior friction into completed jobs — and, if you can earn it, commercial renewals — while the easy-entry crowd keeps the residential price war loud forever.

Some people should start with one serious truck, master soft-wash vs pressure, and prove they can acquire jobs profitably before adding leases.  
Some people should buy a tired local book (or a franchise resale) and install commercial sales + process — consolidators and franchise systems chase this category because the street-level demand is obvious.  
Some people should niche (HOAs, QSR exteriors, fleet, roof soft wash) once the core engine works — Brian’s teaching keeps pointing at commercial as the grown-up game.  
Some people should walk — and put the same capital into a vehicle with cleaner indoor economics, less weather surface area, or less permanent amateur noise.

The question isn’t “do exteriors get dirty?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a stressful wash company that only works when you’re the wand, the ads manager, the weatherman, and the backup crew?**
