Stuff breaks.

Always has. Always will.

Door won’t latch. Fan wobbles. Drywall hole from moving day. Deck board soft. Toilet runs. Ceiling fan box. “Can you just…?”

That’s the handyman business.

Not a licensed plumbing shop.  
Not a full GC remodel company.  
Not “I own a truck and a tool belt” as the whole company.

A local **dispatch + technician utilization** machine that turns a high variety of small (and sometimes not-so-small) jobs into a dense schedule — then upsells trust into bigger projects.

Brian’s frame is blunt:

Literally everyone needs a good handyman.  
Robots aren’t replacing toilets anytime soon.

Simple on a whiteboard.  
Brutal if you confuse “people have odd jobs” with “this runs itself.”

---

## What the business actually is

You’re a **scheduling / craftsman** company wearing a repair brand.

Typical menu (high variety by design):

1. **Punch-list / odd jobs** — hang TVs, patch drywall, fix doors, caulk, minor carpentry, fixture swaps  
2. **Light trade-adjacent** — basic electrical/plumbing *within* local rules (GFCI, disposal, toilet swap — not panel rewires or whole-house plumbing)  
3. **Small projects** — decks, tile, bathroom refresh, fence repair, aging-in-place tweaks  
4. **Commercial / B2B layer** (when you earn it) — property managers, offices, retail punch lists, realtor turnovers  
5. **Niche overlays** — seniors (some systems lean here), real-estate agent speed work, landlord/turnover lists

Loop on a real day:

1. **Call / lead** — Google, LSA, Angi/aggregators, referral, realtor, prior customer, van wraps, sometimes a call center  
2. **Book & estimate** — phone quote when safe; eyes-on when photos lie  
3. **Dispatch** — right tech, right tools, right geography, right time window  
4. **Complete + collect** — card on site; materials marked up or passed through  
5. **Upsell / next visit** — “while we’re here” punch list; book the bathroom refresh; membership/subscription if you run one  
6. **Review / warranty** — your name stays on every patch and hinge

**Scope lock for this page:** residential (and light commercial) **handyman / multi-trade odd-jobs service** — W-2 or employee craftsmen on branded trucks, office/dispatch, scheduling density as the engine. Upsell into projects is part of the model; becoming a full remodel GC is optional drift, not the core.

**Out of scope (different models):**  
- Solo Craigslist handyman as the whole company (craft job with a phone)  
- Pure licensed plumbing / HVAC / electrical *service shops* (see those pages)  
- Pure painting contractor (narrower scope, different utilization math)  
- Full design-build / kitchen GC as the primary identity

Brian’s teaching frame on the category:

- Demand is **timeless** — 1950s houses need repairs; new builds need warranty work; everything in between needs maintenance  
- Ticket range he publishes: roughly **$300 fixes → $15k renovations** (decks, tile, bathroom remodels named as examples)  
- **W-2 employees** handle most of it; when work exceeds scope, you **sub specialized trades** and act as the relationship / GC cut  
- Scale story: **more trucks & technicians = more revenue** — he frames roughly **~$200k–$400k per truck per year** as a category intuition band (education, not a forecast)  
- Most owners start residential, then layer **commercial** for steadier cash  
- Niche proof exists (senior-focused systems; realtor-turnaround focus) — niches are strategy, not magic  
- He’d personally push a **subscription** overlay (~$100/mo for quarterly visits + hours + project discounts) — notes a few brands dabble; **nobody has fully cracked it** yet  
- Named systems exist (Mr. Handyman, Ace Handyman, Handyman Connection, House Doctors, TruBlue, Monty’s, Local Handyman, etc.). Treat them as proof the category franchises — not as the point of this page

His one-line operator truth (public comment on an Ace-style concept): big brand helps. **Hardest part is finding quality handymen.**

## How money comes in

You sell **completed jobs and billable tech time**, not “hours on a timesheet” in the abstract.

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Booked demand** — phone answered, lead converted, calendar filled  
2. **Scheduling density** — billable hours vs windshield, parts runs, and “forgot the bit” second trips  
3. **Average ticket / mix** — $300 punch-list vs multi-hour project vs bathroom refresh  
4. **Close / upsell in the home** — diagnose → options → book the next visit before you leave  
5. **Tech count you can actually staff and keep** — empty vans don’t bill  
6. **Repeat + referral** — trust compounds; one-star reviews erase months of ads

Gross margin lives in **price − materials − tech labor (burdened) − commissions/spiffs (− sub cut on specialty)**.  
Then overhead: dispatch/office, vehicles, insurance, marketing, software (FSM/CRM), tools replenishment, and (if franchised) royalty + brand fund + tech fees.

Revenue mix usually looks like some blend of:

| Channel | What it feels like |
|--------|---------------------|
| **Residential odd jobs** | High variety. Lower ticket. Density or die. |
| **Small projects / remodel-adjacent** | Higher ticket. Longer cycle. Scope risk. Margin if estimated well. |
| **Commercial / property / realtor** | Steadier when won. Price pressure from PMs; speed/trust from agents. |
| **Subscription / membership (if you run it)** | Recurring visits. Smoother book. Still rare as a fully cracked category play. |

Industry shape outside Brian (education only): many U.S. markets still see routine handyman tickets discussed roughly in the **mid-hundreds to low-thousands**; multi-tech project work jumps into the **low-to-mid five figures**. Healthy operators watch **billable hours per tech per day** and **revenue per truck** as hard as “leads.”

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Housing stock ages. Closets overflow with deferred maintenance. Boomers age in place and stop climbing ladders. Licensed trades are scarce and expensive for a $400 job — so the “can you just fix this?” call goes to a generalist who shows up this week.

When the economy is good, people upgrade decks, baths, and punch lists before listing.  
When the economy is tight, stuff still breaks — and a large share of calls are **can’t-wait / annoying-enough-to-pay**.

That’s Brian’s “recession-proof / timeless demand” pitch.

The constraint isn’t “houses exist.”

It’s **reliable craftsmen + dense routing + trust** in *your* market.

Easy entry (van + tools + Facebook ads) keeps competition loud. Brand, systems, and recruiting are how a *company* separates from a guy with a ladder.

## Micro: what actually drives one company

**Dispatch / call handling**  
Missed calls and slow estimates kill leads you already paid for. Some owners insist on local CSRs for evenings; others lean call centers. Either way: speed is part of the product.

**Scheduling density**  
This is the quiet P&L. Three $350 jobs with 40 minutes of drive between each is a different business than one $1,200 job in a tight cluster. Route software helps. Geography and book discipline decide.

**Technician recruiting & retention**  
Brian’s own words: hardest part is finding quality. You need people who can swing a hammer *and* talk a homeowner through options without creating callbacks or license violations. Employer brand > another Indeed blast.

**Scope control**  
Handyman menus invite “can you also…?” Scope creep without price = free work. Scope creep into licensed trades without credentials = existential risk. Clear yes/no lists beat hero culture.

**Upsell into projects**  
The model’s upside is turning trust from a door hinge into a bathroom refresh or deck. The failure mode is becoming an accidental GC with no estimating discipline.

**Materials & truck stock**  
Understock → second trips → density dies. Overstock → cash in bins. Project materials are a different animal than “common fasteners.”

**Owner role (honest version)**  
Early stage: recruiting, marketing/lead flow, pricing, culture, cash, and often backup tech/estimate hero.  
Not “I bought a logo and went fishing.”  
Same ownership muscle Brian uses elsewhere: **hire the tradespeople; run the business.**

**Systems**  
FSM/CRM, recorded calls, lead-source tracking, flat-rate or estimate books, QA photos, review engine. Guesswork doesn’t scale past one heroic craftsman.

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

**Brian’s published category intuition (newsletter education):**

- Job size span: roughly **$300 → $15k** depending on punch-list vs renovation-adjacent work  
- Truck productivity framing: roughly **$200k–$400k revenue per truck per year** *as a teaching band* — utilization and ticket mix decide whether that’s fantasy or baseline  
- Scale lever: more trucks + techs (not “work harder on the same van”)  
- Subscription thought experiment: ~**$100/month** for quarterly visits + capped hours + project discounts — idea, not a proven category standard

**Startup / investment shape (education — verify current FDD Item 7):**

- Public aggregator reads of major handyman brands commonly sit roughly **mid-$100ks to low-$200ks** all-in (vehicles, tools, insurance, working capital, fee)  
- Royalty + brand-fund stacks in that peer set often land around **mid-to-high single digits of gross combined**, plus software/tech fees — system-specific  
- Brian’s broad home-services teaching often lives in a similar **~$100k–$200k** conversation for asset-light field services — *category* band, not a specific offer

**Operator anecdote (podcast education, one resale path — not a template):**  
A Business With Beers interview with a first-time handyman franchisee described buying an existing multi-territory book (~**$800k** trailing sales conversation; seller SDE framed roughly mid-**$100ks**; purchase price discussed around **~$300k** with vans/tools in the deal) and then investing heavily in staff + marketing to grow. Useful for “this is a real ops business,” not for your forecast.

**FranServe placement history** shows Ace Handyman, TruBlue, Mr. Handyman, Monty’s, and other handyman labels actively awarding — **category proof**, not your P&L.

Diligence questions that matter more than memorizing averages:

- What’s billable utilization by tech last 12 months? Windshield %?  
- Average ticket and job mix (odd job vs project vs commercial)?  
- First-trip completion / callback rate?  
- Tech vacancy / time-to-hire / tenure?  
- Close rate by lead source (Google vs aggregator vs referral vs realtor)?  
- % of revenue from top 20 customers (PM concentration risk)?  
- License / insurance posture for *this* state — what are techs allowed to touch?  
- If franchised: royalty + MAP + required local spend + software fees as % of gross  
- Owner hours on the tools vs on recruiting/dispatch — is this a transferable company?

What that means in plain English:

The *category* has durable demand and can scale truck-by-truck.  
A *specific shop* can be busy and broke — or quiet because it can’t hire.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

**1. Finding quality craftsmen**  
Brian’s line. Demand without reliable people is just a ringing phone and angry Google reviews.

**2. Scheduling density / windshield death**  
Low-ticket variety without route discipline means you work hard and net nothing.

**3. High variety = high chaos**  
Every job is different. Estimating errors, missing tools, and “while you’re here” freebies destroy the day.

**4. Scope & licensing landmines**  
Light electrical/plumbing helps tickets — until a board, insurer, or lawsuit says you crossed a line.

**5. Easy-entry competition**  
Any competent DIYer can look like a competitor on Facebook. Trust, show-up rate, and warranty are the product.

**6. Underpricing to win the book**  
New owners discount to fill vans, then can’t raise prices fast enough when wages move.

**7. Lead conversion laziness**  
Paid leads die if nobody answers, estimates slow, or follow-up is soft. Density starts at the phone.

**8. Upsell without project discipline**  
Chasing $15k bathrooms without estimating skill creates warranty monsters and unpaid change orders.

**9. Property-manager price pressure**  
Commercial/PM work can steady the calendar — and train your team to race to the bottom.

**10. Owner still on the truck (or still the only closer)**  
If the company only works when you’re the hero tech, you own a job with a wrap.

**11. Callbacks across a thousand job types**  
One leaky toilet swap or crooked TV mount becomes a neighborhood story.

**12. Working capital on a “small ticket” business**  
Payroll, vans, tools, and marketing burn while the calendar fills. Undercapitalized launches stall in month four.

**13. Semi-absentee fantasy**  
Manager salary on thin early revenue is how shops bleed. Semi-absentee usually needs a mature book — not a greenfield hope.

**14. Franchise stack (if you go that route)**  
Royalty + rules + territory vs playbook, brand, software, and recruiting help. Worth it only if the system actually fills trucks and trains people — not because the logo looks good on a van.

**15. Confusing “stuff breaks” with “easy money”**  
Timeless demand is real. Your P&L still needs operators.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means handyman is a **real service business** where labor utilization, recruiting, and schedule density *are* the product — and projects are the upside, not the free lunch.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re wired to hire, schedule, and lead craftsmen (Brian’s hire-the-tech lens)  
- You like operational puzzles (routing, mix, upsell) more than being the only person with a tool belt  
- High-variety residential energy sounds fun — not exhausting  
- You can hold standards on quality and scope while moving volume  
- Truck-by-truck scale (and maybe commercial / senior / realtor niches later) sounds exciting once one van is dialed

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want passive / semi-absentee from month one  
- You refuse to recruit or manage technicians  
- You’re actually looking for a craft job with a logo (you *being* the only handyman)  
- You want pure high-ticket sales with long project cycles and hate punch-list chaos (roofing/cabinets energy ≠ this day-to-day)  
- You’re undercapitalized for vans, tools, payroll ramp, and marketing  
- You’re allergic to compliance, insurance, and “the board says…” realities

Franchise vs independent is a separate call.  
Same category either way: demand → dispatch → tech → dense route → collect → earn the next job.

## Risks (the short list)

- Can’t hire / keep craftsmen → capacity ceiling  
- Low density → high ad spend, thin profit  
- Scope/license miss → existential  
- Project upsell without estimating skill → callbacks and cash traps  
- Owner dependency on the tools or the phone  
- PM concentration or aggregator CAC spiral  
- Royalty drag without lead/recruiting advantage  
- Thinking “everyone needs a handyman” substitutes for operator skill

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “stuff breaks.”

You’re buying the right to run a **dispatch machine** that turns household (and light commercial) friction into booked, completed jobs — if you can recruit craftsmen, pack the calendar, control scope, and convert trust into the next ticket.

Some people should start greenfield with one or two trucks.  
Some people should buy a tired local book and install systems (resale path exists in this category).  
Some people should niche (seniors, agents, commercial punch lists) once the core engine works.  
Some people should walk — and put the same capital into a vehicle with cleaner recurring route economics or a narrower trade fit.

The question isn’t “do houses need fixing?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a stressful van company that only works when you’re the dispatcher, the closer, and the backup tech?**

If you want help comparing doors (this model vs plumbing vs painting vs others), that’s a conversation.  
Not a pitch deck.
