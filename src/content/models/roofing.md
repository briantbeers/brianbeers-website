Everyone needs a roof over their head.

When it fails, everything under it is at risk.  
Water. Mold. Insurance fights. A five-figure decision with a stranger on your ladder.

That’s why roofing stays on the “essential” list.

But here’s the part most outsiders miss:

A modern residential roofing *company* (especially the franchise versions Brian talks about) is often **not** “you up on a roof with a nail gun.”

It’s a **sales + project management + insurance navigation** business that **orchestrates** crews.

High ticket.  
Storm spikes.  
Commission sales.  
1099 production.

Simple on a whiteboard.  
Brutal if you confuse “people need roofs” with “this runs itself.”

---

## What the business actually is

You sell roof repairs and replacements (and often related exterior work: gutters, siding, sometimes soft-wash / storm restoration adjacent).

Typical loop:

1. **Lead** — storm canvass, Google, referral, insurance adjuster relationship, prior customer  
2. **Inspect / document** — ladder, photos, increasingly drones  
3. **Propose** — retail cash job *or* insurance claim path  
4. **Close** — financing, deductible, supplements, scope fights  
5. **Produce** — schedule materials + crew (often subcontracted)  
6. **Collect** — homeowner cash, or insurance ACV → RCV / depreciation / supplements  
7. **Warranty** — your name stays on the house for years

**Scope lock for this page:** contractor / franchise that sells and installs roofs.  
Not a materials distributor.  
Not a pure public adjuster.  
Not “I own a truck and a ladder” solo craftsman (that’s a job with a logo — different math).

Brian’s teaching frame on the category:

- Residential roofing is **high ticket**  
- It’s a **very sales-driven** model  
- After a storm: teams of **100% commission door knockers** canvassing for hail / wind damage  
- Homeowner insurance often covers the job (minus deductible / fight)  
- Actual install often done by **1099 contractors**  
- Highly competitive — and can still become a multi-million-dollar book *quickly* if sales + production click  
- Franchisors lean on marketing, insurance billing systems, sales training, and tech (drones for estimating / PM)

Named systems exist (Mighty Dog, Bumble, Action, etc.). Treat them as proof the category franchises — not as the point of this page.

## How money comes in

You sell **jobs**, not hours.

A single residential re-roof is often mid-four to mid-five figures depending on squares, pitch, material, tear-off, and market.  
Commercial / large flat roofs can jump into the mid-five and beyond (Brian’s own buyer-side social: two NJ quotes ~$73k and ~$80k on ~5,000 sq ft — that’s the *buyer* staring at the ticket, wondering where materials end and margin begins).

Revenue mix usually looks like some blend of:

| Channel | What it feels like |
|--------|---------------------|
| **Retail / cash** | Homeowner pays. Faster cash. Competitive bids. Trust + reviews matter. |
| **Insurance / storm** | Claim-driven. Canvass after weather. Scope + supplements. Slower pay. Working capital hungry. |
| **Commercial** | Bigger tickets, longer cycles, different crews/relationships. Hedge when residential storms are quiet. |

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Close rate on qualified leads** — sales process, not “more canvassers” alone  
2. **Average job size / upsells** — material tier, accessories, related exterior  
3. **Production quality + speed** — callbacks and warranty eat margin  
4. **Collections discipline** — especially insurance (ACV upfront, depreciation later, supplements)  
5. **Crew capacity** — you can sell yourself into a backlog you can’t staff  
6. **Mix** — pure storm-chase looks exciting until the weather (or carriers) change

Gross margin lives in **price − materials − production labor − sales commission − warranty**.  
Then overhead: office, software, insurance (yours), trucks/branding, local marketing, and (if franchised) royalty + brand fund.

Industry shape (education only, not Brian claims): many shops see materials + crew as the majority of the job cost; healthy operators watch insurance vs cash mix and cash conversion cycle as hard as “revenue.”

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Roofs wear out. Storms don’t ask the Fed for permission. Housing stock ages.

When the economy is good, people upgrade and replace before failure.  
When the economy is bad, roofs still leak — and insurance still pays *some* of the time.

That’s the “essential” pitch.

The constraint isn’t demand in the abstract.

It’s **trust + sales capacity + production capacity + cash** in *your* territory.

Hail belts and wind corridors create storm surges.  
Quiet weather markets force more retail / commercial muscle.  
Same category. Different operating reality.

## Micro: what actually drives one company

**Sales engine**  
Brian’s anti-fit teaching for semi-absentee: sales-heavy, high-ticket home services (roofing, flooring, cabinets) are hard to hand off. Owners bring energy to lead gen and closing that a hired manager often can’t match on day one.  
If you hate selling and hate managing salespeople, this model will feel like punishment.

**Storm / insurance skill**  
Documenting damage. Talking to adjusters. Supplements. Avoiding the reputation of “storm chaser scam.”  
Systems and training matter here — franchise or home-built.

**Subcontractor (or crew) network**  
Many modern systems run **sales in-house, production 1099**.  
You win or lose on vetted crews: show-up, quality, insurance, capacity after a storm when *everyone* wants the same guys.

**Working capital**  
Insurance jobs can leave you financing materials and labor while you wait on depreciation / supplements.  
Cash retail shortens the cycle. Pure insurance shops burn owners who undercapitalize.

**Tech / estimating**  
Drones, photo docs, CRM, production scheduling — table stakes for looking like a real company vs a flyer on a doorknob.

**Warranty & reputation**  
Your brand lives on the roof for 10–50 years depending on the product story. One bad tear-off neighborhood can torch Google forever.

**Owner role (honest version)**  
Early stage: lead flow, sales leadership, production coordination, cash.  
Not “I got a franchise and went fishing.”

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

Ticket intuition:

- Residential re-roofs: often **mid four figures to mid five** by market / size / material  
- Brian’s commercial buyer example: **~$73k–$80k** quotes on a **~5,000 sq ft** NJ roof (two bids) — useful for “this is real money,” not for your P&L forecast  
- Franchise investment bands vary widely by system (Notion franchisor notes for one roofing brand sat roughly **~$215k–$320k** all-in range on that card — always verify current FDD Item 7)

Unit economics to diligence (ask these, don’t memorize averages):

- What’s the **insurance vs cash vs commercial** mix last 24 months?  
- Days sales outstanding on insurance jobs?  
- Close rate by lead source (canvass vs Google vs referral)?  
- Sales commission structure?  
- % of production subcontracted vs W-2?  
- Callback / warranty cost as % of revenue?  
- Local marketing spend and CAC by channel?  
- If franchised: royalty + brand fund + required local spend as % of gross  

Industry chatter often puts franchise royalty stacks in the mid-single to high-single digits of gross (system-specific). Independents trade that for the burden of building their own sales/ops machine.

What that means in plain English:

The *category* can scale fast because tickets are large.  
A *specific office* can go broke on bad mix, bad crews, or underfunded insurance AR — while posting “record sales.”

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

**1. It’s a sales business wearing a hard hat**  
If you can’t build a sales culture, the trucks don’t matter.

**2. Storm feast / famine**  
Hail year = chaos. Quiet year = you find out whether you have a retail muscle.

**3. Insurance complexity and slow pay**  
Scope fights, supplements, depreciation holdbacks, carrier rule changes. Working capital is a silent killer.

**4. Reputation risk / storm-chaser stigma**  
Door knock + insurance = easy to look slimy. Trust is the product.

**5. Subcontractor capacity when everyone is busy**  
After a regional storm, good crews are booked. You sold jobs you can’t install on time.

**6. Quality / callbacks / warranty**  
One leak after a “sold” job becomes a five-figure headache and a one-star review.

**7. Commission sales culture**  
100% commission canvassers and closers: high upside, high drama, high turnover, compliance risk if scripts go rogue.

**8. Competition**  
Low differentiation. Price wars on retail. Canvass wars after storms. Franchise logos everywhere.

**9. Owner dependency**  
Brian’s point: hard to make this truly semi-absentee early. Sales energy doesn’t clone easily.

**10. Safety / liability**  
Heights. Jobsite injuries. Subcontractor insurance gaps. Your entity still owns the customer relationship.

**11. Materials price and supply swings**  
Shingle / underlayment / metal costs move. Bids go stale.

**12. Weather windows**  
You can’t install in ice / storms. Backlog piles up. Customers get angry on a schedule you don’t fully control.

**13. Franchise stack (if you go that route)**  
Royalty + rules + territory limits vs playbook, brand, and insurance systems. Worth it only if the system actually wins jobs and collections — not because the logo looks cool on a truck.

**14. Confusing revenue with profit**  
Big top line after a storm is not the same as cash in the bank after commissions, materials, callbacks, and slow insurance checks.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means roofing is a **real high-ticket sales + production business**, not a weather lottery ticket.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re wired for sales leadership and pipeline discipline  
- You’re okay managing commission egos and 1099 production relationships  
- You can hold a standard on quality while moving volume  
- You’re capitalized for insurance AR (or intentional about cash-heavy mix)  
- High-ticket home services excite you more than recurring hourly routes  

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want passive / semi-absentee from month one  
- You hate selling and hate hiring sellers  
- You’re looking for predictable weekly route revenue (think pest, lawn, senior care hours)  
- You’re undercapitalized for a slow insurance cycle  
- You want to *be* the roofer on the ladder as the whole company (craft job ≠ this page’s model)

Franchise vs independent is a separate call.  
Same category either way: sales, production, cash, reputation.

---

## Risks (the short list)

- Sold more than you can produce  
- Insurance AR strangling payroll / materials  
- Storm dependency in a quiet climate  
- One viral bad job  
- Sales team compliance blowup  
- Crew insurance / injury gap  
- Royalty drag without lead/system advantage  
- Thinking “essential demand” substitutes for operator skill

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “people need roofs.”

You’re buying the right to run a **high-ticket sales machine** that turns weather, housing stock, and trust into installed jobs — if you can close, produce, and collect.

Some people should build retail-first and use storm as a booster.  
Some people should lean insurance and capitalize for it.  
Some people should walk — and put the same capital into a vehicle with cleaner recurring economics.

The question isn’t “do roofs fail?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a stressful commission culture that only works when the sky cooperates?**
