Leaves in the skimmer.  
Water that went cloudy after the weekend party.  
A pump that hummed fine in May and died in July heat.

How long do you wait — and who shows up on a **schedule**, not a one-off panic scoop?

There’s a lot of things you can put off.  
A pool that turns green (or fails a commercial water-test) is not one of them.

That’s **pool service**.

Not a neighbor kid with a net and a Facebook post.  
Not pure **pool construction / remodel** as the whole company (project cousin — different P&L).  
Not a retail chemical warehouse pretending to be a route book.

A local **route + chemistry + equipment** company that sells recurring cleans, keeps water balanced, catches failing equipment before it becomes a lawsuit, and lives or dies on **whether last year’s customers still want your tech at the gate this year**.

Simple on a whiteboard: pools need weekly care → routes fill → LTV compounds (plus pumps and filters when they don’t).  
Brutal if you confuse “people love pools” with “this runs itself.”

---

## What the business actually is

You’re a **recurring-route field service** company wearing a pool brand — and the category secretly contains **maintenance cash flow** plus **equipment / repair / specialty upside** that share the same truck.

Typical menu:

1. **Recurring residential maintenance** — weekly / bi-weekly clean, brush, vacuum, filter check, chemical balance (the spine of most books)  
2. **Hot tub / spa maintenance** — same rhythm, often attached to the pool stop or sold as its own small route  
3. **Commercial / multi-family / hospitality** — hotels, apartments, gyms, YMCAs, HOA community pools; higher frequency (sometimes **daily** water testing); paperwork and liability jump  
4. **Openings / closings** — seasonal big-ticket transitions (especially northern climates)  
5. **Specialty / recovery** — green-to-clean, filter cleans, acid washes, full drains, algae wars after storms or owner neglect  
6. **Equipment repair & replacement** — pumps, motors, filters, heaters, salt systems, automation, leak hunts (higher margin *when staffed*)  
7. **Light remodel / renovation attach** (when you run it) — tile, resurfacing referrals, equipment packages — lumpier than the route

Typical loop:

1. **Lead** — Google / LSA, neighborhood density, realtor / builder / PM referrals, truck wraps, prior-year renewals, commercial RFP / property-manager relationships  
2. **Sell the plan** — not just “scoop today”; frequency + what’s included + chemical policy + after-hours rules  
3. **Route the tech** — density beats hero drives; locked gates and “dog loose” wreck utilization  
4. **Service + document** — readings, chemicals used, equipment notes, photos, next visit  
5. **Collect / retain** — card on file or monthly billing, cancel-save, review ask  
6. **Upsell equipment / specialty without becoming a free consultant at every stop** — the repair ticket is where many books actually make money

**Scope lock for this page:** branded (or independent) **pool / spa cleaning & maintenance** built on **route density**, chemical discipline, technician utilization, and equipment-repair attach. Openings/closings and commercial are part of many real books — call them out in diligence; don’t pretend every greenfield starts as a construction yard.

**Out of scope (different models):**  
- Pure **new pool construction / major remodel** as the identity of the company  
- Pure **pool retail store / showroom** without a service book (adjacency — some franchise hybrids force retail later)  
- Solo “I own a truck and a test kit” as the whole *company* story  
- Big-box DIY chemical retail alone

Brian’s published frame on the category:

- Recurring revenue is the holy grail outside software — **pool cleaning** sits in that bucket with pest, cleaning, landscaping, filters, etc.  
- YT teaching (Puddle Pools segment): residential *and* commercial sign recurring; **commercial is the bigger opportunity** — hotels, sports clubs, YMCAs, gyms; some need weekly or **daily** work  
- Northern climates: commercial accounts can keep you busy through winter when residential books go quiet  
- Start with **one truck + one tech**; no expensive real estate required to begin; owner sells accounts → then add techs and vans  
- Stickiness anecdote: he’d used the **same pool company for ~six years** after moving in — reliable + good job = sticky book  
- Menu he highlights: weekly/bi-weekly maintenance (pool / hot tub / spa), openings/closings, specialty (filter clean, full drain, green-to-clean)  
- Newsletter framing: homeowner low-ticket consistency + commercial contracts that boost margins; year-round *as a strategy*, not a climate guarantee  
- Named systems exist (Puddle Pools, ASP / America’s Swimming Pool Company, Poolwerx, regional independents, PE-curious route consolidators). Treat them as **proof the category scales and franchises** — not as the point of this page

Mobile-service staging notes apply the same ladder as landscaping / pest / plumbing / HVAC: 1–2 trucks you’re the dispatcher-closer → 3–5 with lead tech + office → multi-crew → multi-market GMs.

## How money comes in

You sell **completed service visits on a book of recurring pool accounts**, plus equipment and specialty work that rides the relationship — not “hours on a timesheet” the customer sees, and not a one-time scoop that never returns.

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Recurring book size** — active accounts × frequency × average plan price  
2. **Retention / cancel rate** — high LTV *if* they stay; CAC is a tax you pay again every spring  
3. **Route density** — stops per tech per day vs windshield; gate codes and “not home” friction  
4. **Mix** — residential weekly vs commercial vs openings/closings vs specialty vs equipment repair  
5. **Chemical COGS discipline** — over-chem and margin dies; under-chem and callbacks / green pools destroy the brand  
6. **Equipment / repair attach rate** — pumps and filters are often where the *company* margin lives  
7. **Price discipline** — racing the uninsured truck to the bottom fills the day and empties the margin

Gross margin lives in **price − tech labor (burdened) − chemicals − parts (− warranty callbacks)**.  
Then overhead: trucks, insurance (GL + auto + workers’ comp; pollution/chemical where required), licensing where your state cares, marketing, routing / FSM software, chemical storage, and (if franchised) royalty + brand/MAP + tech fees.

Revenue mix usually looks like some blend of:

| Channel | What it feels like |
|--------|---------------------|
| **Residential recurring maintenance** | The spine. Predictable if retained. Density or die. Easy-entry noise is loudest here. |
| **Commercial / hospitality / multi-family** | Higher frequency, bigger tickets, audits and liability. Winter hedge in cold markets *when you can win and staff it*. |
| **Openings / closings** | Seasonal cash spike. Labor scramble. Don’t spend it like permanent income. |
| **Specialty / green-to-clean** | Marketing hook + recovery margin — or expensive hero work that wrecks the route day. |
| **Equipment repair / replacement** | Higher margin when diagnosed and produced. Skill and parts inventory required. |
| **Light remodel / construction attach** | Exciting invoices. Different CAPEX, estimating, and warranty risk — don’t confuse it with the route. |

Industry shape (education only): residential monthly plans often discussed in the **roughly ~$100–$200+/month** band depending on market, pool size, and visit frequency (weekly vs bi-weekly); commercial contracts climb hard with frequency and compliance. Healthy operators watch **pools per tech per day**, **retention %**, **chemical % of revenue**, and **repair attach** as hard as “leads.” Route books trade as assets in this category for a reason — durable cash flow *when density and retention work*, not a guarantee your unit prints.

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Pools already exist. New builds still happen in Sun Belt metros. Hotels and apartments still fail health inspections when chemistry slips. Homeowners still want the weekend party without becoming a chemist.

When the economy is good, people open more outdoor living and buy equipment upgrades (heaters, automation, nicer finishes).  
When the economy is tight, a share of residential customers cut frequency or DIY chemicals — **attrition is the risk** — but commercial and “my pool turned green and company is coming Saturday” demand doesn’t vanish.

That’s why Brian groups pool routes with other “boring / essential / recurring” home services: the need doesn’t disappear; **your book and your techs can**.

The constraint isn’t “pools need water care.”

It’s **route density + chemical discipline + tech supply + seasonality honesty** in *your* metro.

Easy entry (truck + test kit + Craigslist) keeps competition loud at the bottom. Brand, insurance, documentation, equipment competence, and retention systems are how a *company* separates from a guy with a net.

## Micro: what actually drives one company

**Route density & scheduling**  
Three sparse zip codes and a locked-gate list is a different business than a tight cluster of weekly stops. Routing software helps. Geography and book quality decide. Same mobile staging truth as pest and lawn: you don’t skip from one truck to a fleet without dispatch and a lead tech that isn’t you.

**Retention / cancel-save**  
High LTV is conditional. Missed visits, rude techs, cloudy water after you left, and spring price shocks without a save offer destroy the asset you paid CAC to build. Brian’s stickiness story only works if you’re **reliable**. The company is the **renewing book**.

**Chemicals & water-quality competence**  
Wrong dose, wrong product, ignored CYA, salt-system neglect — callbacks, plaster damage claims, and “you ruined my pool” reviews. This isn’t optional paperwork. Amateurs undercutting you often skip it until someone gets a rash or a lawsuit.

**Technician recruiting, training & retention**  
People who can balance water *and* talk a homeowner through a dying pump without creating free callbacks. Physical work, heat, chemical exposure, early starts, and a labor market that will poach anyone who can run a route without destroying equipment. Demand without people is a ringing phone and green-pool reviews.

**Equipment upsells / repair engine**  
Maintenance gets you in the gate. Pumps, motors, filters, and heaters often pay the bills. Operators who only scoop and never build a repair bench leave money (and customer trust) on the pad. Operators who oversell parts they can’t install create warranty hell.

**Seasonality (climate is a co-owner)**  
Sun Belt can lean year-round. Northern books slam in spring (openings), run hard in summer, then face a winter hole unless commercial / indoor / spa / service contracts hedge it. Brian’s commercial teaching is exactly that hedge story — not a promise every metro has daily hotel pools waiting.

**Owner role (honest version)**  
Early stage: selling accounts, riding routes, hiring, chemical QA, cash, weather triage, and often backup tech.  
Not “I bought a logo and went fishing.”  
Same ownership muscle elsewhere: **hire the people who service; run the density and the mix**.

**Systems**  
Routing / CRM, card-on-file or monthly billing, chemical logs, equipment notes, review engine, cancel-save scripts, parts inventory. Guesswork doesn’t scale past one heroic tech.

**Operating intuition (industry education, not forecasts):**

- Tech throughput chatter often lives around **~8–12 residential stops/day** when density works — windshield and commercial frequency change the math  
- Residential plan pricing commonly discussed ~**$100–$200+/month** depending on market and frequency; weekly vs bi-weekly is a strategic choice, not a vibe  
- Chemical COGS is a dial: operator composites often treat chemicals as a meaningful single-digit-to-teens % of revenue when controlled — over-chem and “free extras” quietly murder GP  
- Equipment repair attach is frequently where healthy books separate from busy scoopers  
- Route valuation chatter (broker / industry): books sometimes discussed as multiples of **monthly recurring revenue** — useful for diligence framing, not a price you’ll get  
- Seasonality discount: northern / short-season operators should not copy Sun Belt annualized math without a winter plan

Diligence questions that matter more than memorizing averages:

- What’s **active recurring accounts**, average plan price / frequency, and **trailing retention / cancel rate**?  
- Stops per tech per day? Windshield %? Gate / access failure rate?  
- Mix: residential vs commercial vs openings/closings vs specialty vs equipment repair?  
- Chemical COGS % and green-pool / callback rate?  
- Repair attach rate and average ticket? Who diagnoses — tech or separate repair bench?  
- Commercial concentration and insurance / audit posture?  
- Off-season cash plan if northern — commercial hedge, indoor/spa, openings reserves, or owner draws that ignore November?  
- License / chemical handling posture for *this* state  
- Sales CAC by channel vs lifetime gross margin of a retained account  
- If franchised: royalty + MAP + required local spend + software as % of gross; territory pool density you can actually route; retail-store requirements (if any)  
- Owner hours on the pad vs on recruiting/retention — is this a transferable company?  
- If buying a book: verified recurring list, post-close attrition, equipment/truck condition, and what’s *recurring* vs one-time fluff  

What that means in plain English:

The *category* has durable demand and can compound when retention, density, and chemical/repair competence work.  
A *specific book* can look slammed every July and still be broke — cheap prices, empty winter, chemical callbacks, or an owner who never got off the scoop.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

**1. Route density / windshield death**  
Low-ticket weekly stops without geography discipline means you work hard and net nothing. Sparse territories and locked gates quietly murder utilization. Density isn’t a nice-to-have — it’s the business.

**2. Seasonality is a co-owner (especially outside the Sun Belt)**  
Spring openings are a riot. Summer is heat and call volume. Winter can be a hole. Owners who treat July like permanent income meet November as a cash crisis — unless commercial, spa, indoor, or reserves were the plan *before* Labor Day.

**3. Chemical / water-quality landmines**  
Wrong chemistry isn’t a “learning moment.” It’s callbacks, surface damage claims, health complaints, and brand death. Documentation and training beat “I eyeballed the chlorine.”

**4. Technician scarcity (competent, not just willing)**  
Demand without people who can balance water *and* represent the brand is a ringing phone and green-pool reviews. Training pipelines are slow; poaching is expensive; summer heat attrition is real.

**5. Easy-entry noise at the bottom**  
Anyone with a truck, a net, and a Facebook ad can *look* like a competitor by Monday. You carry insurance, chemical discipline, callbacks, and equipment competence. Racing them on price is how professionals go broke.

**6. Confusing scooping with a company**  
A route that only cleans and never builds repair capability leaves higher-margin work to the specialty shop — or to whoever the homeowner Googles when the pump dies. A company that oversells repairs it can’t produce creates warranty hell.

**7. Equipment upsells without parts / skill / warranty process**  
Pumps and heaters print when diagnosed right. They also create truck-stock chaos, manufacturer warranty fights, and “you sold me a lemon” reviews when process is weak.

**8. Retention / spring cancel culture**  
High LTV is a *result*, not a birthright. Missed visits, cloudy water, rude techs, and price shocks without a save offer turn “recurring” into a revolving door of expensive CAC every March.

**9. Commercial bid pressure, audits & liability**  
Hotels and multi-family can steady the calendar — and train you to race to the bottom while drowning in water-test logs, night windows, and insurance requirements. Winning commercial is not the same as *staffing* commercial.

**10. Openings / closings chaos**  
Big seasonal tickets feel great until every customer wants the same two weeks. Labor scramble, overtime, and quality slips in the rush create the reviews that haunt summer.

**11. Weather & algae whiplash**  
Heat waves, storms, pollen, and “we had a party and dumped juice in the pool” spike callbacks together. Your Google reviews don’t care that NOAA ruined chemistry week.

**12. Working capital on a “simple” recurring story**  
Payroll, chemicals, trucks, insurance, and spring marketing burn while the book fills. Undercapitalized launches stall when hiring and peak-season CAC hit the same month.

**13. Owner still on the pad (or still the only closer)**  
If the company only works when you’re the tech, the dispatcher, and the cancel-save hero, you own a job with a wrap — Brian’s staging ladder applies here too.

**14. Sales engine that outruns service**  
Paid search and door hangers can fill a book fast. If tech capacity and chemistry QA can’t keep up, you bought churn with a royalty (or a truck payment) attached.

**15. Franchise stack (if you go that route)**  
Royalty + MAP + required spend + rules + territory vs playbook, brand, routing, and peer network. Worth it only if the system fills routes and trains people — not because the logo looks good on a tank. Retail-hybrid requirements (store within X months) are a different capital story than pure mobile.

**16. Buying a tired book without attrition math**  
Customer lists look like assets until post-close cancels reveal you bought a phone book. Verify recurring, density, and tech tenure — not “we’ve cleaned 10,000 pools historically.”

**17. Semi-absentee fantasy**  
Manager salary on a thin early book (plus seasonal holes) is how shops bleed. Semi-absentee usually needs a mature multi-truck machine — not a greenfield hope or one hot summer.

**18. Confusing “pools always need care” with “easy money”**  
Timeless demand is real. Your P&L still needs operators, density, chemistry, and seasonality discipline.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means pool service is a **real route + chemical + equipment business** where **high LTV only shows up when customers stay** — and seasonality plus easy-entry amateurs will punish anyone who thought a net was a strategy.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re wired to hire, schedule, and lead field techs (Brian’s hire-the-tech lens) — and respect chemistry as a real skill  
- Recurring-route puzzles (density, retention, cancel-save) excite you more than one-off construction heroics  
- You’re okay competing on reliability and professionalism in a loud, easy-entry market  
- Equipment repair attach (or partnering it cleanly) sounds like part of the job — because it is  
- Truck-by-truck scale (and maybe commercial or specialty later) sounds exciting once one route is dialed  
- If northern / short-season: you can stomach (and plan for) off-season cash holes — or honestly pursue commercial hedges

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want passive / semi-absentee from month one  
- You refuse to recruit or manage technicians  
- You’re allergic to chemicals, water testing, heat, and “the pool went green over the weekend” realities  
- You need weekly *construction* project spikes and hate small-ticket route rhythm  
- You’re undercapitalized for trucks, insurance, payroll ramp, spring CAC, chemical inventory, and winter reserves  
- You want pure pool *construction* economics — different page / different risk  
- You’re looking for a craft job with a logo (you *being* the only tech forever)

Franchise vs independent is a separate call.  
Same category either way: sell the plan → dense route → chemistry to standard → retain → repair when equipment fails → compound the book.

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “people love swimming.”

You’re buying the right to run a **route + chemistry + equipment machine** that turns pool friction into a renewing book — if you can recruit technicians, pack dense stops, stay competent with water and gear, survive the seasons, and keep last year’s customers from shopping the cheaper truck every spring.

Some people should start greenfield with one or two trucks and prove density before adding leases.  
Some people should buy a tired local route book (or a franchise resale) and install retention + repair systems — consolidators chase recurring outdoor service for a reason.  
Some people should layer commercial / spa / openings once residential density is real — Brian’s teaching loves the commercial winter hedge *when it’s earned*.  
Some people should walk — and put the same capital into a vehicle with cleaner indoor economics, less weather surface area, or a different project profile.

The question isn’t “do pools need maintenance?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a stressful outdoor company that only works when you’re the scoop, the chemist, the dispatcher, and the cancel-save department?**
