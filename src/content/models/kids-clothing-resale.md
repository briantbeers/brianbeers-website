Kids outgrow clothes faster than parents outgrow patience.

Onesies last months.  
Sneakers last a season.  
Car seats and strollers sit in the garage until someone finally decides they’re done.

That’s the kids clothing **resale** model.

Not a charity drop bin.  
Not a Facebook Marketplace free-for-all in a parking lot.

A retail store that **buys** gently used kids’ apparel, shoes, toys, and baby gear from parents walking in…  
and **sells** it to other parents walking in.

Same door. Two jobs.

Same engine as adult used-clothing resale — different SKU lifecycle, different buyer urgency, different pain when you buy wrong.

---

## What the business actually is

You’re running a curated secondhand kids store.

Racks of name-brand kid clothes that still look new.  
Bins of shoes sorted by size.  
Strollers, high chairs, pack-and-plays, books, toys — the bulky stuff that makes parents swear they’ll “just sell it online” and never do.

Sellers bring bags (and car trunks) of what their kid just outgrew.  
You pay cash or store credit for what you want.  
You hang it. Tag it. Turn it.

The loop:

**Buy inventory from parents → price it → sell it → buy more.**

Two common flavors exist in the wild:

1. **Buy-outright (take title)** — cash leaves when you buy; cash returns when it sells. Bad buys are *your* problem. Stronger systems lean this way so the rack is owned inventory, not a consignment hostage situation.
2. **Classic consignment** — you hang someone else’s stuff and split when it sells. Lower cash out the door up front. Slower turns, messier seller drama, and your “eye” (or your software) still has to protect the floor.

Brian’s framing on the adult sister model is blunt: this category works best when it’s **not** classic consignment — you take title, you own the turn.

Sister-concept angle (optional scale, not day-one requirement): some operators run **adult** used clothing next door to **kids** under related brands — same family brings teen hoodies to one door and toddler snowsuits to the other. Lifetime closet overflow. One household, two racks.

You’re not inventing kids’ fashion.  
You’re moving outgrown product faster than a garage sale and cleaner than a thrift free-for-all.

---

## How money comes in

Gross margin lives in the **spread**.

You buy a winter coat for $8.  
You sell it for $24.

Multiply by thousands of SKUs — and by how fast size 4T clears before spring.

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Buy right by size + season** — a perfect brand in the wrong size month is dead fabric  
2. **Price for speed** — parents shop urgency, not “maybe later” fashion browsing  
3. **Markdown with discipline** — slow movers become trash (or Facebook leftovers) if you wait  
4. **Two-sided traffic** — sellers *and* buyers. Empty racks kill sell-through. Full racks with no shoppers kill cash  
5. **Mix** — basics and gear often turn cleaner than trendy kid fashion; fashion still pulls traffic

In disclosed kids buy-sell systems, **gross margin** (sales minus COGS, *before* rent/labor/royalty) often lands in the mid-to-high 60s percent — meaning COGS roughly low-to-mid 30s. That’s the spread, not your take-home.

Then the real P&L swings hit:

- **Labor** at the buy counter and on the floor  
- **Rent** for a box big enough for strollers and racks  
- **Shrink** (theft, damage, “adjustments”)  
- **Marketing** to keep both sides of the door busy  
- **Royalty / ads** if you’re in a franchise stack

You’re not a parenting influencer.  
You’re a **turns** company wearing a kids brand.

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Kids keep growing. That’s the whole macro.

Every year a new class of parents needs size 2T.  
Every year another class dumps size 2T.

Good times → parents buy new → closets and garages overflow → they need to sell.  
Tight times → parents hunt value → they need to buy used.

Supply and demand both show up. Just for different reasons.

Add the adult contrast lightly:

Adult used clothing rides fashion cycles, thrift-as-trend, and “I want that brand cheaper.”  
Kids resale rides **biology** — outgrow is mandatory. Fashion still matters; growth is the metronome.

Gas is high. Childcare is high. Brand-new car seats are expensive either way.

That’s the engine. Not vibes. Not one TikTok toy craze (those help a week — they don’t build the store).

## Micro: what actually drives one store

A single location lives or dies on a few boring things:

**Trade area / kid density**  
Enough families with kids in the age band you stock. Suburbs with strollers beat a downtown fashion strip that only looks good on Instagram.

**Location / lease**  
Strip or lifestyle center with parking. Room for gear. Rent you can survive when January is slow and August is chaos.  
(Retail box math — same quiet killer as adult resale.)

**Buy desk skill (or system)**  
Mom-and-pop kids consignment often lives on the owner’s “eye.” Fragile. One buyer leaves, quality tanks.

Stronger versions use **software / playbooks** to tell staff:

- which brands and categories turn in *this* market  
- what to pay by condition and demand  
- what to price for speed  
- when to markdown  
- what inventory is actually worth right now

Taste still helps.  
Guesswork shouldn’t be the whole company.

**Size + season merchandising**  
Adult thrift can hide a wrong buy in “vintage.”  
Kids thrift cannot hide a wall of size 6 coats in May.

**Labor at the buy counter**  
Peak hours = parents with bins wanting evaluations *now*. Labor scales with activity. Schedule wrong and you either lose sellers or bleed payroll.

**Inventory as working capital**  
Opening inventory isn’t decoration — it’s product you’ll sell. Underbuy and the floor looks dead. Overbuy and cash is trapped in snowsuits nobody wants in April.

**Owner involvement**  
Same lesson as the adult live deal breakdown: remote / distracted ownership shows up in the numbers. Same model. Different execution. Different outcome.

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

Franchise brands in this category that publish an Item 19 will show you **ranges** — sales and gross profit, not a guarantee of what hits your bank account after rent, labor, debt, and paying yourself.

Shape from one large kids buy-sell system’s recent disclosure (education only — always pull the current FDD for any brand you’re actually considering):

- **Average unit:** roughly ~$1.3M gross sales, with average **gross** profit in the mid-$800ks (~**67%** gross margin before operating expenses)  
- **Top quartile:** closer to ~$2.0M sales (and much higher gross profit dollars)  
- **Bottom quartile:** can sit near ~$700k average sales — still a real store, thinner room for rent/labor mistakes  
- **Royalty:** in that family, often around **~5%** of gross sales (plus local marketing commitments — read Item 6)  
- **New build investment band:** mid-six figures all-in — think roughly **~$350k–$490k** in a recent Item 7, driven hard by fixtures, POS, buildout, opening inventory (~$75k–$85k), and working capital  
- **Mature vs new:** older stores in the pack often show higher average sales than brand-new cohorts — ramp is real

What that means in plain English:

The *model* can work.  
A *specific store* can be a dog or a rocket.

Your job in diligence isn’t to memorize the average.  
It’s to ask: **where does this unit sit in the pack, and can I move it?**

Same spreadsheet brain you’d use on adult used-clothing resale:

- location / kid density / parking  
- system quality (buy tools, training, brand awareness)  
- growth potential from *this* baseline  
- multi-unit proof (do operators already stack 2–3–4?)  
- existing vs new build math  
- is it worth *your* cash vs a different vehicle entirely

## Biggest challenges

Say these out loud before you fall in love with the cute racks.

**Seasonality by size**  
Winter coats, swimwear, back-to-school, holiday sets — demand spikes and dies on a calendar *and* a growth chart. Wrong size-season mix freezes cash.

**Sourcing density**  
You need a steady river of parents bringing product. Thin trade area = empty buy desk = empty floor three weeks later. You can’t invent sellers with a pretty Instagram.

**Fashion vs basics**  
Character tees and trendy brands pull traffic. Basics, denim, outerwear, and **gear** often pay the rent. Over-index on cute and you get a museum. Over-index on boring and traffic softens. Balance is the job.

**Mall / strip rent**  
You need square footage for strollers and racks. Rent that looks fine at peak season looks brutal in the soft months. Lease terms are not a footnote.

**Labor at the buy counter**  
Evaluations are the product. Slow buys = angry sellers who go to Marketplace instead. Fast, sloppy buys = trash on hangers. Training + staffing here is the heartbeat.

**Theft / shrink**  
Open retail, small items, busy weekends. Shrink sits inside COGS in serious disclosures for a reason. Cameras and process aren’t optional theater.

**Competition from Facebook Marketplace (and friends)**  
Parents can list a stroller at 10 p.m. Your edge is **convenience, curation, cash today, and a store that doesn’t flake**. If you’re slower and colder than Marketplace, you lose the easy sellers — and eventually the buyers.

**Adult contrast, one line:** adult fashion resale fights style cycles and thrift competition; kids resale fights **size calendars, gear bulk, and free online listings** for the same car seat.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means kids resale is a **real retail business**, not a cute side hustle.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You like retail energy (people, product, pace) and don’t mind kids-adjacent chaos  
- You’re willing to live in inventory turns, size walls, and labor schedules  
- You’re fine owning a local brand that wins on execution, not a “set it and forget it” app  
- Multi-unit or adult+kids sister pairing sounds exciting, not exhausting

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want pure passive income  
- You hate managing people / part-time turnover  
- You’re allergic to leases, theft risk, cash handling, and buy-counter conflict  
- You need a blank-slate creative project (this is a playbook business, not an art project)  
- You think “parents love bargains” replaces trade-area math

Also: estate-sale / household liquidation operators sometimes touch kids gear in one-off clearouts — different model. Resale retail is **ongoing turns**, not a three-day house sale.

---

## Risks (say them out loud)

- **Wrong buy** → racks of stuff in the wrong size/season  
- **Slow turns** → cash frozen in inventory  
- **Labor creep** → volume up, profit flat  
- **Lease / buildout** → the quiet killer of “affordable” retail deals  
- **Distracted ownership** → same brand, bottom-quartile results  
- **Marketplace leakage** → sellers skip you; buyers price-check you to death  
- **Franchise stack** (if you go that route) → royalties + rules; you trade freedom for a system — make sure the system is worth it  
- **Disclosure footnotes** → “gross profit” is not what hits your personal bank account after you pay rent, staff, the bank, and yourself

None of that means walk away by default.  
It means diligence is the product.

## The ownership point

You’re not buying onesies.

You’re buying a machine that converts **outgrown closets** into cash flow — if you run the buy desk, the turns, and the labor.

Some people should start one.  
Some people should buy a tired one and fix the mix.  
Some people should pair it with adult used-clothing resale and catch a family’s whole lifecycle.  
Some people should walk — and put the down payment into a different vehicle entirely.

The question isn’t “are kids cute?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or just a busier version of a job with more glitter glue?**
