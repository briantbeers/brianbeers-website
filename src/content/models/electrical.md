Power’s out and the fridge is warming up.  
Half the house is dark and the breaker’s hot to the touch.  
You’re adding an EV charger, a generator, or a panel that hasn’t been touched since the Carter administration.

How long do you wait — and who do you trust with live voltage?

There’s a lot of things you can put off.  
Electricity is not one of them.

That’s residential **electrical service**.

Not a wholesale supply house.  
Not a new-construction rough-in GC as the whole company.  
Not “I own a meter and a van” solo craftsman as the ownership model.

A local company that **answers the phone**, **dispatches licensed technicians**, and **turns house calls into completed jobs** — service repairs, code-driven upgrades, and larger tickets like panels, generators, and EV setups.

Brian’s frame is the same one he uses for auto repair and the other trades:

Your goal isn’t to pull wire yourself.  
It’s to **hire the electricians** who are the experts — then run marketing, process, people, and compliance.

He owns auto shops without fixing cars.  
Same ownership muscle. Different trade. Same scarcity fight — and a thicker permit/code layer than most homeowners ever see.

Simple on a whiteboard.  
Brutal if you confuse “electricity is essential” with “this runs itself.”

## What the business actually is

You’re a **dispatch + technician utilization + code-compliant ticket** company wearing an electrical brand.

Brian’s service menu from the essential-franchises teaching (and what residential shops actually book):

1. **Emergency / can’t-wait** — no power, tripped / failing breakers, burnt outlets, “something smells like melting plastic,” storm / outage aftermath  
2. **Service & small installs** — outlets, switches, ceiling fans, lighting, smoke/CO alarms, circuit adds, troubleshooting  
3. **Upgrades / larger tickets** — **main panels and subpanels**, surge protection, generators, **EV charger setups**, smart-home wiring, whole-home rewires adjacent to service

Loop on a real day:

1. **Call / lead** — Google, referral, prior customer, realtor/GC relationship, sometimes a call center  
2. **Book & dispatch** — right licensed tech, right truck stock, right geography  
3. **Diagnose & sell** — flat-rate book or estimate; options; when the job becomes a **permit + inspection** path, say it before the work starts  
4. **Complete (or schedule the upgrade)** — same-trip repair if the truck has the part; second trip / permit window if it’s a panel, service upgrade, or generator  
5. **Collect / review / next job** — cash, card, financing on bigger tickets; ask for surge, generator, or membership-style preventive work before you leave

**Scope lock for this page:** residential (and light commercial) **service electrical** — W-2 or employee techs on branded trucks, office/dispatch, inventory discipline, license/permit posture that survives an inspection.

**Out of scope (different models):**  
- Pure new-construction rough-in / builder electrical  
- Pure **solar / battery / EV as a specialty EPC** (adjacent — many service shops install Level-2 chargers; full solar is a different CapEx, sales cycle, and utility fight)  
- Low-voltage-only (alarms, AV, data) as the whole company  
- Solo handyman who “does some outlets” without license/ops (handyman pages live *below* the license line — see adjacency)

**Handyman adjacency (don’t confuse the two):**  
Handyman brands often touch *light* electrical *within* local rules (GFCI swap, basic fixture). Panel work, service upgrades, and most circuit work belong to a **licensed electrical contractor**. Crossing that line is how shops meet boards, insurers, and lawsuits. Ambitious owners sometimes stack brands (Brian’s guest teaching: painting / roofing / **electrical** under one warehouse) — that’s scale math, not day-one scope.

Named systems exist (Mr. Electric, Mister Sparky, peer logos and strong local independents). Treat them as proof the category franchises — not as the point of this page. Brian names Mr. Sparky and Mr. Electric as *examples* in the 9-franchises segment; his public idea list puts Electric Services next to plumbing and HVAC the same way.

## How money comes in

You sell **completed service calls and upgrade tickets**, not “hours on a timesheet” in the abstract.

Two revenue shapes live in the same truck — and owners who don’t separate them get surprised:

| Mix | What it feels like |
|-----|---------------------|
| **Service / diagnose / small install** | Higher volume, faster cash, lower average ticket. Utilization and first-trip completion decide the week. |
| **Panel / service upgrade / generator / EV** | Fewer jobs, **much** bigger tickets, **permit + inspection + scheduling** drag. Working capital and license posture matter more than Google ads alone. |

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Booked demand** — phone answered, lead converted, schedule filled  
2. **Technician utilization** — billable hours vs windshield, supply-house runs, waiting on inspection, callbacks  
3. **Average ticket / mix** — outlet swap vs panel upgrade vs generator — mix decides whether a “busy” week pays  
4. **First-trip completion on service** — truck stock that finishes without a second visit  
5. **Close rate in the home** — options presented, flat-rate book followed, upgrade path offered when the panel/load calc says so  
6. **Permit / inspection cycle time on upgrades** — sold work sitting in “waiting on AHJ” is sold revenue that isn’t cash yet  
7. **Tech count you can actually staff** — empty trucks don’t bill; complex work without a journeyman / master available doesn’t either

Gross margin lives in **price − materials − tech labor (burdened) − commissions/spiffs − permit/inspection fees (when you eat them)**.  
Then overhead: dispatch/office, vehicles, insurance (GL + commercial auto + workers comp — electrical claims are not theoretical), marketing, software (FSM), warehouse/flex space, bond/license costs, and (if franchised) royalty + brand fund.

Industry shape (education only, not Brian claims): many residential shops live or die on **billable hours per tech per day** and **revenue per truck** — *and* on whether **panel/upgrade tickets** are filling the margin hole that pure “$150 diagnose” work can’t. Parts runs, bad routing, and permit delays quietly steal the month.

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Housing stock ages. Panels fill up. Circuits fail. DIY electrical is how house fires start — which is exactly why most people won’t touch it.

Brian’s line in the essential-franchises teaching:

Electrical work is **beyond most people’s DIY skills**.  
It can be **very dangerous** if you don’t know what you’re doing.  
As the owner, **you shouldn’t be doing the repairs either** — that’s the tech’s job.

When the economy is good, people add EV chargers, generators, smart lighting, and service upgrades for renovations.  
When the economy is bad, breakers still trip, outlets still fail, and a large share of calls are **urgent / nondiscretionary**. Brian puts electrical next to plumbing, HVAC, roofing, and auto on the essential / recession-resilient list for that reason.

That’s the “essential” pitch.

The constraint isn’t “people need electricians” in the abstract.

It’s **licensed technicians + permit/code discipline + dispatch + truck stock + trust** in *your* AHJ (authority having jurisdiction).

Trade schools and Boomer retirements don’t refill the journeyman bench as fast as demand shows up — EV load, aging panels, and insurance/underwriter pressure on outdated services only add fuel. Tech scarcity is structural pain *and* the reason a well-run shop stays valuable.

Code cycles and local enforcement create different operating realities by city.  
Same category. Different inspection calendar.

## Micro: what actually drives one company

**Dispatch / call handling**  
Speed is the product on no-power and “something’s sparking” calls. Missed calls, slow booking, and sloppy routing = lost revenue you already paid to generate. Many systems lean on call centers early so owners aren’t the only person answering after dark.

**Technician recruiting & retention**  
Same bottleneck as plumbing, HVAC, and auto: licensed people who show up, sell ethically, pull permits correctly, and don’t create callbacks or failed inspections. Your employer brand matters as much as your Google reviews. Brian’s multi-trade teaching: hire the expert — don’t cosplay as one.

**Permit / code / AHJ reality (say it before you buy)**  
This is the layer that separates electrical from “just another van trade.” Service calls often don’t need a permit. **Panel changes, service upgrades, new circuits, generators, and many EV installs do.** Wrong assumptions about who pulls the permit, what the inspector wants, and how long the window takes will trash utilization and customer trust. State licensing + local amendments + responsible-in-charge rules are not marketing copy.

**Panel upgrades vs service calls (the mix that decides your P&L)**  
Service work fills the calendar and trains the truck. Panel / service upgrades, generators, and EV chargers fill the **margin**. Shops that only chase $200–$400 tickets stay busy and thin. Shops that only chase panels starve between big jobs and need working capital for materials and inspection lag. The operator’s job is a deliberate mix — not hoping Google sends you whatever.

**Truck stock**  
A residential electrical service truck is a rolling inventory room — breakers, devices, wire, connectors, common panel parts, testing gear. Understock → second trips → utilization dies. Overstock → cash frozen in copper and breakers. Generator / EV / panel jobs add distributor lead times and “the equipment is on a truck somewhere” drama.

**Utilization & routing**  
Drive time between calls is unpaid theater. Dense book + smart sequencing beats “more vans” until the schedule is actually full — then you hit the license/tech ceiling again. Inspection wait days are utilization killers if you didn’t schedule around them.

**In-home selling & flat-rate discipline**  
Diagnostic fees, options, and “your panel is maxed / unsafe / obsolete” conversations. Push upgrades too hard and you look like a shark. Never offer the upgrade when load calc and condition say it’s rational and you leave money (and safety) on the table. Trust is the product.

**Referral / GC / realtor / property-manager relationships**  
Brian’s teaching: build through **referral relationships, networking, and marketing**. Franchise systems often add training and **access to master electricians for complex problems**, plus playbooks for scaling residential → light commercial. Independents own that network themselves.

**Owner role (honest version)**  
Early stage: recruiting, marketing/lead flow, pricing discipline, culture, cash, and **license/permit posture**.  
Not “I bought a logo and went fishing.”  
Same as Brian’s auto story: you run the *business*; techs run the *trade*. Stacking brands later (electrical + another home-service under one back office) is a scale option — not a substitute for dialing the first truck.

**Systems**  
Flat-rate book, FSM/dispatch software, QA, permit tracking, review engine, financing partners on bigger tickets. Guesswork doesn’t scale past one heroic journeyman.

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

**Startup / investment shape (education):**

- Brian’s broad home-services franchise band often lands roughly **~$100k–$200k** in related teaching (painting, roofing, plumbing, flooring, restoration named together) — *category* conversation. Electrical service franchises often sit in a **similar-to-wider** neighborhood once trucks, tools, licensing, insurance, launch marketing, and working capital are real.  
- Public aggregator reads of 2025/2026 FDDs for brands Brian names as category examples: **Mr. Electric** roughly **~$160k–$357k** Item 7; **Mister Sparky** new-build roughly **~$133k–$277k** Item 7 (conversion bands often lower). Always verify current FDD.  
- Royalties in that peer set often land around **~6% of gross** plus brand/MAP / tech fees — system-specific.  
- Independents trade the royalty for the burden of building brand, playbooks, supplier programs, and recruiting help themselves — CapEx for trucks/tools/licensing is still real.

**Operating intuition (industry education, not forecasts):**

- Routine residential **service tickets** are often discussed from the **low hundreds into the mid-hundreds**; strong selling techs push higher with options, surge, and small circuit work. **Panel / service upgrades, generators, and EV charger installs** commonly jump into the **low-to-mid four figures and up** depending on amperage, trench/conduit, AHJ requirements, and market.  
- Trade ops writing often frames **revenue per tech** in roughly the **mid-six figures** annually for solid performers — utilization, ticket mix (service vs upgrade), and whether you can *hire licensed people* decide if that’s fantasy or baseline.  
- Healthy shops watch **service vs upgrade mix**, **first-trip completion**, **permit cycle time**, and **failed-inspection / callback rate**.  
- Billable-hours intuition tracks other residential trades: roughly **~5.5–6.0** billable hours in an 8-hour day is often treated as a healthy band in cost-of-doing-business chatter — below ~5.0 usually means routing, stock, callbacks, or “waiting on the inspector.”

Diligence questions that matter more than memorizing averages:

- What’s billable utilization by tech last 12 months?  
- Service vs panel/upgrade/generator/EV mix? Gross margin by mix?  
- First-trip completion rate? Parts-run frequency? Callback rate?  
- Average ticket and close rate in-home (service vs upgrade)?  
- Permit pull rate and average days from sold upgrade → inspected complete? Failed inspection rate?  
- Tech vacancy / time-to-hire for *licensed* electricians? Apprentice pipeline? Who holds master / responsible-in-charge?  
- Call answer rate / after-hours coverage model?  
- Inventory turns on trucks vs warehouse? Distributor terms on panels/generators?  
- If franchised: royalty + MAP + required local spend + software fees as % of gross  
- License / bond / insurance posture for *this* state and the AHJs you’ll actually work in

What that means in plain English:

The *category* has nondiscretionary demand and can scale truck-by-truck.  
A *specific shop* can be slammed on service calls and broke on margin — or quiet all year because it can’t hire a licensed tech or survive inspections.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

**1. Technician scarcity (licensed, not “handy”)**  
Demand without journeymen is just a ringing phone you can’t legally fill. Apprentices help the pipeline; they don’t replace the license on day one.

**2. Permit / code / AHJ friction**  
Sold upgrades that sit in inspection limbo trash cash and reviews. Local amendments catch out-of-town owners who assumed “electrical is electrical.”

**3. Panel-upgrade feast vs service-call famine (or the reverse)**  
Live only on panic service calls and margins stay thin. Live only on panels and your calendar (and working capital) get chaotic. Mix is an operating system, not a vibe.

**4. Utilization death by windshield (and supply-house / inspection wait)**  
Bad routing, thin density, parts runs, and “can’t finish until Tuesday’s inspection” steal billable hours you already paid for.

**5. Truck stock vs cash vs lead times**  
Empty bins = second trips. Overfilled bins = working capital stuck in breakers and devices. Sold generators/panels waiting on equipment = angry customers.

**6. Call handling / after-hours**  
If a human doesn’t catch the no-power / sparking call, the competitor with a call center does — and they get the panel job later.

**7. License, bond, insurance, and responsible-charge rules**  
State boards and carriers don’t care that your marketing is clever. Wrong responsible-in-charge assumptions get existential.

**8. Quality / callbacks / failed inspections / reviews**  
One botched panel or a callback that becomes a scorch mark on drywall is a one-star neighborhood story *and* an insurance event.

**9. Price pressure vs flat-rate discipline**  
“I’ll beat any quote” without a book is how shops get busy and thin. Bid wars on panels are worse when someone skips the permit.

**10. DIY / unlicensed competition + trust**  
Handymen, marketplace apps, and “my cousin” undercut on small tickets. Reputation, license proof, and speed beat slogans — until someone buys your Google clicks out from under you.

**11. EV / generator / smart-home temptation without ops math**  
Chasing every shiny adjacent ticket without truck stock, training, and AHJ fluency turns into expensive callbacks — or a second business you didn’t staff.

**12. Owner still on the tools (or still the only closer / permit puller)**  
If the company only works when you’re the hero electrician *and* the office, you own a job with vans.

**13. Working capital on upgrades**  
Materials, payroll, and inspection lag can pinch even when “sales” look great.

**14. Franchise stack (if you go that route)**  
Royalty + rules + territory vs playbook, brand, suppliers, master-electrician access, and recruiting help. Worth it only if the system actually fills trucks and trains people — not because the logo looks good on a wrap. Brian’s pitch in the category: training + master access + residential-to-commercial scaling methods — verify that in diligence, don’t assume the brochure.

**15. Confusing “essential demand” with “easy money”**  
Power fails either way. Your P&L still needs operators, licensed techs, and a permit process that doesn’t implode.

**16. Stacking brands before the first truck is dialed**  
Warehouse synergies are real (Brian’s guest teaching). Premature multi-brand is how you own three thin P&Ls instead of one solid dispatch machine.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means residential electrical is a **real service + code-compliance business** where labor utilization, license posture, and panel-vs-service mix *are* the product.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re wired to hire, schedule, and lead tradespeople (Brian’s hire-the-tech lens)  
- You’re okay with phones, dispatch drama, Google reviews, and **inspectors** as a scoreboard  
- You like operational puzzles (routing, stock, flat-rate, permit tracking, ticket mix) more than living in panels yourself  
- Essential, nondiscretionary demand matters more to you than fad categories  
- You’ll deliberately balance service volume with upgrade tickets — not just chase whichever call is loudest  
- Multi-truck / multi-territory (or brand-stack later) sounds exciting once the first truck is dialed

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want passive income from month one  
- You refuse to recruit or manage licensed technicians  
- You’re actually looking for a craft job with a logo (you *being* the only electrician)  
- You’re undercapitalized for trucks, inventory, upgrade materials, and a slow ramp  
- You hate compliance, permits, insurance, and “the board / AHJ says…” realities  
- You want pure solar EPC or low-voltage-only economics — adjacent, not this page’s core model  
- You think handyman-scope electrical *is* this business

Franchise vs independent is a separate call.  
Same category either way: demand → dispatch → licensed tech → truck → service/upgrade → permit when required → collect.

## Risks (the short list)

- Can’t hire / keep licensed techs → capacity ceiling  
- Permit / inspection failures → cash lag, callbacks, reputation damage  
- Low utilization → high ad spend, thin profit  
- Service-only mix → busy and broke; upgrade-only mix → feast/famine  
- Inventory / equipment lead-time mistakes → cash or callbacks  
- License / bond / insurance miss → existential  
- Owner dependency on the tools, the phone, or every permit  
- Royalty drag without lead/recruiting/master-access advantage  
- Thinking “people always need electricians” substitutes for operator skill

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “houses need power.”

You’re buying the right to run a **dispatch machine** that turns urgent electrical failures (and planned upgrades) into booked, inspected, completed jobs — if you can recruit licensed technicians, stock trucks, answer the phone, hold a code standard, and balance service volume with panel-sized tickets.

Some people should start greenfield with one or two trucks and a ruthless service + upgrade mix.  
Some people should buy a tired local shop and install systems (rollup math exists in this trade like other essential services).  
Some people should walk — and put the same capital into a vehicle with cleaner recurring route economics or a different trade fit.

The question isn’t “is electrical essential?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a stressful van company that only works when you’re still the backup tech and every permit routes through your phone?**

If you want help comparing doors (this model vs plumbing vs HVAC vs handyman vs others), that’s a conversation.  
Not a pitch deck.
