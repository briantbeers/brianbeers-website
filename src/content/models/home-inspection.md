Contingency clock is ticking.  
Buyer’s agent wants “your guy” on the calendar tomorrow morning.  
Seller’s agent is already nervous about what the report will say.  
You’re crawling a crawlspace with a flashlight, a moisture meter, and an E&O policy that only works if you wrote what you saw — and didn’t write what you guessed.

That’s **home inspection**.

Not “buy houses and be a landlord.”  
Not fixing the HVAC, plumbing, or panel you just flagged (those are **trades** — different companies, different licenses).  
Not property management (sticky fees on doors you operate — different page).  
Not a brokerage listing specialty (commission economics — see **senior real estate** when the aging seller is the product).

You’re a **local report company** whose product is a time-boxed condition assessment for a **housing transaction** — sold mostly through **agent trust**, delivered as a digital report, defended by process and insurance when someone later claims you “missed it.”

Simple on a whiteboard: more closes in the market → more inspections → more fees → hire inspectors → scale.  
Hard in real life — because **agent concentration**, **E&O**, **housing-cycle volume**, **scheduling density**, **specialty add-ons**, and **license / standards** all show up before the “be your own boss with a ladder” brochure does.

---

## What the business actually is

You’re a **residential home inspector** — independent, association-credentialed (ASHI / InterNACHI–style category), or branded — paid to show up between contract and close, walk the accessible systems, document defects and limitations, and deliver a report buyers (and their agents) can act on inside the contingency window.

Typical job: a buyer under contract needs eyes on roof, structure, exterior, electrical, plumbing, HVAC, insulation, interiors, and whatever your state / SOP allows — in a few hours on site plus report time. Agents decide who gets recommended. Your calendar fills or dies on that recommendation loop.

Brian’s materials put this row on the **250 Franchise Ideas** sheet as **Home Inspection Services**, with **Pillar To Post**, **AmeriSpec**, and **HouseMaster** as category-proof names — not as “this page is a brand pitch.” Separately, his low-ticket spectrum teaching has named **home inspection** in the roughly **~$50k–$100k** “buy yourself a job” band — useful as *investment-shape orientation*, not as a promise that you’ll stay small or get rich. Independents with **ASHI**-style standards sit in the same category umbrella without a logo.

Typical menu (mix varies by market / company):

1. **General home inspection** — the core product; SOP-driven; photo-heavy digital report  
2. **Specialty / ancillary attach** — radon, sewer scope, mold sampling, WDI/termite (where licensed), thermal imaging, pool/spa, water quality, drone roof views, new-construction phase inspections  
3. **Pre-listing / seller inspections** — less common than buyer-side in many markets; different agent politics  
4. **Investment / rental / commercial-light** — some firms expand; different SOP, insurance, and sales motion  
5. **Re-inspections / walk-throughs** — repair verification after negotiation  
6. **Not the same company:** performing the repairs you recommended (conflict / license landmines), owning the house, or managing the property after close

Typical loop:

1. **Win the agent (or buyer-direct)** — lunch, open houses, CE lunch-and-learns, Google/reviews, franchise/brand lead flow, prior buyers  
2. **Book inside the contingency** — same-week or next-day availability is the product feature agents actually feel  
3. **Inspect** — systems walk, photos, limitations stated out loud when you can’t access something  
4. **Attach** — radon device, sewer camera, etc., when the buyer opts in and you’re equipped / certified  
5. **Report same day / next morning** — clear language; neither alarmist theater nor “everything’s fine” soft-pedal  
6. **Post-call / agent care** — answer buyer questions without becoming their free general contractor; keep the referring agent looking competent  
7. **Grow past solo** — second inspector, QA, shared agent book, calendar software — or stay a well-paid job with a ladder

**Scope lock:** this is an **ops / services model** — transaction-tied inspections, agent marketing, report quality, insurance.

It is **not** landlord / buy-and-hold investing (different capital and who owns the asset — Brian’s ownership frame keeps **business vs real estate vs investing** as separate doors).  
It is **not** HVAC, plumbing, or electrical *repair* as the P&L (you may *refer* into those trades; you don’t usually *become* them on the same job).  
It is **not** property management (doors + trust accounting).  
It is **not** “set it and forget it passive income with a franchise logo.”

Named proof points exist: national inspection brands and strong local independents with association credentials. Treat logos and letters as **category proof** — not as the point of this page, and not as a substitute for a transferable agent book, clean claims history, and report systems that survive diligence.

Independents, franchisees, and multi-inspector firms can all sit in this category. Same umbrella. Wildly different P&Ls and liability maturity.

## How money comes in

You sell **inspection capacity and trusted reports** — not the underlying real-estate commission, and not the repair work your findings create for other companies.

Pricing / economics shapes you’ll see:

- **Base general inspection fee** — often educated in roughly **~$350–$650+** bands depending on market, square footage, age, and travel (national quote averages in industry software chatter have hovered near the mid-**$400s**; always verify *your* metro)  
- **Ancillary / specialty add-ons** — radon, sewer scope, mold, WDI, thermal, pool/spa, water tests commonly educated in roughly **~$75–$350+** per attach; mature shops treat attach rate as a second P&L lever  
- **New-construction / phase inspections** — package pricing; different sales channel (builders / buyer-agents on new builds)  
- **Re-inspection fees** — smaller; protect calendar abuse  
- **What you generally do *not* own:** the listing commission, the repair invoice, appreciation — those are other doors in the housing stack

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Closed (or under-contract) transactions that choose you** — volume tracks the housing market and *your* agent share of it  
2. **Inspections per inspector per week** — time on site + report writing is the capacity unit; two to four general inspections in a peak day is common teaching, not a guarantee  
3. **Average ticket = base + attach** — shops that only sell the cheapest base fee stay busy and thin  
4. **Agent concentration** — a handful of producing agents can fill a solo calendar — and empty it overnight  
5. **Show rate / weather / access** — no-shows, locked attic hatches, and ice storms eat utilization  
6. **Staff leverage** — multi-inspector only works if recruiting, QA, and agent relationships transfer beyond the founder’s phone

Gross fee *looks* simple on a napkin.  
Then unpaid agent lunches, software subscriptions, E&O premiums, equipment, drive time, same-day report nights, claim defense deductibles, and the week the MLS goes quiet show up.

This is a **trust + standards + calendar** business.  
Not rental NOI. Not a listing split. Not a repair van (though you’ll live in a truck some weeks).

**Light contrast (same housing stack, different check):**

| Model | Who pays you | What you “sell” | Overhead feel |
|-------|--------------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Property management** | Owner (fees on doors you operate) | Leasing + ops + trust + vendors | Sticky fees; after-hours; trust accounting |
| **Senior real estate specialty** | Commission on closed deals (via brokerage) | List / buy / close + transition stack | License splits; sparse high-touch files |
| **Residential plumbing / HVAC / electrical** | Homeowner (repair / replace) | Diagnose + fix / install | Tech scarcity; trucks; permits on upgrades |
| **Home inspection** | Buyer / sometimes deal party | Condition report for a transaction | Agent marketing; solo → multi-inspector; E&O; cycle with closes |

Brian’s ownership teaching keeps **operating a business** separate from **investing in real estate**. This page is the former: you get paid when someone else’s deal needs a report — not when the asset appreciates.

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

America still buys and sells houses — and most serious buyers still want (or lenders / contingency culture still expect) a third-party look before they wire earnest money into forever.

Every under-contract residential deal is a potential inspection. Agents still gatekeep a large share of who gets called. Housing stock ages. New construction still needs phase eyes in some markets. Specialty attach (radon maps, sewer laterals, pools) keeps expanding the ticket without inventing a new industry.

The constraint isn’t “do houses exist?”

It’s **enough trusted agent relationships + enough licensed capacity on the calendar + enough insurance and standards hygiene to survive a miss** — without building a book that disappears when rates spike and closings freeze.

National brands exist. ASHI / InterNACHI–credentialed independents exist. Software exists.  
Same category umbrella. Different operating reality.

## Micro: what actually drives one company

**Agent book (the real CRM)**  
You’re not primarily scaling “impressions.” You’re scaling *who will text you when their buyer goes under contract*. One productive agent can feel like a territory. Ten can fill a solo. Lose three and the month breaks. Treat agent relationships like a route book — because functionally they are.

**Transaction-tied volume**  
Your demand curve follows under-contract activity, not “homeowner need” in the HVAC sense. Rate shocks, inventory droughts, and seasonal listing patterns show up in your inbox before they show up in a national headline you can do anything about.

**Scheduling density**  
Drive time between jobs is unpaid. Clustering inspections geographically, protecting buffer for report writing, and holding same-week availability for top agents is the utilization game. Oversell the calendar and quality (and E&O risk) collapses. Undersell it and agents find someone faster.

**Report quality + turnaround**  
Agents rebook the inspector who delivers a clear, timely report that helps the buyer decide — without torching every deal with unforced alarmism or soft-pedaling real defects. Tone, photos, and limitation language are the product.

**E&O as operating system**  
Missed defects, access limitations you didn’t document, and “you said it was fine” memories are how claims start. Standards of practice, photo proof, written limitations, and knowing when to recommend a specialist are not paperwork theater — they’re how the company survives year ten.

**Specialty add-ons**  
Radon, sewer scope, mold, WDI, thermal, pool/spa — attach lifts average ticket and can improve effective hourly rate when equipment is already on site. They also add certifications, device inventory, lab relationships, and new ways to get sued if you play outside competence.

**License / association / CE**  
States vary hard: some regulate heavily (hours, supervised inspections, exams, mandated E&O); some barely. Association credentials (ASHI-style category) and CE still matter to agents and insurers even where the state is quiet. “My buddy taught me on weekends” is how amateurs meet boards and plaintiff’s counsel.

**Solo → multi-inspector wall**  
Solo is a job with upside and a ladder. Scale means hiring inspectors who won’t trash your agent book, QA’ing their reports, sharing (or segmenting) relationships without civil war, and accepting lower margin % for higher absolute dollars — or staying intentionally small on purpose.

**Owner role (honest version)**  
Early stage: you *are* marketing, inspecting, writing, and answering the 9 p.m. buyer freak-out.  
Semi-absentee fairy tales are how people buy a territory, a logo, and a calendar of resentment when housing slows.  
Scale means other licensed humans, shared standards, and a book that transfers — not “houses always sell so I’ll fish.”

**Adjacent stack**  
Plumbing, HVAC, electrical, roofing, pest, restoration — you don’t need to own them. You do need named, ethical referrals (no illegal kickback theater) and the humility to say “this needs a specialist” in the report.

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

**Operating intuition (education — not forecasts):**

- Money event = **completed paid inspections** (base + attach), not “agents who said they’d send you work”  
- Public education bands often put general inspection fees around **~$350–$650+** (market / size / age dependent); industry quote averages have sat near the mid-**$400s** in recent software snapshots — verify locally; don’t memorize a blog  
- Agent referrals are commonly educated as roughly **~60–80%** of volume for many shops — which is why concentration risk is structural, not a vibe  
- Ancillary attach (radon / sewer / etc.) is often taught as the lever that lifts ticket **~30–60%** when offered consistently — equipment, certs, and ethics required  
- Capacity intuition you’ll hear: peak days of roughly **~2–4** generals per inspector before report quality and safety degrade — **none of that is your guarantee**  
- Brian’s corpus is **strong** on category naming (250-ideas Home Inspection Services row; low-ticket “buy yourself a job” adjacency) and **thin** on a dedicated HouseMaster / Pillar To Post / AmeriSpec Item 19 / inspector-level earnings table — do **not** invent one from franchise marketing PDFs

**Startup / investment shape (education — verify current quotes / state requirements):**

- Training / exam / license where required, E&O + general liability, tools/meters/ladder/PPE, report software, website/reviews, working capital for slow agent-ramp months  
- Franchise / brand paths (HouseMaster / Pillar To Post / AmeriSpec–style category) add fee + system + sometimes territory math — still not a substitute for local agent trust and report competence  
- Indie ASHI / InterNACHI–style path: lower logo cost, higher DIY on marketing and standards discipline  
- Buying an existing **inspection book** is a different check (diligence on agent concentration, claims history, whether relationships follow the buyer, equipment, and whether the founder *was* the brand)  
- You’re often **home-based / truck-based** early — not a retail buildout. You *are* buying the ability to show up on time with a defensible report when the contingency clock is loud  
- Orientation only: Brian’s spectrum teaching has parked inspection near the **~$50k–$100k** “job-with-a-ladder” conversation — scale past solo is a separate decision, not included in that band

Your diligence questions should sound like:

- Inspections trailing 24 months — volume by month vs local closed-sales / under-contract activity?  
- Average ticket (base only vs base + attach)? Attach rate by specialty?  
- Agent concentration — top 5 agents / brokerages as % of jobs?  
- What happens when a top agent leaves, retires, or switches preferred inspectors?  
- Report turnaround SLA and rewrite / complaint rate?  
- E&O carrier, limits, deductible, claims history, open matters?  
- License / CE status for every inspector; SOP followed; association credentials current?  
- Scheduling density — average drive time; no-show rate; weather cancellation policy?  
- Specialty equipment owned vs subcontracted; certs for radon / WDI / etc.?  
- If multi-inspector: QA process, pay model (W-2 vs 1099 realities), who “owns” which agents?  
- If buying a book: will agents re-book *you*; non-solicit / transition plan; software data exportable?

Same brain you’d use buying any trust + referral book: concentration, claims, capacity, and whether *you* want to live inside other people’s contingencies for years.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

This section is the point. Soft marketing skips it. Operators live it.

**1. Agent concentration**  
A short list of producing agents can be 50%+ of a solo calendar. Feels efficient until one switches to a brother-in-law, a cheaper inspector, or leaves the business. Diversify deliberately or admit you’re running a key-person (key-agent) risk with a flashlight.

**2. E&O / “you missed it” liability**  
The job is finding defects *and* documenting limitations. Attics you couldn’t safely enter, roofs you wouldn’t walk, systems you aren’t licensed to dig into — if it isn’t photographed and written, a jury may hear a different story. One claim can erase years of fees even when you “win.”

**3. Cycle with housing transactions**  
When rates spike, inventory freezes, or closings fall, your phone gets quiet *before* your fixed costs do. This is not HVAC “no heat in January” demand. Budget for feast/famine or you’ll hate the model in year two of a slow market.

**4. Scheduling density**  
Same-week availability wins agents. Geographic sprawl, report backlog, and “I’ll squeeze one more in” destroy quality and family life. Density is a routing problem *and* a sales promise problem — overpromise once and the agent remembers.

**5. Specialty add-ons (margin and competence)**  
Radon, sewer scope, mold, thermal, pool, WDI — attach is how tickets grow. It’s also how you buy new certifications, lab drama, equipment failure, and claims outside your general SOP. Selling attach you can’t defend is worse than leaving money on the table.

**6. License / standards / CE variance**  
Cross a state line and the rules change. Unlicensed markets still have association, insurance, and agent expectations. “Everyone does it this way” is not a defense. Franchise playbooks help; they don’t replace local counsel and your SOP.

**7. Report tone politics**  
Too soft → buyer blindsided after close → claim and agent anger. Too harsh / theatrical → deals die and listing agents blacklist you. Clarity with evidence is the product. People-pleasing is how companies die slowly.

**8. Conflict of interest / repair kickbacks**  
Referring work is normal. Getting paid under the table to steer repairs is how you meet regulators and lose every serious agent. Stay boring: disclose, don’t perform repairs on your own findings without a lawyer-shaped plan, don’t play contractor.

**9. Recruiting / QA for multi-inspector**  
Your second hire can double capacity or torch the agent book in a month. Pay models, ride-alongs, report review, and who gets which agent relationships are culture fights wearing a scheduling app.

**10. Price races and “cheap inspector” Google ads**  
Someone always undercuts by $75 and markets fear. Agents who only shop price train buyers to treat you like a commodity — then punish you when commodity thoroughness arrives. Competing only on fee is how you fund your competitor’s E&O deductible.

**11. Access / safety / weather**  
Crawlspaces, roofs, electrical panels, dogs, hoarding, ice — injuries and incomplete inspections are real. Safety policies that cancel a job anger someone every time; no policy injures you.

**12. Software / process debt**  
Scheduling + agreements + payments + report templates + photo storage. Chaos scales into missed clauses and lost images. Migrations mid-book are painful when agents expect same-day PDFs.

**13. Claims defense time (even frivolous)**  
E&O isn’t only a premium line — it’s calendar theft. Document now or pay later in depositions.

**14. Semi-absentee myth**  
“Hire inspectors and watch fees compound.” Early reality is you on roofs and at agent breakfasts. Earn leverage with standards and leaders later — don’t buy the myth with a territory map.

**15. Confusing “real estate investing” with “this company”**  
Landlord / syndication / BRRRR is a different door in Brian’s ownership frame. This page is a **services practice** that reports on other people’s transactions for fees. CapEx, leverage, and who owns the outcome are not the same conversation — even if both say “houses.”

**16. Thinking “everyone needs an inspection” fills your calendar**  
Macro transaction volume is real. Your company is agents who trust you, jobs you can staff safely, reports that hold up, attach you can defend, and a cost structure that survives a slow closing season.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means home inspection is a **real trust + standards + calendar business**, not a passive ladder with a logo.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re willing to live inside standards, limitations language, and E&O as sacred — not as fine print  
- You can sell / nurture agents **and** produce careful field work (or honestly partner the half you hate)  
- You’re calm under contingency pressure and still willing to say “I can’t safely access that”  
- You like checklists, photos, and documentation more than Instagram house-flip flexing  
- You’re okay with transaction-tied volume — not only “always-on” emergency trades  
- You want a real-estate-adjacent vehicle **without** buying the underlying property CapEx stack yourself  
- You’re willing to fire bad agent relationships when they demand soft reports or illegal kickbacks  
- If you scale: you’ll recruit, QA, and share credit instead of hoarding every roof yourself

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want **landlord / investing** returns and asset appreciation as the whole thesis (different door — say so)  
- You want to *fix* what you find as the core P&L (**plumbing / HVAC / electrical** / roofing — related pages) without a clear conflict plan  
- You want sticky monthly doors under management (**property management**) or listing commissions (**senior real estate**)  
- You hate sales relationships and expect Google alone to fill a contingency calendar  
- You’re looking for passive / “hire inspectors and fish” from month one  
- You’re under-reserved for a slow housing quarter and an E&O deductible  
- You’re allergic to writing, photos, and saying no to unsafe access

Franchise brand vs indie ASHI-style boutique vs buying a book is a separate call.  
Same category either way: agents, reports, E&O, cycle, density, add-ons, license — plus a contingency clock that doesn’t care about your weekend plans.

## Risks (the short list)

- Agent concentration cliffs  
- E&O claims / defense costs / uninsurable history  
- Housing-transaction volume shocks  
- Scheduling sprawl → quality and safety failure  
- Specialty add-ons outside competence  
- License / CE / SOP gaps  
- Report-tone blacklist from either side of the deal  
- Illegal referral / repair conflict blowups  
- Multi-inspector QA failure torching the brand  
- Price races that don’t fund insurance and report time  
- Buying logo theater instead of a transferable agent book + clean claims file  
- Confusing practice ownership with landlord real-estate investing

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “America sells houses” or a pastel promise that inspection fees print while you sleep.

You’re buying the right to stand between a buyer’s contingency and a house’s ugly truths — and get paid in transaction fees — if you can win agents without becoming their captive, deliver reports that hold up, price attach honestly, keep E&O boring, and survive the months when closings thin out.

Some people should launch lean, win ten agents slowly, and master general + one specialty before headcount.  
Some people should buy a tired book and fix claims hygiene + report QA + agent concentration.  
Some people should walk — and put the same capital into landlord investing (if that’s actually the thesis), property management, senior real estate, a trade (plumbing / HVAC / electrical), or a different vehicle entirely.

The question isn’t “will buyers keep wanting inspections?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a calendar full of other people’s contingencies that only pays when the agents stay, the reports defend you, the add-ons stay inside competence, and the housing cycle still funds a week you’d schedule on purpose?**

If you want help comparing doors (this practice vs property management vs senior real estate vs plumbing / HVAC / electrical vs others), that’s a conversation.  
Not a pitch deck.
