Trash that has to go out before the building opens.  
Bathrooms that get judged by every visitor.  
A facilities manager who only notices you when something is wrong.

How long do you wait — and who shows up on a **night contract**, not a daytime maid route with a card on file at a suburban house?

There’s a lot of things a business can put off.  
“The building looks dirty at 8 a.m.” is not one of them when tenants, patients, students, or shoppers walk in.

That’s **commercial janitorial**.

Not a solo cleaner with a Facebook ad and a car trunk.  
Not pure **residential recurring maid routes** (daytime homes, different trust stack, different P&L — [separate page](/models/residential-cleaning)).  
Not pure **facility-management middleman** that subcontracts everything and never employs a night crew (adjacency — see below).

A local **B2B contract + night-crew** company that wins sticky accounts, staffs buildings after hours, and lives or dies on **whether labor cost, scope creep, and quality hold on thin margins — month after month**.

Simple on a whiteboard: dirty buildings → nightly clean → monthly invoice → renew.  
Brutal if you confuse “sticky recurring revenue” with “easy money.”

---

## What the business actually is

You’re a **commercial cleaning / facility services** company wearing a janitorial brand — selling **contracts**, not bi-weekly house visits.

Typical menu (general commercial — not residential maid specialty alone):

1. **Nightly / scheduled office & facility cleaning** — the spine: trash, restrooms, floors, common areas on a recurring contract  
2. **Day porter / daytime coverage** — bathrooms, spills, lobby presence while the building is occupied  
3. **Specialty / periodic work** — floor care (strip/wax, carpet extract), window interiors, post-construction punch, disinfection events  
4. **Vertical niches** — medical/dental/surgery centers, schools, churches/religious, industrial/warehouse, dealerships, select property management (when you run them)  
5. **Multi-site / portfolio accounts** — same customer, many doors; pricing and QA get harder, stickiness can get better

Typical loop:

1. **Lead** — outbound to facility / office / property managers, RFP / bid portals, referrals, brand sales teams (franchise-fed accounts), prior customers expanding sites  
2. **Walkthrough + scope** — square footage, frequencies, restrooms, floors, security access, after-hours windows, what’s *in* vs *out* of the contract  
3. **Bid / present** — price per month (or per visit) against incumbents; top-third vs race-to-bottom is a strategy choice  
4. **Win + transition** — often ~30-day notice to the old vendor; hire/onboard for *that* account; startup meeting; keys / badges / alarm codes  
5. **Staff the nights** — crews by building (often account-specific), supervisors, call-outs, no-shows  
6. **QA + retain** — checklists / photos / inspections; fix repeating mistakes before the customer shops the bid again  
7. **Collect / renew / re-price** — monthly billing; wage pressure vs stale contracts; expansion to sister sites

**Scope lock for this page:** branded (or independent) **commercial janitorial / office & facility cleaning** built on **B2B contracts**, night (and sometimes day) crews, account-level labor math, and retention of sticky but price-sensitive customers. Light residential attach and post-construction spikes appear in some books — call them out in diligence; don’t pretend every greenfield starts as a suburban maid route.

**Out of scope (different models):**  
- Pure **residential recurring house cleaning / maid service** (daytime homes, route density, household cancel culture — **[residential cleaning](/models/residential-cleaning)**)  
- Pure **carpet / upholstery truck-mount** specialty  
- Pure **window cleaning** routes (related glass — separate page)  
- Pure **restoration** (water/fire — different channel and capital)  
- Solo “I clean offices cash after hours” as the whole *company* story  
- Pure **facility-services broker** (sell + subcontract everything; Citywide-style foot-in-door adjacency — different labor surface)

**Light contrast with residential cleaning (don’t collapse the two):**

| | **Residential cleaning** | **Commercial janitorial** |
|--|--------------------------|---------------------------|
| Customer | Households | Buildings / facility managers |
| Rhythm | Often Mon–Fri daytime | Often **nights / weekends** around occupancy |
| Sale | Schedule + trust in a home | **Contract + scope + bid** |
| Stickiness | High *if* retained; cancel culture real | Often sticky — until quality slips or a cheaper bid wins |
| Margins | Labor-heavy; can be healthier when priced | **Sticky revenue, thin margins** is the teaching frame |
| Competition noise | Uninsured solos + Facebook ads | Bid wars + national BSCs + fed-account franchises |

Brian’s published commercial teaching (Corporate Cleaning Group conversation + franchise-idea sheet):

- Commercial cleaning is a huge, often **unsophisticated** industry — operators who bring systems, QA tech, and people process can differentiate  
- Recurring contracts are the point; lead → qualify → walkthrough → present → close → **first-customer handhold** through hire/onboard  
- Why customers leave incumbents: **repeating mistakes / inconsistency**, not one-off misses — systems that prevent the same miss twice are the product  
- Labor retention is the fear that weighs on every new managing partner; culture and engagement on night crews (who often feel “on an island”) are the lever  
- Price posture in his guest teaching: compete in the **top third**, not the ridiculous low — commodity racing is how you win the building and lose the P&L  
- Named category systems exist on the franchise-idea sheet as **Office Cleaning & Janitorial** proof: **Jan-Pro, Anago, Coverall** (and peers like Vanguard / Stratus / Jani-King in the broader market). Treat them as **proof the category franchises and scales** — not as the point of this page  
- Structural fork he explicitly walked through on-air: many commercial systems use a **master franchisee** (regional mini-franchisor who sells unit licenses and often **feeds accounts**) vs a **managing-partner / single-unit** model that wants business people building a true asset with layers (supervisors / ops) — **pick the ownership job, not the brochure**  
- Adjacent YT teaching: facility-solutions concepts that use **janitorial as the foot-in-door**, then attach landscaping, snow, pest, HVAC coordination — related *customer*, different company identity

His ownership muscle still applies:  
**Cleaner** does the work on the nights. **Supervisor / manager** bridges customers and crews (QA, call-outs, account health). **Owner** builds sales + culture and puts the right people in the right seats — franchisors generally **don’t want you scrubbing toilets forever**.

## How money comes in

You sell **fulfilled contract hours / monthly service on a book of commercial accounts**, not “hours on a timesheet” the customer sees as a maid visit — and not a one-time deep clean that never returns.

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Contract book size** — active accounts × monthly contract value (or visit frequency × rate)  
2. **Account retention / bid loss rate** — sticky *if* quality holds; one lost large site punches a hole payroll can’t instantly shrink  
3. **Labor % by account** — burdened wages vs contract price; overtime, ghost hours, and scope creep quietly kill “thin margin” businesses  
4. **Crew utilization & coverage** — call-outs, no-shows, and “building empty but crew still paid” nights  
5. **Mix** — offices vs healthcare / schools / industrial vs periodic floor care / day porter  
6. **Price discipline** — winning every RFP at the lowest number fills the vans and empties the margin  
7. **Sales cycle + pipeline** — 60–90 day lead-to-close rhythms are common teaching; working capital must survive the wait  
8. **Fed accounts vs self-won** (franchise-shaped books) — guaranteed or assigned volume vs you owning the relationship and renewal

Gross margin lives in **contract price − cleaner labor (burdened) − supplies (− supervisor load)**.  
Then overhead: vehicles, insurance (GL + workers’ comp + bonding / employee dishonesty where required), phones / radios / badges, QA software, office, sales payroll or lead vendors, and (if franchised) royalty + brand/MAP + tech + (in master systems) account-assignment economics.

Revenue mix usually looks like some blend of:

| Channel | What it feels like |
|--------|---------------------|
| **Nightly office / facility contracts** | The spine. Sticky if retained. Thin if underpriced. |
| **Day porter / daytime** | Visibility + higher trust bar; different wage and scheduling. |
| **Specialty periodic (floors, extract, events)** | Margin when staffed; feast/famine if it becomes the identity. |
| **Niche verticals (healthcare, schools, churches)** | Different compliance / hours / stickiness; can buffer office-vacancy risk. |
| **Multi-site portfolios** | Scale + concentration risk; one decision-maker can add or cut many doors. |

Industry shape (education only): Brian’s commercial guest teaching often lands **gross margin roughly mid-40s to mid-50s** in the Item 19 conversations he hosts for niche commercial concepts; broader industry education for office janitorial often cites **gross in the ~30–45% band** for many SMB operators and **net in the mid-single to low-double digits** when labor is controlled — public integrated facility players run even thinner. Healthy operators watch **labor % by account**, **account retention**, **cleaner turnover**, and **overtime / unbilled hours** as hard as “new logos won.” Sticky revenue is real. **Thin margins are the tax.**

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Buildings still need trash emptied, restrooms stocked, floors maintained, and common areas presentable — whether the tenant is an office, clinic, school, warehouse, or dealership.

When the economy is good, companies buy cleaner buildings and daytime porters.  
When the economy is tight, they **bid-shop, cut frequency, or shrink footprints** — **scope and price pressure are the risks** — but the need doesn’t vanish for occupied facilities that still have to look open for business.

That’s why Brian groups commercial / facility cleaning with other “boring / essential / recurring” B2B services: the need doesn’t vanish; **your margin and your labor bench can**.

The constraint isn’t “buildings get dirty.”

It’s **reliable night crews + honest account-level pricing + QA that stops repeating mistakes + working capital through a long sales cycle** in *your* metro.

Easy entry (mops + a van + a low bid) keeps competition loud at the bottom. Systems, bonding, show-up rate, and supervisor density are how a *company* separates from a person with a vacuum and a midnight key.

Office vacancy / hybrid work is a real macro headwind for pure Class-A office books. Operators who lean **healthcare, education, industrial, religious, and other occupied niches** (as in Brian’s CCG conversation) treat that as portfolio design — not as “offices always fill.”

## Micro: what actually drives one company

**Account-level labor math**  
Company-wide “labor looks fine” can hide one underpriced building that eats the profit of three good ones. Scope creep (“can you also…”) without a change order is how sticky accounts become charity.

**Night-crew recruiting, quality & retention**  
Brian’s blunt framing across cleaning: **people are the #1 challenge**. Commercial adds nights, weekends, and “island” crews who never come to a daytime office. Culture, appreciation, and stable building assignments beat “we’ll pay a dollar more” — until a competitor does both. Industry education routinely cites **very high hourly turnover** in janitorial; operators who treat retention as optional own a revolving door with badges.

**QA / inconsistency as the churn engine**  
Customers can forgive a miss. They shop the bid when the **same miss repeats**. Checklists, photo uploads, supervisor inspections, and startup discipline are not “corporate fluff” — they’re how you keep the sticky revenue everyone marketed to you.

**Bid culture & price courage**  
RFPs and “we’re getting three quotes” are normal. Racing the uninsured or desperate incumbent fills the book and starves the P&L. Top-third positioning (Brian’s guest teaching) only works if your sales story and QA actually look different.

**Sales cycle vs payroll timing**  
Lead → walkthrough → present → close → 30-day transition → hire → first clean. Working capital has to bankroll **payroll before (and as) revenue ramps**. Undercapitalized launches stall when the first big win arrives and the checking account can’t float two weeks of wages.

**Owner role (honest version)**  
Early stage: pipeline, walkthroughs, pricing, hiring for new accounts, cash, customer saves, and often backup night / QA hero.  
Not “I bought a logo and went fishing.”  
Same ownership muscle elsewhere: **hire the people who clean; run the business that keeps accounts *and* crews**.

**Control spectrum (if franchised)**  
Two common commercial shapes:

1. **Unit + fed accounts (classic master systems)** — lower ticket unit licenses; regional master or corporate sales **assigns** volume; you may be closer to an operator of assigned work than a full relationship owner. Diligence what you *own* at renewal and what happens if assigned accounts move.  
2. **Managing-partner / build-the-asset** — larger territory math, you (or your sales engine) win and keep relationships, layer supervisors so you’re not forever in the field. Higher bar on who they recruit (“business people into janitorial,” in Brian’s CCG contrast).

**Jan-Pro / Anago / Coverall (and peers)** sit in the broader market as recognizable commercial franchise architectures — often discussed in unit-vs-master terms. Use them as **category maps for diligence**, not as endorsements. Read the current FDD: who sells, who services, who owns the customer, what “guaranteed” accounts actually mean, and what royalties / account fees do to thin margins.

**Systems**  
Timekeeping that matches site hours, account P&Ls, QA apps, badge/key control, review / complaint escalation, and re-bid calendars. Guesswork doesn’t scale past one heroic night supervisor.

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

**Brian’s published teaching points (education — not your forecast):**

- Corporate Cleaning Group podcast teaching: franchise fee anecdote ~**$54k**; all-in / operate band discussed roughly **$70k–$130k**, driven hard by **working capital to bankroll field payroll** — *their* framing, not your budget  
- Same conversation: Item 19 anecdote around roughly **~$1.5M** average sales; gross margin discussed roughly **45–55%** in that disclosure frame; corporate locations cited as multi-million proof of scale *potential* — *their* numbers, not your target  
- Sales-cycle teaching: lead-to-close often discussed in a **~60–90 day** band when things move; 30-day incumbent notice is common before first clean  
- Lead-engine teaching in that model: corporate + franchisee prospecting toward a stated lead cadence; franchisee still owns walkthrough / close / ops  
- Broader cleaning franchise teaching (residential-leaning episodes that still discuss the category): recurring revenue + semi-absentee *after* a real management team; **~40% semi-absentee** anecdotes in some systems are a signal of *possible* lifestyle *and* of buyable under-optimized books — not a greenfield promise  
- Franchise-idea sheet: **Office Cleaning & Janitorial** named with **Jan-Pro, Anago, Coverall** as category proof brands  
- Adjacent recurring YT: facility-solutions concepts that land **janitorial first**, then attach other building services

**Startup / investment shape (education — verify current FDD Item 7):**

- **Unit** commercial cleaning franchises in aggregator reads often advertise **low five-figures to mid five-figures** when accounts are packaged/assigned — diligence *what* you’re buying (job vs company)  
- **Managing-partner / larger commercial** concepts often live in a **~$70k–$150k+** all-in conversation once working capital is honest  
- **Master / territory developer** tiers (Anago / Jan-Pro / Coverall peer set in public aggregator summaries) commonly jump to **high-six / low-seven-figure** capital and net-worth conversations — a different buyer than a night-crew operator  
- Royalty + brand-fund + account / marketing fees can be material on **thin net** — read Item 6 as carefully as Item 19  
- Buying an **existing contract book** can look cheaper than greenfield — diligence retention, labor %, and customer concentration after close, not the logo on the polo

**Operating intuition (industry education, not forecasts):**

- Direct labor often lands roughly **~45–55% of revenue** for many commercial operators; well-run books fight to hold the low-40s; struggling books creep above 55%  
- Net margins for healthy SMB janitorial are frequently discussed in a roughly **~5–15%** band; a 2-point labor miss is a large share of net  
- Account retention can be strong when quality works (industry surveys often show many operators losing **under ~10%/year** of accounts) — the asset is real *until* it isn’t  
- Hourly turnover in janitorial is structurally high in industry research (often cited around **~100–200%+** annualized depending on source) — training tax and quality dips are the hidden COGS  
- Office-only books carry **occupancy / hybrid-work** risk; niche mix is a portfolio decision

Diligence questions that matter more than memorizing averages:

- What’s **active contract count**, monthly recurring revenue, and **trailing-12 account loss / retention**?  
- Labor % **by account** (not just company-wide)? Overtime %? Unbilled / ghost hours?  
- Mix: office vs healthcare / school / industrial / other? Day porter vs night?  
- Cleaner turnover, time-to-hire, supervisor-to-crew ratio, and % of revenue in the top 3 accounts?  
- Callback / complaint rate and **repeat** defect rate?  
- Sales CAC / cycle length by channel vs lifetime gross margin of a retained account?  
- Bonding / background / workers’ comp / insurance posture — what happens on a theft, injury, or property-damage claim?  
- If franchised: royalty + MAP + account fees + required spend as % of gross; who owns the customer at renewal; unit vs master economics; territory commercial density you can actually sell  
- Working capital: can you float payroll through first wins and a lost large account?  
- Owner hours on the mop vs on sales/QA/recruiting — is this a transferable company?  
- If buying a book: verified contracts, assignment/consent clauses, post-close attrition, and wage vs price gaps already baked in

What that means in plain English:

The *category* has durable facility demand and can compound when contracts stick and labor is controlled.  
A *specific book* can look full every night and still be broke — or quiet because it can’t staff the wins it already sold.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

**1. Hiring and retaining night-crew cleaners**  
Brian’s #1 across cleaning — louder at 11 p.m. Demand without reliable people is empty buildings, angry facility managers, and an owner on the vacuum. Night / weekend labor pools and “island” crews need engagement systems, not pep talks.

**2. Thin margins leave no room for sloppy labor math**  
Sticky revenue feels great until burdened labor, overtime, and supplies leave single-digit net. One underpriced account can erase three good ones.

**3. Scope creep without change orders**  
“Can you also do the boardroom / the new wing / extra restrooms?” starts as helpful and ends as free work. Contracts that don’t get re-cut when buildings change are silent margin death.

**4. Bid wars and commodity positioning**  
Everyone can mop. If your only story is price, you win the RFP and lose the P&L. Top-third pricing requires real QA and sales discipline — Brian’s guest teaching, not a slogan.

**5. Inconsistency / repeating mistakes = churn**  
Customers leave when the same trash miss or bathroom fail keeps happening. Systems that catch repeats are the retention product.

**6. Cleaner attrition compounds account risk**  
When a preferred night lead quits, quality dips before the customer’s next walkthrough. Your “sticky” account was partly a person with a badge.

**7. Long sales cycle vs working-capital reality**  
60–90 day closes and 30-day transitions are normal teaching. Payroll doesn’t wait. Undercapitalized owners choke on their first big win.

**8. Account concentration**  
One multi-site customer or one Class-A tower can be most of the book. Sticky until the RFP — or the vacancy — hits.

**9. Office vacancy / hybrid-work headwinds**  
Pure office portfolios got a structural shock. Niche mix (healthcare, education, industrial, religious, etc.) is risk management, not branding fluff.

**10. Easy-entry noise at the bottom**  
Anyone with a van and a low number can *look* like a competitor by Monday. You carry insurance, workers’ comp, bonding, training, and re-clean policies. Amateurs undercutting you are both competition *and* why facility managers are skeptical.

**11. Trust / bonding / after-hours access risk**  
One theft allegation, one unlocked door, one creepy incident — existential for a local brand. Badges, keys, alarm codes, and background checks aren’t paperwork theater.

**12. Sales engine that outruns staffing**  
Fed accounts or a hot closer can fill a book fast. If hiring and QA can’t keep up, you bought churn (and maybe a royalty) with a night shift attached.

**13. Wage pressure vs stale contract pricing**  
Labor markets move. Multi-year contracts that don’t escalate as fast as wages quietly convert sticky revenue into a nonprofit.

**14. Supervisor density / QA coverage**  
Without enough eyes on nights, you don’t have a company — you have hope and Google reviews waiting to happen.

**15. Owner still on the mop (or still the only closer / night backup)**  
If the company only works when you’re the emergency cleaner and the bid desk, you own a job with a wrap — Brian’s hire-the-tech frame applies here too.

**16. Semi-absentee fantasy on thin early books**  
Manager salary on a thin contract base is how shops bleed. Semi-absentee usually needs layered supervisors and a mature book — Brian’s “after a good management team” caveat matters.

**17. Franchise stack / fed-account ambiguity (if you go that route)**  
Royalty + MAP + account fees + “guaranteed” volume that isn’t really yours at renewal. Master vs unit vs managing-partner is a **job description** choice. Worth it only if the system fills profitable accounts and helps you keep crews — not because Jan-Pro / Anago / Coverall (or any peer) looks familiar on a brochure.

**18. Buying a tired book without account-level P&Ls**  
Customer lists look like assets until post-close re-bids and wage gaps reveal you bought revenue without margin. Verify contracts, labor %, and concentration — not “we’ve cleaned 10 million square feet historically.”

**19. Confusing “buildings always get dirty” with “easy sticky money”**  
Timeless demand is real. Your P&L still needs operators who can win the labor war, the bid war, and the QA war at the same time — on thin margins.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means commercial janitorial is a **real contract + night-labor + thin-margin business** where **sticky revenue only shows up as wealth when accounts stay, crews stay, and labor % by building stays honest** — and low differentiation means systems and price courage do the hard work brand fantasy won’t.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re wired to hire, schedule, and lead cleaning crews (Brian’s hire-the-tech lens) — including **nights** — and respect that culture beats wage-only retention  
- B2B contract puzzles (scope, bids, account P&Ls, renewals) excite you more than daytime residential route charm  
- You’re okay competing on consistency and professionalism in a loud, easy-entry, bid-heavy market  
- Matching **accounts and crews** at the same time sounds like the job — because it is  
- Thin margins don’t scare you off if the levers (labor %, QA, price discipline) feel controllable  
- Building layers (supervisors → ops) so you’re not forever the night backup sounds like the point  
- Comparing **unit / fed-account vs managing-partner vs master** structures sounds like diligence you want to do with eyes open

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want passive / semi-absentee from month one  
- You refuse to recruit or manage people who clean buildings after hours  
- You’re allergic to “soft” labor issues (call-outs, drama, trust incidents) and hoped for a pure tech/product business  
- You need high-margin project spikes and hate thin-net contract rhythm (cabinets / roofing energy ≠ this day-to-day)  
- You’re undercapitalized for payroll float, insurance/bonding, and a long sales cycle  
- You want pure **residential maid** economics and a family daytime calendar — that’s the [residential cleaning](/models/residential-cleaning) spine, not this one  
- You’re looking for a craft job with a logo (you *being* the only cleaner forever)  
- You’re chasing a master franchise ego trip without the capital, sales DNA, or stomach for unit economics underneath

Franchise vs independent is a separate call.  
Same category either way: win the contract → staff the nights → clean to standard → retain the account **and** the crew → keep labor % honest → compound the book.

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “buildings get dirty.”

You’re buying the right to run a **contract + night-crew machine** that turns facility need into a renewing B2B book — if you can win scopes without racing to the bottom, staff buildings people trust after hours, stop repeating mistakes, and keep labor % from eating the thin margin everyone warned you about.

Some people should start greenfield with one or two accounts and prove account-level math before adding leases.  
Some people should buy a tired local contract book (or a franchise resale) and install QA + recruiting + re-pricing discipline.  
Some people should compare **unit / fed-account commercial systems** (Jan-Pro / Anago / Coverall peer set as category maps) against **managing-partner build-the-asset** models — and pick the job they actually want.  
Some people should walk — and put the same capital into residential cleaning, another home service, or a vehicle with cleaner project economics or less night-labor surface area.

The question isn’t “do buildings get dirty?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a stressful janitorial company that only works when you’re the night backup, the bid desk, and the QA department on thin margins?**

If you want help comparing doors (this model vs [residential cleaning](/models/residential-cleaning) vs handyman vs pest vs others), that’s a conversation.
