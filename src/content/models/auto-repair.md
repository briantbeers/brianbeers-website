Cars break.

Not *if*. *When*.

Oil light. Soft brakes. Alignment that eats tires.  
The commute still has to happen Monday morning.

That’s why auto repair stays on the “essential” list — boom or bust.

But here’s the part most outsiders miss:

A modern shop (especially the multi-unit branded versions Brian actually runs) is often **not** “owner under a car with a wrench.”

It’s a **bay utilization + technician labor + parts margin + car count** business.

Lifts. Advisors. Inventory. Reviews.  
Leases that don’t care that a tech called out.  
Same model that can print in a hard Philly neighborhood *or* stall in a rich zip code with a tired waiting room.

Simple on a whiteboard.  
Brutal if you confuse “people need cars fixed” with “this runs itself.”

---

## What the business actually is

You’re running a retail service shop that turns broken / worn vehicles into completed repair orders.

Typical loop:

1. **Car in** — drive-by, Google, referral, tire program, prior customer, sometimes a tow  
2. **Advise / inspect** — write-up, increasingly **digital vehicle inspection** (photos/video texted to the customer)  
3. **Authorize** — labor hours + parts; options, not mystery invoices  
4. **Produce** — tech on a bay; parts staged; specialty bays (alignment) guarded carefully  
5. **Collect / review / rebook** — cash, card, financing; next service sold before they leave  
6. **Come back** — oil, brakes, tires, suspension. Trust is the recurring engine

**Scope lock for this page:** general / branded **auto repair shop** — fixed facility, multiple bays, W-2 (or equivalent) techs + a front-counter / service-advisor layer, parts inventory, local retail demand.

**Out of scope (different models):**  
- Pure **quick lube** throughput (oil-membership machine)  
- Pure **tire** warehouse / install-only  
- **Collision / body** (insurance-driven; different margins, different ops — Brian’s teaching: don’t casually mix body + retail repair as one brain)  
- Mobile-only / parking-lot mechanic as the whole company  
- Dealership service departments (OEM warranty / captive book)

Brian’s ownership frame (same one he uses for plumbing):

Your goal isn’t to fix cars.  
It’s to **hire the technicians** who are the experts — then run marketing, process, people, and standards.

He owns dozens of shops and has never fixed a car in his life.  
That’s not a flex. That’s the job description for the *operator* seat.

Named systems exist (Midas, Meineke, Christian Brothers, and independents with strong local brands). Treat them as proof the category scales with playbooks — not as the point of this page.

## How money comes in

You sell **repair orders**, not “hours on a timesheet” in the abstract.

A car comes in.  
You sell labor (door rate × billed hours) **and** parts.  
In Brian’s teaching math, that mix is often roughly **~50/50** parts and labor on a ticket — so a labor-rate increase of 10% is closer to a ~5% ticket increase if parts stay flat. Pricing psychology matters.

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Car count** — how many vehicles you process (capacity + demand + trust)  
2. **Average repair order (ARO)** — ticket size: diagnose well, recommend honestly, close the work that needs doing  
3. **Bay utilization** — empty lifts are rent with a logo; overloaded lifts create callbacks and chaos  
4. **Technician productivity** — billed hours vs clock hours; waiting on parts kills both  
5. **Parts margin discipline** — markup matrices, vendor programs, tire vs attach mix  
6. **Mix** — oil/tires/brakes/suspension (Brian’s shops lean hard on brakes–tires–suspension as a core ~60% shape) vs big-ticket engines/transmissions that soak a bay for days

Gross profit lives in **labor GP + parts GP**.  
Then the killers: payroll (techs + advisors + managers), rent/lease, shop supplies, insurance, marketing, credit-card fees, and (if franchised) royalty + brand/ad stack.

**Tire truth (Brian, shop-floor):**  
You often **don’t** make a killing on the tires themselves.  
You make money on the **other stuff** people buy where they buy tires — brakes, alignments, suspension — and on having *storage* so you can actually sell the sets. Bays without tire capacity leave money on the curb.

**Alignment bay truth:**  
A dedicated alignment bay + machine (five-figure CapEx) can be high-margin when it turns (Brian’s live example: ~$130 alignments, sometimes ~10 minutes with the right tech/tech). Tie that bay up doing brakes and you bottleneck the specialty you paid for.

You’re not a software company.  
You’re a **throughput** company wearing a wrench brand.

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Vehicles age. Miles accumulate. DIY shrinks as cars get more complicated.  
Certified techs become scarcer — which is both the pain and the moat for shops that can recruit.

When the economy is good, people still maintain (and some upgrade).  
When the economy is bad, they **must** keep the current car alive — oil, brakes, tires, “safe enough to commute.”

That’s the recession-resistant pitch Brian uses in the essential-franchises teaching.

The constraint isn’t demand in the abstract.

It’s **technicians + bays + trust + lease math** in *your* trade area.

Urban shops can crush volume when real estate fits (Brian’s preference for denser footprints when he can find them — five-bay minimum in *his* multi-unit model).  
Small towns can print if you’re the only serious brand for miles and the manager owns the market for 15 years.  
Rich zip codes do **not** automatically mean rich P&Ls — expectations, remodel CapEx, and advisor skill still decide.

Same category. Different operating reality.

## Micro: what actually drives one shop

**Bays / footprint**  
In Brian’s acquisition checklist: **size matters**. More bays → more cars at once → more capacity. His multi-unit filter often wants larger boxes (he’s called out ~**five bays as a minimum** for *their* model). Tiny two-bay “jobs with a sign” don’t copy-paste the same way.

**Tire storage + retail location**  
Major road. Traffic. Visibility. Ideally retail neighbors that create trip patterns (his running joke: fast food collocators). Neighborhood quality and “will people swing in when a light comes on?” beat vanity zip codes.

**Technicians**  
“Without high quality technicians, I would be sitting around twiddling my thumbs.”  
Shortage is structural: fewer people enter the trade; veterans retire. Culture, pay path, tools, and managers decide whether you keep the good ones. Entry-level stories in his org (e.g. hired around **$14/hr** who grow into assistant → store manager → multi-store roles) are proof of a **people system**, not a wage promise for your market.

**Service advisors / managers**  
The bridge between customer and tech. Sales, phones, inspections, expectations. Weak front counter = empty bays *or* angry reviews. Strong managers are how multi-unit exists at all.

**Parts / vendors / programs**  
COGS discipline. National programs (tires, parts) vs local. Inventory that turns vs inventory that rots on a rack.

**Process on every car**  
Digital inspections, photo proof, consistent write-ups. Brian’s turnaround stories lean hard on showing the customer the bushing split / tire wear / rotor truth — trust → authorization → ARO.

**Lease + fixed cost**  
Rent doesn’t care about your car count. Royalty (if franchised) doesn’t either. Cash timing (royalty week + payroll + sales tax + credit-card settle) can feel worse than the P&L looks — his “know cash vs profit” teaching.

**Owner role (honest version)**  
Early: hiring, standards, marketing, numbers, presence.  
At scale: systems, district managers, scorecards — still not fishing. Distracted ownership (shiny adjacent bets while core shops need focus) shows up in the numbers. Brian’s own towing experiment is the cautionary tale he tells publicly: thesis sounded synergistic; execution distracted from the diamonds already under the bays.

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

### Illustrative unit math (Brian teaching — education only)

From his public *How much do auto repair shops make?* teaching (Notion / social script), an **illustrative average location** looked roughly like:

| Lever | Illustrative shape |
|-------|--------------------|
| Monthly revenue | ~**$135k** |
| Car count × ARO | ~**335** customers × ~**$400** ticket |
| Parts / COGS | ~**25%** of revenue |
| Payroll (techs + managers) | ~**30%** |
| Franchise stack (his framing) | ~**5%** royalty + ~**5%** advertising (+ ~**3%** card fees called out separately) |
| Fixed (rent, insurance, utilities, etc.) | ~**$25k**/month |
| Rough leftover (before tax & debt) | ~**$18k**/month in *that* franchise-with-managers sketch |

Same script’s contrast (still education, not a forecast): an independent **owner-operator** skipping royalty and not paying a store manager might keep more on *that same revenue sketch* — his point wasn’t “quit your job for $32k/month,” it was **portfolio math**: he’d rather make *less per door* and own **many** doors as a business operator, not a wrench hero.

### Operator targets he teaches out loud

On the spreadsheet / systems side ([Svzt-WYnyXQ](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Svzt-WYnyXQ), [Aj3jeseLj0w](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Aj3jeseLj0w)):

- Mental model: a store should clear something like **~$125k+/month** revenue with a path toward ~**20%** leftover *in his framework* when COGS, payroll, and fixed land in band  
- Weekly smell-test: if a store does **~$40k** in a week with COGS and payroll each roughly in the mid-20s %, he can already tell it’s a strong week; **~$20k** week with both near **30%** and he knows it’s underwater  
- Labor rate example from ops teaching: shops sitting at **~$170/hr** door rate, ripping the Band-Aid toward **~$190** rather than nickel-and-diming +$5 — because labor is only half the ticket  
- Valuation intuition (More shorts): a **~$1.2M** sales shop might cash-flow around **~$150k** in a healthy line and trade near ~**3.5×** that cash flow in *his* deal-thinking (ballpark **~$500–525k** for the *business*, before lease/real-estate facts) — thin-profit or losing shops often price closer to **asset value**, not hope  
- Acquisition pattern he repeats: lots of doors via **seller financing** (illustrative downs like **~$50k** on much larger purchase prices — structure-dependent, not a template you should copy blindly)

### Portfolio / location anecdotes (shape, not your forecast)

- Multi-unit branded portfolio narrative: **~35–36+** shops, **~$50M+/yr** class revenue across the book  
- Single-shop stories on camera: **~$1.7M/yr** class location in a rough Philly footprint; a small-town shop (~**4–5k** people in the named town, larger draw radius) described as a **~$2M** class store with a 15-year manager and thin competition  
- Turnarounds: competitor location once doing ~**$50k/month** that his systems pushed toward ~**$100k/month** inside a year; other stores swinging from losing quarters to **~$60–70k** profit quarters after process/people fixes  
- Franchise royalty called out in one conversion story at about **~10%** of revenue to the brand (with new-store reductions / media credits in that system — always verify current FDD; effective cash royalty ≠ headline number)

### Industry education (category research — not Brian claims)

Useful diligence context from trade surveys / ops writing:

- Many independents cluster ARO roughly **~$250–$750** (modes vary by year/survey); top shops push higher via inspection + matrix pricing  
- Parts gross profit targets often discussed around the **~45–58%** band (Institute for Automotive Business Excellence–style **~58%** benchmark appears often in trade content)  
- Bay throughput chatter: on the order of ~**2+ cars per bay per day** as a common reference; strong operators chase higher. Annual revenue-per-bay bands in ops writing often get framed in the mid-six-figures  
- Labor door rates commonly discussed from roughly **~$120–$180+/hr** depending on metro — coastal and high-skill diagnostics sit higher

### Diligence questions that beat memorizing averages

- Car count and ARO last 24 months (by month — seasonality is real; Brian notes late summer strong in his markets)  
- Effective labor rate vs billed hours; tech efficiency  
- Parts GP % and tire vs undercar mix  
- Bay count, specialty bay constraints, tire storage  
- Tech vacancy, time-to-hire, advisor tenure  
- Lease term / escalators / assignment (cheap rent today, landlord surprise tomorrow)  
- Review velocity + complaint patterns  
- If franchised: royalty + ad + software + required local spend; conversion vs new-build Item 7  
- Real estate vs business value split (dated equipment + thin profit → you’re often buying dirt and lifts, not a cash machine)

What that means in plain English:

The *category* has nondiscretionary demand and can scale door-by-door.  
A *specific shop* can do $1M+ sales and still lose money — or look “small town” and dominate a radius.

Franchise Item 7 bands for branded general repair (education only; verify current FDD) often land roughly **mid-six-figures to ~$1M+** all-in for a new multi-bay build, with conversion ranges all over the map based on what’s already in the building.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

This section is the point. Soft marketing skips it. Operators live it.

**1. Technician scarcity**  
#1 bottleneck. Fewer entrants, retiring veterans, dealer competition for talent. No techs = empty bays = you own rent.

**2. Bay utilization (too empty *or* too jammed)**  
Empty lifts = fixed costs eating you. Overbooked lifts = rushed work, callbacks, and advisors inventing ETAs they can’t keep. Specialty bays (alignment) create hidden bottlenecks.

**3. Parts margin leakage**  
Wrong matrix, weak vendor programs, giveaways to close the sale, tires sold without attach. Busy shops go broke here quietly.

**4. Car count without ARO (or ARO without trust)**  
Racing cars through at tiny tickets. Or pushing tickets so hard you torch Google. Inspections done right are the middle path.

**5. Advisor / manager quality**  
Techs can’t save a weak counter. Multi-unit dies when every store needs the owner as the closer.

**6. Distracted ownership**  
Same brand, different outcomes. Tired waiting rooms, skipped process, shiny-object side hustles (towing, “synergies”) while core bays need focus. Brian’s public lesson: diamonds under the existing shops beat sexy adjacent bets you don’t operate well.

**7. Lease and real-estate mismatch**  
Too few bays. No tire storage. Landlord escalators. Buying a “deal” that’s really a bad box.

**8. Cash vs profit timing**  
Royalty week + payroll + tax + card settle can zero the account while the P&L still “looks fine.” Multi-unit amplifies the calendar.

**9. Franchise stack (if you go that route)**  
Headline royalties can be steep (Brian’s on-camera ~10% class example in one system). You pay for brand, marketing, playbooks, buying power — only worth it if those actually fill bays and train people. Independents keep the points and carry the brand-building burden.

**10. Quality, callbacks, liability**  
Comebacks destroy utilization and reputation. Modern cars (ADAS calibrations, electronics) raise the skill floor.

**11. Seasonality and weather**  
Battery/AC/tires spike; slow months still owe rent. Planning beats vibes.

**12. Competition + trust deficit**  
National logos, independents, dealerships, and “cheap quote” shops. Retail automotive is a trust sale with grease on it.

**13. Hiring managers who aren’t mechanics — and still can lead mechanics**  
Brian’s whole thesis. Harder than it sounds. Wrong hire → culture rot → tech exodus.

**14. Confusing revenue with profit**  
$1.2M sales with 30%+ payroll and fat COGS is a job with anxiety. Know the three numbers cold: sales, COGS, payroll.

**15. Valuation traps on acquisition**  
Paying for hope on a zero-profit book. Ignoring lease landmines. Mixing body-shop economics with retail repair. Overpaying because the seller “had an offer.”

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means auto repair is a **real multi-variable ops business**, not a recession lottery ticket.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re wired to hire, retain, and lead tradespeople (Brian’s hire-the-tech lens)  
- You like scorecards: car count, ARO, COGS %, payroll %, bay throughput  
- You’re willing to live in standards, inspections, and Google reviews as a scoreboard  
- Multi-unit / copy-paste sounds exciting — regional density, seller-finance deals, systems over heroics  
- You’re fine owning a boring compounding machine (brakes and tires) instead of a shiny startup story  

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want passive / semi-absentee from month one with no leadership bench  
- You insist on *being* the best tech as the whole company (craft job ≠ this page’s model)  
- You hate recruiting, confrontation, or holding a manager accountable  
- You’re undercapitalized for lease + payroll + parts while demand ramps  
- You need a blank-slate creative project (this is proven playbook territory)  
- You’re actually shopping **quick lube**, **collision**, or **mobile-only** — different pages, different math  

Franchise vs independent is a separate call.  
Same levers either way: bays, techs, parts, car count, trust.

## Risks (the short list)

- Can’t staff techs → capacity collapses  
- Fat payroll + soft ARO → busy and broke  
- Bad lease / too few bays → ceiling you can’t market past  
- Parts and tire margin laziness → GP death by a thousand invoices  
- Distracted or absentee ownership → same brand, bottom-quartile shop  
- Royalty/ad stack without lead or system advantage  
- One viral bad job / safety miss  
- Paying seller hope instead of cash flow (or dirt + assets)

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “cars break.”

You’re buying the right to run a **bay-and-people machine** that turns local traffic and trust into repair orders — if you can hire techs, staff the counter, guard utilization, and know your numbers cold.

Some people should buy a tired shop and install process (DVI, pricing, managers).  
Some people should build greenfield where competitors failed — if the box has bays and the lease doesn’t murder you.  
Some people should walk — and put the same capital into a vehicle with cleaner recurring economics or less technical labor risk.

The question isn’t “do people need oil changes?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a stressful job under a lift you swore you wouldn’t crawl under?**

If you want help comparing doors (this model vs others), that’s a conversation.  
Not a pitch deck.
