America is getting older.

People live longer.  
Kids live farther away.  
Nobody wants Mom in a nursing home if she can stay in *her* house.

So someone has to show up.  
Cook. Bathe. Drive to appointments. Sit so she isn’t alone at 2am.

That’s in-home **non-medical** senior care.

Not a hospital.  
Not a facility.  
Not skilled nursing (unless you expand into it later).

Caregivers go to the client’s home.  
You bill by the hour (or by the live-in package).  
You pay the caregiver.  
You keep the spread — after office, insurance, marketing, and everything else that keeps the lights on.

Simple on a whiteboard.  
Hard in real life — because the product *is* people showing up on time for other people’s parents.

---

## What the business actually is

You’re a staffing + relationship company wearing a care brand.

Important scope lock: this page is the **agency** model — you recruit, pay, and send caregivers.  
It is **not** skilled nursing / Medicare clinical home health.  
It is **not** a senior-placement referral fee business.  
It is **not** “direct hire” / registry-only concepts where families hire the caregiver and you mostly match (Brian’s contrast: most home care of this type needs an office and a real caregiver army).

Typical service menu (non-medical):

- **Companionship** — consistent human company  
- **Homemaking** — cleaning, laundry, mail, errands  
- **Personal care** — bathing, meals, mobility help  
- **Live-in / around-the-clock** — heavier packages  
- **Memory care support** — Alzheimer’s / dementia stages (still non-medical in this model)

A client often starts with a few hours a week.  
Needs usually *grow* as the senior declines — more hours, more days, sometimes live-in.

That’s the recurring engine: once you’re trusted in the house, hours tend to expand… until they don’t (hospital, facility, death). Attrition is part of the model. You plan for it.

Medical / skilled nursing is a different license, different risk, different staffing.  
Many owners stay non-medical on purpose. Some add skilled later when cash flow and systems can carry it.

You’re not inventing demand.  
You’re organizing labor against a demand curve that demographics keep pushing up.

## How money comes in

You sell **billable hours**.

Not “number of logos on a client list.”  
Hours. Product mix. Utilization.

Private-pay clients get billed an hourly rate.  
Caregivers get paid an hourly rate.  
Your gross margin lives in that spread — before office staff, rent, recruiting, insurance, marketing, and (if franchised) royalty.

In Brian’s public education on the category, illustrative private-pay bands looked roughly like:

- Bill ~$28–$34 / hour  
- Pay caregivers ~$14–$18 / hour  

Those move hard by market. Coastal wages crush Midwestern ones. Always verify *your* metro.

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Billable hours** — the real KPI (not vanity client count)  
2. **Product mix** — short-term vs long-term vs live-in vs (if you take it) Medicaid-heavy work. Mix changes vulnerability  
3. **Utilization** — caregivers scheduled vs sitting idle; clients covered vs calling out empty  
4. **Referral engine** — hospitals, SNFs, discharge planners, elder-law / financial advisors, existing families  
5. **Retention** — of *both* clients and caregivers. Lose caregivers and you can’t take the next case

This is a **volume + systems** business.  
Operators across brands run from small local books to multi-million (and, in outlier cases, much larger) revenue — same model, wildly different execution.

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Demographics are not subtle.

Seniors 85+ in the U.S. were on a path to roughly double (Brian’s newsletter cited ~7M → ~15M by 2040).  
People want to age in place. Facilities are expensive and emotionally hard. Home care sits in the gap.

Demand is not the scarce resource.

**Caregivers are.**

Every serious operator says some version of the same thing: the bottleneck is finding and keeping people who will do this job well, show up, and stay.

Aging population + preference for home + constrained labor supply = structural opportunity *and* structural pain. You don’t get one without the other.

## Micro: what actually drives one office

A territory lives or dies on boring ops:

**Territory / TAM**  
Protected geography (if franchised) or a practical service radius.  
Huge senior counts on paper mean nothing if you can’t staff cases.  
A mobile conversation Brian’s used: tens of clients against ~100k seniors in a territory is still a tiny share — room to grow, and also proof that “market size” isn’t the constraint.

**Caregiver pipeline**  
Recruiting. Screening. Background checks. Onboarding. Scheduling. Culture.  
This is the factory. No caregivers = no billable hours = no business.

**Client acquisition**  
B2B relationships (discharge, facilities, professionals) plus family inbound.  
Trust sells. Google ads help. Reputation keeps the phone ringing.

**Scheduling / coverage**  
Someone calls out Friday at 6pm. Mom still needs a bath Saturday morning.  
Your brand lives or dies in that gap.

**Office + owner roles (early stage)**  
Day one is usually two seats:  
- **Sales / marketing** — relationships, intakes, community presence  
- **Office / ops** — hiring, scheduling, payroll, billing  

Many owners *are* one of those seats for a long time.  
Ted’s framing from the interview: he didn’t buy a passive company — he **purchased a job** (seller/spouse had been doing payroll/billing/HR; he owned sales). That’s honest. Stage-1 senior care is not a spreadsheet investment.

**Client book shape**  
A few huge live-in cases look great until one goes to the hospital.  
(In Ted’s interview, a single living client could represent on the order of ~20% of a week’s hours — high LTV, high concentration.)  
A base of smaller-hour clients can keep caregivers working when a big case disappears. Mix matters more than ego.

**Systems**  
Recruiting process, billing accuracy, onboarding speed, caregiver communication.  
Ted’s growth story after takeover was systems lifts — not “try harder.”

**Overhead shape**  
Small office (interview / onboard / admin). Not a big retail box.  
Low facility cost vs retail or restaurant — then labor eats the advantage if you’re sloppy.

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

Startup for many non-medical senior care franchises often lands roughly **$90k–$200k** all-in depending on the system and working-capital buffer (Brian’s newsletter cited ~$90–150k; another teaching clip cited ~$130–200k). Fee, marketing, deposits, software, training, working capital — Item 7 in an FDD is the real checklist. Independent startups vary.

You need a small physical office.  
You need working capital so you can pay caregivers before families pay you.

Illustrative operator math (education only — from Brian’s partner / playbook material, not a guarantee):

- Multi-territory books in the low millions of revenue with dozens of clients is a *shape* people chase  
- Rough mental model some owners use: revenue ≈ clients × average annual hours value (e.g. playbook-style ~$60k/client/year illustrations — market and mix dependent)  
- Outliers exist. So do stalled offices that never crack staffing

What that means in plain English:

The *category* has demand and can scale.  
A *specific office* is a people-ops machine. Weak recruiting = weak P&L.

Your diligence questions should sound like:

- What’s the caregiver vacancy / fill rate?  
- What’s average tenure?  
- What’s the billable-hour mix (short / long / live-in / payer type)?  
- What happens when the top 3 clients leave in the same quarter?  
- Who owns sales vs scheduling today — and what happens if that person quits?  
- Private pay vs Medicaid exposure?  
- License / compliance posture for *this* state?

Same brain you’d use buying any service business: baseline, systems, labor risk, growth path, and whether *you* want to run this for years.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

This section is the point. Soft marketing skips it. Operators live it.

**1. Finding and retaining caregivers**  
#1 bottleneck. Competitive wages, tough work, emotional labor. Your “employer brand” matters as much as your consumer brand.

**2. Call-outs, no-shows, last-minute coverage**  
The client doesn’t care that your caregiver’s car died. Your reputation does.

**3. Fragmented remote workforce**  
Caregivers work alone in strangers’ homes. Culture, accountability, and quality control are harder than a shop floor.

**4. Margin pressure (bill rate vs pay rate)**  
Raise caregiver pay to recruit → margin compresses unless bill rates and utilization keep up. Markets move. You’re always adjusting.

**5. Utilization & schedule density**  
Idle caregivers cost you. Overbooked caregivers burn out. Geography between clients wastes unpaid time.

**6. Client attrition you can’t control**  
Hospitalization. Move to facility. Death. Family conflict. Cases end. You need a pipeline, not one whale.

**7. Book concentration risk**  
Too much revenue in a handful of live-in cases = fragile. Ted’s lesson: grow a base of smaller accounts so caregivers stay busy when a big one disappears.

**8. Private pay vs Medicaid (if you take it)**  
Different rates, paperwork, cash timing, and client profiles. Mix changes your vulnerability. Know what you’re actually selling.

**9. Licensing, compliance, and state rules**  
Non-medical still has rules. Background checks, insurance, documentation. Wrong state assumptions get expensive.

**10. Liability and trust**  
You’re sending employees into homes with vulnerable adults. Insurance, screening, training, and incident response aren’t optional line items.

**11. Family dynamics**  
Adult children disagree. Guilt. Money stress. You’re managing emotions as much as hours.

**12. After-hours and weekend reality**  
Care doesn’t clock out at 5. Coverage models that ignore nights/weekends break.

**13. Referral relationship building**  
Hospitals and facilities don’t hand you cases because you bought a logo. Consistency over months.

**14. Competition**  
50+ franchise brands in the space plus independents. Differentiation is execution and trust, not a clever slogan.

**15. Owner intensity in stage 1**  
Active involvement is the norm. If you want pure passive from day one, this category will punish you. Many buyers are buying a leadership job with upside — be honest about that.

**16. Scaling past the founder**  
Systems for recruiting, billing, onboarding, and sales have to outlive your personal hustle. Otherwise you own a stressful job with a logo.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means home care is a **real service business** where labor *is* the product.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re wired for people ops and relationship selling  
- You’re compassionate *and* willing to hold standards (soft heart, hard systems)  
- You’re okay building a recruiting machine, not just a marketing machine  
- Multi-year grind toward a professionalized office sounds exciting  
- You want a business tied to a demographic wave, not a fad

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want passive income immediately  
- You hate recruiting, scheduling drama, or emotional family calls  
- You’re looking for a “set it and forget it” asset  
- You freeze when someone calls out and a client is uncovered  
- You need a creative blank slate (this is proven playbook territory)
- You’re actually looking for a lean “matchmaking / direct-hire” concept — different model, different page

Franchise vs independent is a separate decision.  
Same model either way. Franchise trades fee/royalty for playbook, brand, and peer network — only worth it if the system actually helps you win caregivers and cases.

---

## Risks (the short list)

- Can’t staff → can’t grow (or keep) revenue  
- Wage spiral without bill-rate discipline → busy and broke  
- Whale-client concentration → one hospital trip nukes the month  
- Compliance / liability miss → existential  
- Distracted or absentee ownership → same brand, stalled office  
- Franchise stack (if applicable) → royalties + rules; verify the system is worth it  
- Confusing “market size” with “your fillable hours”

---

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “aging population.”

You’re buying the right to organize caregivers against families who need help — if you can recruit, schedule, sell, and retain.

Some people should start greenfield.  
Some people should buy a tired office and install systems (rollup of legacy owners is a real strategy in this category).  
Some people should walk — and put the capital into a different vehicle.

The question isn’t “are seniors a growth market?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or just a busier version of a job with higher stakes when someone doesn’t show up?**

If you want help comparing doors (this model vs others), that’s a conversation.  
Not a pitch deck.
