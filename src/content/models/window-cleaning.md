Streaks on the south side.  
A restaurant storefront that looks tired by Tuesday lunch.  
A homeowner who wants the glass clear before holiday guests — and again before spring.

How long do you wait — and who shows up with a **real route and a process**, not a ladder-on-a-roof gamble and a cash quote from Craigslist?

There's a lot of things you can put off.  
"The windows look filthy from the sidewalk" is not one of them when curb appeal, guest nights, or commercial foot traffic are on the line.

That's **window cleaning**.

Not a solo squeegee with a Facebook ad and a consumer ladder.  
Not pure **residential maid routes** as the whole company (related recurring muscle — different inside-the-home trust stack).  
Not pure **pressure washing / soft wash** as the identity of the book (common attach — separate spine).

A **route + density** exterior (and sometimes interior) glass company that sells residential packages and commercial accounts, keeps crews safe at height, and lives or dies on **whether dirty windows turn into *renewing* calendar stops — without racing every uninsured weekend warrior to the bottom**.

Simple on a whiteboard: dirty glass → clean → invoice → book the next visit.  
Brutal if you confuse "anyone can buy a squeegee" with "this scales itself."

## What the business actually is

You're a **mobile glass / exterior cleaning** company — and the category secretly contains two customer worlds that share a van but not the same sales cycle:

1. **Residential** — exterior packages on a cadence (often quarterly-ish); interior less often; first-clean and "company is coming" spikes  
2. **Commercial** — storefronts, restaurants, offices, hotels, light multi-story (system-dependent); often **weekly / bi-weekly / monthly** when you win the account

Typical menu:

1. **Residential exterior window clean** — panes, frames, screens (menu varies); the marketing hook in many markets  
2. **Residential interior** — less frequent; access and pet/furniture friction; higher trust bar  
3. **Commercial storefront / restaurant glass** — high-visibility recurring; card-on-file when you can win it  
4. **Multi-story / water-fed pole work** — pure-water poles that keep feet on the ground up to system height limits (often taught as roughly mid-rise, not true high-rise rope work)  
5. **Attach services** — gutters, soft wash / pressure wash, screens, solar panels (system- and skill-dependent)  
6. **One-time / seasonal spikes** — move-in, post-construction, holiday hosting, pollen dumps, storm grit

Typical loop:

1. **Lead** — Google / LSA, door-to-door / storefront walk, truck wraps / uniforms, referrals, property managers, sister-brand handoffs (paint, exterior clean), prior-year residential rebook, commercial renewals  
2. **Quote** — pane count / access / stories / interior vs exterior / screens / water source / ladder vs pole decision  
3. **Route the van** — density beats hero drives; weather windows; two-person crews common  
4. **Clean + document** — before/after when useful, panes completed, streaks/callbacks caught same day  
5. **Collect / put on the book** — card on file for commercial cadence; residential package / next-visit calendar  
6. **Chase density + renewals** — the quiet half of a *company* vs a busy Saturday ladder guy

**Scope lock for this page:** branded (or independent) **window cleaning** built on **route density**, residential packages + commercial recurring accounts, water-fed / ladder safety reality, and (when you run them) gutter / soft-wash attaches. High-rise rope access and pure janitorial glass contracts appear in some metros — call them out in diligence; don't pretend every greenfield starts as a downtown tower specialist.

**Out of scope (different models):**  
- Pure **residential maid / house cleaning** routes (adjacent recurring — separate page)  
- Pure **pressure washing / soft wash** as the company identity (adjacent attach — separate page)  
- Pure **high-rise / rope-access** facade companies  
- Solo "I own a squeegee and a Facebook ad" as the whole *company* story  
- Pure **blind / window-covering install** measure-and-product companies

Brian's published frame on the category:

- Recurring-revenue YT (Pink's segment): residential often on a longer cadence (think quarterly-ish exteriors); commercial can be **weekly / bi-weekly** — restaurant glass that looks filthy kills foot traffic; framed as **low-cost / low-overhead** that scales by **people + trucks + reputation**  
- Full Pink's podcast interview: commercial vs residential split; water-fed poles vs ladders; density (most revenue from a tight zip cluster); van-as-unit economics; heights capped below true high-rise; culture / brand as hiring wedge — treat as *their* operating story, not your forecast  
- Teaching he amplifies: **windows get dirty again** — one good clean → maintenance plan or next-quarter rebook is the asset  
- Stacking-trucks teaching elsewhere: window cleaning named as a capacity business you grow **van by van** once one crew's route is full  
- Named systems exist (Fish Window Cleaning, Pink's / peers, Shine Window Cleaning, regional independents). Treat them as **proof the category franchises and places** — **not** as the point of this page

Mobile-service staging notes apply the same ladder as maid routes / exterior wash: 1 van you're the closer-dispatcher-backup tech → 2–3 with a lead cleaner → multi-crew + sales seat → multi-market only after density and weather math work.

## How money comes in

You sell **completed window cleans** — residential packages and commercial account visits — not "hours on a timesheet" the customer sees, and not a free "I'll wipe your front panes if you leave a review" hobby.

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Route density** — stops per day vs windshield; zip discipline; commercial clusters on the same strip  
2. **Recurring book vs one-time fluff** — commercial cadence + residential packages beat one spring Saturday forever  
3. **Average ticket / mix** — exterior-only vs interior+exterior vs storefront weekly vs light multi-story vs stadium/portfolio work  
4. **Van + crew utilization** — billable clean days vs weather, callbacks, and "wrong access / locked gate" wasted trips  
5. **Labor %** — crew burdened cost on physical, outdoor, height-adjacent work  
6. **Marketing CAC vs close rate** — easy-entry noise is loud; brand/show-up/referrals are the escape hatch  
7. **Commercial retention** — card-on-file weekly restaurants and monthly offices compound; rebid risk is real on price-sensitive accounts  
8. **Attach / upsell** — gutters, soft wash, screens — margin *or* chaos if every job becomes a different company

Gross margin lives in **price − crew labor (burdened) − supplies/water filtration (− commissions/spiffs)**.  
Then overhead: van leases or notes + wraps, insurance (GL + auto + workers' comp; height/ladder exposure matters), marketing, small yard/lot if you stage vans, software, and (if franchised) royalty + brand/MAP + tech fees.

Revenue mix usually looks like some blend of:

| Channel | What it feels like |
|--------|---------------------|
| **Residential exterior packages** | Marketing-visible. Mid tickets. Rebook / quarterly cadence when you sell the plan. Easy-entry noise is loudest here. |
| **Residential interior** | Higher trust + access friction. Less frequent. Converts from exterior relationships — or becomes custom chaos. |
| **Commercial storefront / restaurant** | Recurring spine when won. Weekly/bi-weekly possible. Card-on-file is gold. Price pressure on renewals. |
| **Offices / hotels / light multi-story** | Bigger tickets, bid process, insurance docs. Pole systems matter. |
| **Large venues / portfolios** | Five-figure jobs exist in some books (arenas, campuses). Capability + crew surge / sub network — not week-one default. |
| **Attach (gutters / soft wash / screens)** | Margin when staffed; dilutes focus and safety stack when every crew becomes a different trade. |
| **One-time / seasonal** | Spikes that feel like permanent income until the calendar goes quiet. |

Industry shape (education only): Brian's Pink's interview aired roughly **~$1,800** residential *annual* customer value on a package-shaped book, commercial visit floors in the **~$150–$300** band for smaller accounts, and large venue cleans that can sit in the **tens of thousands** — useful for intuition, not your forecast. Affiliate teaching has framed **one van** in the **~$250k–$300k** revenue conversation on a mature density story. Healthy operators watch **revenue per van-day**, **stops per route**, **% recurring**, and **commercial renewal rate** as hard as "leads."

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Windows get dirty. Pollen, rain spots, kitchen grease film, winter grit, construction dust. Restaurants need glass that doesn't scream neglect. Offices and retail want the front of house to match the brand. Homeowners buy curb appeal and "I can see outside again."

When the economy is good, discretionary residential packages and "whole street looks sharp" work spikes.  
When the economy is tight, commercial maintenance and "the storefront has to look acceptable" work still creates vans to fill — ticket mix and residential volume shift; demand doesn't vanish.

That's why Brian puts window cleaning in the **recurring / mobile exterior** family: the need resets on a calendar; **your density and your safety discipline still can fail**.

The constraint isn't "glass gets dirty."

It's **route density + recurring conversion + heights/safety competence + commercial fulfillment** in *your* metro.

Easy entry (squeegee + bucket + Craigslist + a logo on a door) keeps competition permanently loud. Process, insurance, water-fed systems, show-up rate, and commercial accounts amateurs can't keep are how a *company* separates from a guy who climbed a ladder for the weekend.

## Micro: what actually drives one company

**Route density (this category's quiet killer)**  
Brian's amplified Pink's story: a large share of revenue from a **tight zip cluster**, crews staying roughly **~20–25 minutes** from the van lot — not random suburb hero drives. Three sparse neighborhoods and a "gate code failed / dog loose / third-story screen stuck" list is a different business than a strip of restaurants on the same corridor. Geography and book quality decide.

**Recurring conversion (commercial + residential packages)**  
Commercial *can* be weekly. Residential *can* be quarterly exteriors + rarer interiors — **if** you sell the plan and rebook instead of hoping they remember next spring. One-time cleans that never return are expensive CAC with a streak guarantee attached.

**Heights, ladders & water-fed poles (safety is the product)**  
Ladders are where injuries, insurance, and "I'm afraid of heights" hiring walls live. Water-fed / pure-water pole systems (filtration + pole brush + rinse) are how many professional books keep **both feet on the ground** up to mid-story limits. True high-rise rope work is a different company. Brian's guests taught maxing expertise at roughly **~five stories and below** and leaving tower specialists the skinny tall slice — strategy, not universal law. Owners who treat ladder day as casual meet workers' comp and Google reviews the hard way.

**Easy entry vs professional bar**  
Home Depot starter kits exist by design. Teachable craft (day-one cleaner vs senior is often *speed*, not whether glass can get clean) is a feature — and the reason amateurs undercut forever. Insurance, poles, commercial docs, and show-up cadence are the wedge.

**Commercial sales vs residential grind**  
Brian's recurring segment loves restaurant / retail glass on a short cadence. Winning it needs reliability, insurance certificates, and crews that don't streak the front door before Friday dinner. Residential fills reviews and packages; it also invites the cash-ladder crowd. Price-sensitive commercial renewals can compress margin even when volume looks great.

**Crew recruiting & retention**  
Outdoor, early, physical, weather-exposed, occasionally ladder-adjacent. Brand and culture can fill a pipeline (Pink's teaching leaned hard here) — but the work is still hard. Demand without cleaners is a ringing phone and angry "they never came after the rain delay" reviews.

**Owner role (honest version)**  
Early stage: quoting, door-knocking storefronts, riding jobs, hiring, weather triage, cash, and often backup tech.  
Not "I bought a logo and went fishing."  
Later: sales seat + lead tech so you're not every squeegee and every Google reply — still not passive.

**Systems**  
CRM / quoting (pane counts matter), card-on-file billing, route software, review engine, weather reschedule scripts, commercial renewal calendar, equipment / filter maintenance. Guesswork doesn't scale past one heroic van.

The *category* has durable, visible demand and can build recurring commercial + packaged residential when density, safety, and sales work.  
A *specific book* can look slammed every Saturday and still be broke — fat windshield, one-time only, ladder claims, cheap residential only, or an owner who never got off the squeegee.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

**1. Easy entry is the trap**  
Anyone can buy a squeegee and *look* like a competitor by Monday. Trust, insurance, show-up rate, streak-free glass, and commercial fulfillment are the product — not the cheapest "I'll do your front panes Saturday" text. Simple to *start*. Hard to *build a company*.

**2. Route density / windshield death**  
Low-ticket stops without geography discipline means you work hard and net nothing. Sparse territories and access failures quietly murder utilization. Density is strategy, not a nice-to-have.

**3. Confusing one-time cleans with a recurring business**  
Brian's teaching loves the "windows get dirty again" loop. If you never sell packages, card-on-file commercial, or next-visit calendar holds, you own a project hustle with outdoor weather risk — not a renewing book.

**4. Heights, ladders & safety landmines**  
Falls, tip-overs, second-story screens, "just this once" roof edges. Water-fed poles reduce ladder days when used correctly — they don't eliminate liability, training, or the hiring wall of people who won't leave the ground. High-rise ego without rope-access competence is how shops meet lawyers.

**5. Weather & seasonality are co-owners**  
Rain-outs, freeze, heat that cooks crews, pollen weeks that spike then vanish. Northern owners who annualize peak months meet winter as a cash crisis — unless mix, process, or reserves were the plan *before* the first ice.

**6. Residential price war with uninsured amateurs**  
They undercut. You carry commercial auto, workers' comp, real equipment, and callback policies. Racing them to the bottom is how professionals go broke while Instagram still looks busy.

**7. Commercial is the opportunity — and the renewal fight**  
Weekly restaurants and retail glass can be the spine. Winning them needs reliability and insurance. Keeping them means surviving price-sensitive renewals and "we've always paid this much" procurement. Selling commercial you can't staff creates reputation death faster than no marketing at all.

**8. Streaks, callbacks & "clean" is binary**  
Glass is yes/no in the customer's eye. One sloppy crew ruins the next four stops' reviews. Speed without QA is how you buy free re-cleans.

**9. Crew scarcity / height comfort**  
Not everyone wants outdoor physical work — fewer still want ladders or poles on windy days. Peak-season no-shows turn weather windows into missed revenue you don't get back.

**10. Interior access & trust friction**  
Pets, alarm codes, "don't move the heirloom," occupied homes. Interior work raises the trust bar toward maid-service energy without always paying maid-service LTV.

**11. Working capital on a "simple" wash story**  
Van note, insurance, poles/filtration, spring ad spend, and payroll burn while density and learning curves hit. Undercapitalized launches stall when CAC and rain hit the same month.

**12. Owner still on the squeegee (or still the only closer)**  
If the company only works when you're the tech, the estimator, and the Google-reply department, you own a job with a wrap — same staging truth as maid routes and exterior wash.

**13. Sales engine that outruns fulfillment**  
Ads or door-knocking can fill a calendar fast. If crews, poles, and QA can't keep up — or rain stacks the backlog — you bought churn and chargebacks with a van payment attached.

**14. Attach-service distraction**  
Gutters, soft wash, screens, solar — profitable *when* you have process. Every new service is a new way to damage property, climb higher, or dilute the crew. Focus is a strategy.

**15. Franchise stack (if you go that route)**  
Royalty + brand fund + local ad minimums + tech fees + rules + territory vs playbook, van specs, lead flow, and peer network. Worth it only if the system fills dense routes and trains safety/process — not because the uniform looks good on Instagram. Young systems can grow outlet counts fast; diligence Item 19 cohort quality and Item 21 franchisor health like an adult.

**16. Buying a tired spring book without renewal math**  
Customer lists look like assets until you discover last year's revenue was one-time Facebook exteriors and a commercial account that already switched. Verify recurring and density — not "we've cleaned 10,000 homes historically."

**17. Semi-absentee fantasy**  
Manager salary on a thin, weather-gated book is how shops bleed. Semi-absentee usually needs a mature multi-van machine with real recurring accounts — not a greenfield hope or one hot April.

**18. Confusing "windows always get dirty" with "easy money"**  
Timeless, visible demand is real. Your P&L still needs operators who can win the density war, the safety war, and the easy-entry price war at the same time.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means window cleaning is a **real route + labor + safety business** where **easy entry creates permanent noise** — and scale only shows up when density, recurring conversion, and heights discipline actually work.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You're wired to sell *and* fulfill — quotes, crews, and streak-free quality control  
- Route-density puzzles (zips, storefront strips, package rebooks) excite you more than one-off project heroics  
- You're willing to take heights/safety seriously — poles, training, insurance — not "we'll figure ladders out"  
- Commercial account hunting (restaurants, retail, PMs) sounds exciting once one van is dialed  
- Van-by-van scale in a loud, easy-entry market sounds like a fight worth picking  
- If northern / wet: you can stomach (and plan for) weather holes and freeze weeks

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want passive / semi-absentee from month one  
- You refuse to recruit people who work outside — or anyone who might touch a ladder / pole  
- You're allergic to weather, callbacks, and "there's a streak on the patio door" realities  
- You thought a Home Depot squeegee kit *was* the business plan  
- You're undercapitalized for a real van, insurance, poles, and density ramp  
- You want pure maid-service indoor rhythm *or* pure pressure-wash truck energy every week and hate glass-package cadence  
- You're looking for a craft job with a logo (you *being* the only cleaner forever)

Franchise vs independent is a separate call.  
Same category either way: generate leads → quote panes/access correctly → clean without streaks or falls → collect → put them on the book → escape the amateur price war on trust, density, and reliability.

## The ownership point

You're not buying "windows get dirty."

You're buying the right to run a **route + density machine** that turns visible glass friction into completed jobs — and, if you can earn it, commercial cadence and residential packages — while the easy-entry crowd keeps the residential price war loud forever.

Some people should start with one serious van, master pole vs ladder safety, and prove they can acquire *and rebook* jobs profitably before adding leases.  
Some people should buy a tired local book (or a franchise resale) and install density + recurring systems — consolidators and franchise systems chase this category because the street-level demand is obvious.  
Some people should niche (restaurant strips, HOA exteriors, light commercial multi-story) once the core engine works — Brian's teaching keeps pointing at recurring commercial as the grown-up game.  
Some people should walk — and put the same capital into a vehicle with cleaner indoor economics, less weather/height surface area, or less permanent amateur noise.

The question isn't "do windows get dirty?"

It's: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a stressful glass company that only works when you're the squeegee, the weatherman, the safety officer, and the backup crew?**
