You’ve seen the bins.

Shopping-center corners. Gas stations. School parking lots. Big metal boxes people dump bags of clothes into and forget about.

Most folks think charity.

A lot of them aren’t.

That’s the first thing to understand about **clothing collection bins**: it’s a **for-profit logistics business** dressed like a donation box.

Not boutique racks and a buy desk.  
Not curated thrift (that’s [used clothing resale](/models/used-clothing-resale) — opposite energy).  
Not “I invent a fashion brand.”

**Routes + bins + wholesale pounds.**  
You win on placements and service — not merchandising.

---

## What the business actually is

You’re not running a thrift store.

You’re not sorting jeans on hangers for retail sell-through.

You’re placing collection bins in a territory, emptying them on a route, staging the clothes, and selling them **wholesale by the pound**.

Think: **opposite of retail**.

Retail dies if the shelf is empty.  
This model dies if the bin isn’t emptied.

It’s closer to a **vending / route / asset** business than a clothing brand.

Typical loop (brand-agnostic shape Brian’s guest walkthroughs teach):

1. **Place bins** at high-convenience spots — schools, churches, gas / c-stores, retail centers, community sites with written permission  
2. **People drop clothes** — usually grocery bags (~10–12 lbs each). Mixed lots. No hangers. No “we only want this season’s color.”  
3. **Sensors + app** (in modern systems) flag which bins are fat  
4. **A driver** empties a route (often ~10 bins / shift early on), wipes, locks, hauls to a staged trailer  
5. When the trailer hits weight (~40,000 lbs is a common teaching number), a **regional textile buyer** swaps trailers  
6. You get paid **per pound** — commodity textile pricing, not SKU fashion pricing

You’re not negotiating Fendi vs stained tees at the dock. In franchised versions of this model, the system often brokers buyers. Independent operators still live in the same physics: **bins full → trailer full → check clears**.

Important industry rule of the model: **don’t cherry-pick the good stuff for eBay.** Buyers expect mixed lots. Sort out the Air Jordans and the price per pound can drop — and trust dies.

**Scope lock for this page:** for-profit (or mixed) **textile collection bins + route service + wholesale pounds**.  
Not [used clothing resale](/models/used-clothing-resale) (buy from the public → hang → turn → sell).  
Not [kids clothing resale](/models/kids-clothing-resale) (same retail turns game, different size calendar).  
Not charity thrift storefronts as the whole company.  
Not [junk removal](/models/junk-removal) (labor loads a customer’s driveway — related *route muscle*, different product and tip-fee math).

Brian’s teaching frame on the category (Clothes Bin and peers as **proof the model franchises** — not the point of the page):

- Asset-based: you’re buying **bins** that sit in the wild collecting free textiles 24/7  
- Tech layer (sensors / app / route tools) is what makes “semi-absentee” *possible* — not what magically removes host relationships  
- Convenience predicts drop-offs; gas / c-store density has been used as a territory measuring stick for a reason  
- Schools are a special channel: permission + revenue share + parent word-of-mouth  
- Scale is more **shifts and bin count**, not inventing a new product every week  
- Named systems exist (Clothes Bin and independent / peer collectors). Treat logos as **category proof**, not a recommendation

## How money comes in

Gross margin lives in **pounds × price per pound − host splits − labor − truck − staging − bad-bin drag**.

You’re not a fashion company.  
You’re a **textile logistics** company that sells mixed material by the pound.

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Placement quality** — convenience spots that fill; dead spots that don’t  
2. **Service discipline** — empty before overflow; wipe; lock; don’t make the host hate you  
3. **Bin density in a tight geography** — windshield kills route businesses the same way it kills scooping and junk  
4. **Commodity pound price** — negotiated (or system-brokered) wholesale; cents move  
5. **Host economics** — school % share vs fixed gas/retail splits; those are real COGS  
6. **Contamination / illegal dumping response** — trash beside the bin is *your* problem with the host and often the city  
7. **Working capital to first trailer sale** — weeks of collection before the first serious check

Revenue is lumpy early: one trailer every ~6–8 weeks at starter bin counts is a common education shape. More bins → more frequent sales, not a totally different playbook.

What you are *not* selling day-to-day:

- Cute Instagram merchandising  
- Individual SKU margins on racks  
- “Is this still in style?”

Downstream buyers sort, shred, export, resell wholesale, turn scrap into insulation, etc. You don’t have to care which path a sock takes — as long as the **pound price holds** and the buyer stays liquid.

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Closets overflow either way.

Good times → people buy new → bags pile in the trunk.  
Bad times → people still clear space (and hunt value elsewhere — that’s the *resale* page).

Most donated clothes never become someone’s new favorite shirt. A huge share still heads toward landfill or industrial reuse. Nonprofits (Goodwill / Salvation Army–type) are only a **slice** of the collection world — guest framing on Brian’s show put nonprofits roughly around ~15% of the segment, with the rest in for-profit / mixed collection. Treat that as education shape, not a census you can take to the bank.

Tax write-off motivation for donors has also weakened over time for many households. What’s left is **convenience**: how long is that bag living in the trunk before someone dumps it?

That’s the demand engine. Not fashion cycles. Not vibes. Lazy logistics for households.

Municipal pressure is the other macro: cities crack down when bins become dumping magnets. That doesn’t kill the category — it **raises the bar** for operators who service cleanly and host relationships that survive inspections.

## Micro: what actually drives one territory

**Location is the business.**

Not branding. Not Instagram. Placement.

**Host agreements**  
Written permission. Revenue share or fixed split. Who cleans overflow. Who gets the call when someone dumps a mattress beside the box. Vague handshake = future eviction.

**Schools (when you can win them)**  
Bins on campus; school gets a cut of what’s collected there (interview education: roughly **low-teens %**). Parents get told “donate here.” Passive fundraising for the school. Principals talk to principals. Relationship channel, not a Google ad.

**Gas / c-store / retail**  
Convenience predicts drops. Usually a **fixed** split / fee. Same physics: permission + service or you’re gone.

**Move the dead assets**  
Bad spot? Track it. Move the bin. That’s the asset advantage vs buying a retail box — the steel can roll (within host and municipal rules).

**Route design**  
Sensors help. Windshield still wins or loses. Cluster bins or you pay a driver to tour the county.

**Staging**  
Box truck → 53' trailer on a boat/RV lot (common teaching picture) → buyer swap. No shelf inventory. Capacity and swap timing matter when volume climbs.

**Labor shape**  
Often a part-time / side-shift driver early (UPS/FedEx-type energy in the interview story). Owner monitors the app a few hours a week *if* placements and hosts are healthy. That “if” is the whole company.

**Owner involvement**  
Semi-absentee is a *design claim*, not a birthright. Overflow, angry hosts, driver no-shows, and city complaints bring you back into the business fast.

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

Franchise brands that publish an Item 19 will show ranges. Independents have P&Ls and scale stories. Brian’s podcast walkthrough + newsletter notes on one system’s disclosures sketched a shape like this — **education only; numbers age; verify current docs**:

**Operations shape (interview / teaching):**

- Starter package often framed around **~20 bins**  
- Bin capacity teaching number ~**600 lbs**  
- Driver route: ~**10 bins** / shift early; box truck weight is a hard limit  
- Trailer sale: ~**40,000 lbs** every **~6–7 weeks** at starter density  
- Wholesale: interview band roughly **mid-50¢/lb** (e.g. ~50–55¢); Brian’s March 2024 newsletter note cited a national partner around **$0.50/lb** — commodity, not a forever quote  
- School share: roughly **~12–15%** of what’s collected at that site (varies)  
- Territory designed to hold more than you start with — teaching band **~60–80+ bins** in a market  
- Growth: buy bins in increments (packages of 10/20), add shifts, eventually lease/buy the truck instead of renting every week

**Unit / disclosure education (one brand’s public Item 19 shapes Brian surfaced — not your forecast):**

- Average bin collected ~**902 lbs/month** (2024 FDD Item 19 education note in Brian’s thread)  
- Avg franchisee figures discussed in that writeup era: roughly **~$362k revenue / ~$138k income** style averages — read footnotes; “income” ≠ your take-home after every real cost  
- Top-end unit stories in the same notes climbed into **seven-figure sales** with much higher disclosed income — outliers exist; so do quiet territories  
- Investment band discussed: roughly **~$140k–$194k** all-in for fee + ~20 bins + working capital to first sale window (Item 7 shapes move by year)  
- Bin cost teaching: roughly **~$3k–$3.5k** each (+ freight) when buying more steel

What that means in plain English:

The *model* can work as a lean route + asset machine.  
A *specific territory* can be a graveyard of dead placements, host churn, and a pound price that drifted while you weren’t looking.

Diligence questions that matter more than memorizing averages:

- Lbs per bin per month last 12 months — by site type (school vs gas vs retail)?  
- Host churn / eviction rate? Time-to-replace a lost site?  
- Pound price history (trailing 24–36 months) and buyer concentration?  
- Overflow / dumping complaints and municipal permits / bans in *this* city?  
- Driver reliability and true fully loaded route cost per pound?  
- Trailer cycle time and working-capital need between sales?  
- If franchised: royalty / fees, required placement help, buyer brokerage terms, territory size vs realistic bin count  
- Cherry-picking controls (process + culture) — because skimming wrecks the wholesale relationship  
- Owner hours on “app monitoring” vs host sales and firefighting — is this transferable?

Same spreadsheet brain you’d use for any route business: density, asset turns, variable commodity price, and whether the operator can keep hosts happy when it rains.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

**1. Site agreements are the business — and they’re fragile**  
Permission, splits, insurance naming, who cleans overflow, who gets the 2 a.m. complaint. Handshake placements die. Written agreements with clear service SLAs survive. Lose the host and the bin is a $3k lawn ornament until you find a new pad.

**2. Placement is hard (vending energy)**  
Brian’s guest frame: location is the key factor — same class of problem as placing vending machines, with a bit more nimbleness because you can *move* steel. Still: saturated lots, landlords who already hate bins, schools that say no, and “perfect” sites that don’t fill. Finding and keeping great spots is the moat. Servicing is the homework.

**3. Contamination is not a footnote**  
People dump wet trash, half-broken toys, paint cans, electronics, furniture *beside* the bin when it won’t fit. Mixed textile lots already include junk. Your driver still has to deal with the pile, the smell, and the host’s parking lot looking like a dump. Contamination tax shows up as labor, disposal, and lost sites.

**4. Illegal dumping / theft / scavenging**  
Bins attract nighttime dumping and scavenging. Locks and roll-bars help; they don’t make humans honest. Stolen contents, cut locks, and “someone else’s trash next to your brand” are operating costs. Hosts don’t care whose fault the couch is — they care that it sits there.

**5. Commodity pricing can drift**  
Pound price isn’t immune to textile market swings. Interview education: usually cents, not a 20-point cliff — but a few cents on tens of thousands of pounds is real money. You’re exposed to buyer markets you don’t control (export demand, shred/insulation outlets, wholesale sorting capacity).

**6. Municipal bans, permits, and “nuisance” ordinances**  
Cities regulate or ban unattended donation / collection boxes after dumping and blight complaints. Permits, setbacks, spacing rules, maintenance clocks (e.g. clear debris in 48 hours), zoning limits, and joint liability with property owners are common patterns in public ordinances. A “great” territory on a map can be a permitting maze — or a ban. Check the city *before* you fall in love with the steel.

**7. Overflow kills host relationships (and brands)**  
An overflowing, trashy bin is how the category gets regulated. Sensors help you see fat bins; discipline is emptying them. Miss a week and you’ve trained the host to call whoever removes you fastest.

**8. Truck routes and windshield death**  
Scattered bins look fine on a spreadsheet and bankrupt on diesel and hours. Density or die — same law as pet-waste routes and junk trucks. Route optimization software is a tool, not a substitute for clustered placements.

**9. Working capital until the first (and next) trailer clears**  
You buy steel and wait for pounds. Labor, truck rental, host splits, and lot fees run while the trailer fills. Undercapitalized operators panic-sell or skip service — which creates the dumping problems cities hate.

**10. Buyer dependency / concentration**  
Whether a franchisor brokers a pool or you sell locally, you’re exposed to buyer liquidity, honesty, and grading. One bad relationship or a slow weigh-and-pay cycle strands cash in fabric.

**11. Temptation to skim inventory**  
Air Jordans in the lot are a dopamine hit. Skimming wrecks mixed-lot pricing and trust. Process + culture (no personal picking) is a control system, not a moral slogan.

**12. “Green” story vs logistics economics**  
Sustainability marketing is real enough to use carefully. The P&L is still logistics: placements, pounds, routes, and host splits. Virtue doesn’t empty bins.

**13. Driver no-shows and “one person who knows the route”**  
Lean teams are a feature until the only driver ghosts on a Saturday and every school bin is overflowing for Monday drop-off. Cross-training and route SOPs matter more than the app screenshot.

**14. CapEx creep as you scale**  
More bins, owned truck instead of rental, second trailer, more shifts. Scale is formulaic *and* capital-hungry. “Just add 10 bins” assumes you can place them and fund the float.

**15. Semi-absentee fantasy**  
App-with-coffee is the marketing picture. Reality includes raining bin moves, angry principals, city notices, and commodity price calls. Semi-absentee usually means **systems + a reliable driver + healthy placements** — not “set and forget.”

**16. Confusing “people have clothes” with “easy money”**  
Timeless closet overflow is real. Your P&L still needs sites, service, and a buyer who pays.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means clothing bins are a **real route + asset + commodity business** where **hosts, cities, and pound price** decide whether the steel prints cash or rusts in a storage lot.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You like **systems, routes, and asset math** more than fashion identity  
- You’re willing to sell placements and babysit host relationships (relationship muscle > product taste)  
- Boring problems (move the bin, wipe the bin, call the driver) don’t offend you  
- Semi-absentee *after* the foundation is built sounds honest — not “passive from week one”  
- You’re fine owning a logistics machine that converts household convenience into wholesale pounds

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You actually want [used clothing resale](/models/used-clothing-resale) — boutique racks, buy desk, turns, retail energy  
- You need a creative clothing brand or merchandising playground  
- You’re allergic to municipal rules, host politics, and “the lot looks trashy” phone calls  
- You freeze when commodity prices move a few cents  
- You’re undercapitalized for bins + float to first trailer sales + truck costs  
- You want pure passive income with zero relationship work  
- You’re looking for a craft job with a logo (you *being* the only person emptying wet bags forever)

Franchise vs independent is a separate call.  
Same category either way: place → fill → empty clean → stage → sell pounds → protect the host.

## Risks (the short list)

- Dead placements / can’t win sites  
- Host churn from overflow or dumping  
- Municipal ban / permit failure  
- Pound-price drift or buyer concentration  
- Contamination / illegal dumping costs  
- Theft / scavenging / vandalism  
- Driver failure → overflow cascade  
- Working-capital crunch between trailer sales  
- Cherry-picking destroying wholesale terms  
- Royalty / fee stack without placement + buyer advantage (if franchised)  
- Thinking “bins are everywhere” substitutes for operator skill

---

## The ownership point

You’re not buying clothes.

You’re buying the right to run a **route + bin asset machine** that turns household trunk bags into wholesale textile pounds — if you can win sites, keep hosts and cities from hating you, empty on time, and live with commodity pricing.

Some people should start with a small bin package and prove placements before adding steel.  
Some people should buy a tired route book (or a franchise resale) and fix service + density.  
Some people should compare this door to retail used-clothing resale and notice they’re **opposites** — logistics vs merchandising — then pick the one that matches how they actually like to work.  
Some people should walk — and put the same capital into a vehicle with cleaner recurring tickets or less municipal surface area.

The question isn’t “are donation bins everywhere?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a stressful collection of metal boxes that only work when you’re the placement rep, the dispatcher, and the cleanup crew?**

If you want help comparing doors (this model vs used-clothing resale vs junk removal vs other route/asset vehicles), that’s a conversation.  
Not a pitch deck.
