Offices refresh every few years. Multifamily corridors get beat up. Warehouses need striping and safety coats. A property manager has three buildings and a capital budget that closes next quarter.

Someone has to bid it, staff a crew that can work nights and weekends, move through a facility without shutting the business down longer than the contract allows, and stand behind the finish when a punch list shows up after move-in.

That’s **commercial painting** — including **light commercial** (small offices, retail, multifamily turnovers) and heavier facility / contractor work.

Not [residential painting](/models/residential-painting) (living rooms, curb appeal, HOA letters).  
Not [commercial janitorial](/models/commercial-janitorial) (nightly cleaning contracts).  
A **B2B project / bid** business that lives on **relationships, crew scale, downtime windows, and documentation**.

Simple on a whiteboard.  
Brutal if you confuse “buildings need paint” with “this runs itself.”

---

## What the business actually is

You sell paint and coatings work to **businesses, property managers, GCs, and facility owners** — not primarily to homeowners standing in their foyer choosing swatches.

Typical menu:

1. **Light commercial interiors** — offices, medical suites, retail, restaurants (often after-hours)  
2. **Multifamily / turnover** — corridors, units, amenity spaces; volume and schedule density matter  
3. **Exterior commercial** — storefronts, HOA clubhouses, light industrial exteriors (weather + access + branding)  
4. **Facility / institutional** — schools, municipal, healthcare, warehouses — longer bid cycles, compliance paperwork, sometimes prevailing wage  
5. **GC / tenant-improvement attach** — paint as one trade in a bigger build-out

Typical loop:

1. **Lead / RFP** — property-manager relationships, GC bid invites, facility RFPs, past-customer capital cycles, broker intros  
2. **Walk / scope** — measure, photograph, read the spec, spot the gotchas (lead, asbestos adjacency, occupied floors, phasing)  
3. **Bid / negotiate** — lump sum or unit pricing; allowances; alternate deducts; bondability if required  
4. **Award / schedule** — downtime windows, night/weekend premiums, phasing with other trades  
5. **Produce** — larger crews, more protection/masking discipline, daily cleanup for occupied sites  
6. **Punch / collect** — retainage, progress billings, change orders — cash timing is part of the model  
7. **Warranty / next building** — your name stays on the PM’s phone list

**Scope lock for this page:** commercial / light-commercial **painting contractor** (independent or franchise with a B2B book).  
Out of scope: pure residential homeowner painting (see [residential painting](/models/residential-painting)); paint manufacturing; specialty industrial coatings as a different animal unless that’s your niche.

## How money comes in

You sell **projects and (sometimes) maintenance agreements**, not a steady “paint this bedroom Saturday” cadence.

Revenue mix usually looks like some blend of:

| Channel | What it feels like |
|--------|---------------------|
| **Light commercial / TI** | Faster than institutional. Relationship-driven. Often after-hours. |
| **Multifamily / PM accounts** | Repeat potential. Unit turns + corridor packages. Schedule density wins. |
| **GC subcontract** | Bigger tickets, dependent on someone else’s schedule and payment chain. |
| **Institutional / public** | Long bid cycles, paperwork, bonding, sometimes prevailing wage. Steadier once you’re on the list — harder to break in. |
| **Maintenance / touch-up contracts** | Soft recurring layer when you earn it; not automatic like pest routes. |

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Win rate on bids** — estimating skill + relationships, not “more RFPs” alone  
2. **Average job size / change orders** — under-scoped prep and access kill margin; clean change-order discipline saves it  
3. **Crew scale and utilization** — commercial jobs often need more painters at once, nights, or multi-day continuous production  
4. **Downtime / access constraints** — you bid the work *and* the window; miss the window and you eat labor or reputation  
5. **Payment terms / retainage** — 30–60+ day AR and retainage are normal; working capital is part of ownership  
6. **Compliance cost** — insurance limits, certified payroll, safety programs — overhead that residential books sometimes underweight

Gross margin still lives in **price − materials − production labor − supervision − warranty / punch**.  
Then overhead: estimators, PMs, safety, bonding capacity, trucks, marketing to PMs/GCs — and royalty if franchised.

Education shape (not a promise): commercial tickets can look larger than residential room work, but **bid competition, retainage, and night premiums** change the feel of the P&L. Diligence the mix, not a headline revenue number.

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Commercial real estate turns over. Tenants refresh. Brands rebrand. Facilities wear out. Capital budgets refresh on cycles even when homeowners pause vanity projects.

When the economy is good, TI and refresh accelerate.  
When the economy is tight, deferred maintenance and mandatory turnover work still show up — mix shifts toward multifamily turns, safety coats, and “must paint before lease-up,” not trophy lobbies.

The constraint isn’t “buildings exist.”  
It’s **bid relationships + estimating accuracy + crew capacity in awkward hours + cash timing** in *your* market.

## Micro: what actually drives one company

**Relationships beat spray tips**  
Property managers, GCs, and facility directors award work to people they trust to not torch a tenant’s week. Cold RFPs matter; warm phones matter more over time.

**Estimating + specs**  
Commercial scopes hide in the notes: surface prep standards, coating systems, phasing, occupied-building rules. Bad reads become change-order wars or free work.

**Crew scale & supervision**  
A six-painter night crew in an office tower is a different factory than two painters in a living room. Foremen, protection discipline, and daily punch culture are the product.

**Downtime windows**  
Nights, weekends, school breaks, between tenants. You’re selling labor *when the building allows it* — premiums and fatigue are real.

**Working capital**  
Materials up front, labor weekly, customer pays on their AP cycle. Commercial painting can look busy and still starve on AR.

**Owner role (honest version)**  
Early stage: estimating, PM relationships, production leadership, cash.  
Scale: estimators + PMs + crew leads so you’re not personally walking every RFP.  
Absentee with a thin bid pipeline is a slow way to fund someone else’s spray tips.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

**1. Bid cycles are slow and lumpy**  
Residential can close in a living room this week. Commercial can sit in a bid desk for months — then award all at once.

**2. Payment timing and retainage**  
Winning the job ≠ cash in the bank. Plan working capital or you’ll hate every “net 45.”

**3. Prevailing wage / public work (when you chase it)**  
Certified payroll, higher burdened labor, paperwork. Margin math changes; so does who can compete.

**4. Occupied-facility friction**  
Tenants, security badges, noise rules, smell complaints, elevator reservations. Ops excellence is the product.

**5. Crew scale and night work**  
Harder hiring, overtime, quality drift when everyone is tired. Supervision isn’t optional.

**6. GC payment chain risk**  
You’re often waiting on someone else’s draw. Lien rights and contract hygiene matter.

**7. Spec / scope fights**  
Underbid prep or coating system and you donate labor. Over-fight every RFI and you lose the next invite.

**8. Insurance, bonding, safety**  
Limits and EMR can gate which jobs you can even bid. Boring until it blocks a six-figure award.

**9. Confusing light-commercial “easy residential-adjacent” with institutional work**  
A retail refresh and a school bid package are not the same company. Know which book you’re building.

**10. Owner dependency on a few PM relationships**  
Lose two property managers and a “steady” book can go quiet. Diversify or stay close.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means commercial painting is a **real B2B project business**, not residential with a bigger ladder.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re comfortable with B2B sales, bidding, and longer relationship cycles  
- You can build (or hire) supervision for larger / off-hours crews  
- You’re capitalized for materials + AR lag, not just marketing  
- Facility downtime logistics energize you more than they exhaust you  
- You want project work with institutional or PM repeat potential

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want fast homeowner closes and weekend curb-appeal tickets as the whole book — see [residential painting](/models/residential-painting)  
- You hate paperwork, specs, and payment follow-up  
- You’re undercapitalized for retainage and slow AP  
- You want predictable weekly route revenue (janitorial-adjacent recurring is a different machine)  
- You want to *be* the painter on every job as the company

Franchise vs independent is a separate call.  
Same category either way: bid, crew, downtime window, collect, earn the next building.

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “offices look tired.”

You’re buying the right to run a **B2B project machine** that turns facility capital cycles, trust, and crew capacity into finished commercial spaces — if you can bid accurately, staff awkward hours, survive AR, and keep the punch list short.

Some people should build light-commercial off a residential book.  
Some people should go PM / multifamily-first.  
Some people should chase institutional work with bonding and paperwork muscle.  
Some people should walk — and put the same capital into a cleaner recurring B2B vehicle.

The question isn’t “do buildings need paint?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a stressful bid calendar that only works when the RFP awards, the night crew shows up, and someone finally cuts the check?**
