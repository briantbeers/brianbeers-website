Oil-stained slab.  
Hot-tire marks that never quite leave.  
A patio that looks like 1998 — and two contractors who already quoted tear-out and a new pour.

How long do you wait — and who shows up with a **real grind, moisture check, and chemistry**, not a big-box kit and a “we’ll be done by dinner” text?

There’s a lot of things you can put off.  
“The garage looks like a parking garage after winter” is not one of them when curb appeal, listing photos, or a home gym are on the line.

That’s **concrete / garage floor coatings**.

Not a paint aisle.  
Not “I’ll roll epoxy from Home Depot on Saturday.”  
Not pure **flooring install** as the whole company ([flooring install](/models/flooring-install) — product + labor on carpet / LVP / hardwood; different SKU and warranty story).  
Not pure **pressure washing** as the identity of the book ([pressure washing](/models/pressure-washing) — clean the slab; don’t transform it).

A **project sales + surface prep + resin system** company that sells garage floors, patio / pool-deck coatings, and light-commercial concrete finishes — and lives or dies on **whether the floor was prepped honestly before the flakes went down**.

High ticket when the square footage stacks.  
One-to-two day production when chemistry and weather cooperate.  
Warranty claims that show up months later when moisture, hot tires, or skipped grind come due.

Simple on a whiteboard: dirty / ugly concrete → coat → invoice → next lead.  
Brutal if you confuse “DIY kits exist” with “this scales itself.”

## What the business actually is

You’re a **concrete coating / resurfacing** company — and the category secretly contains related crafts that share a grinder but not the same sales pitch or failure mode:

1. **Garage floor coating systems** — full-broadcast flake (what homeowners call “epoxy floors”), often **polyurea / polyaspartic** hybrids marketed under the epoxy name  
2. **Decorative concrete / overlays** — stamp, stain, resurface patios, driveways, pool decks (transform-vs-replace; longer craft curve)  
3. **Interior / light-commercial floors** — basements, showrooms, warehouses, clinics, restaurants (density + documentation; different buyer)

Typical menu:

1. **Residential garage floors** — the marketing hook; 1–2 car / 3-car; flake, metallic, quartz tiers  
2. **Patio / porch / pool-deck coatings** — exterior UV and weather risk; different chemistry and windows  
3. **Basement / interior concrete** — winter hedge in cold markets; moisture drama lives here  
4. **Driveway / walkway systems** (when you take them) — scale + exterior exposure; not every garage crew should bid these  
5. **Light commercial** — auto shops, warehouses, clinics, restaurants, property managers (Brian’s concept notes flag hospitals / pharma / warehouse adjacency as higher-margin when you can fulfill)  
6. **Attach** — crack repair, joint fill, cove base, wall coatings, garage organization / slatwall / cabinets (system-dependent; scope creep risk)  
7. **Recoats / warranty service** — the quiet book that either builds trust or eats every Saturday

Typical loop:

1. **Lead** — Google / LSA, Facebook / Meta before-after ads, yard signs, home shows, realtor / GC / painter / garage-door referrals, past customers, brand call center (if franchised)  
2. **Estimate** — measure SF, photograph, judge slab condition, moisture risk, prior coatings, access, vehicle move-out, weather window  
3. **Propose / close** — good / better / best system tiers; timeline; deposit; warranty language *before* grind day  
4. **Prep** — diamond grind / shotblast, crack repair, dew-point / moisture discipline, dust control  
5. **Coat** — base → broadcast flake / metallic → scrape → topcoat; cure window; customer parking rules  
6. **Collect / review** — card on site; photos that sell the next neighbor  
7. **Stand behind it** — hot-tire, delamination, yellowing, and “it peels at the expansion joint” claims are the real brand

**Scope lock for this page:** branded (or independent) **concrete coating / garage floor / patio coating** companies built on **project tickets, prep quality, chemistry discipline, and warranty follow-through**. Decorative overlay / stamp systems and garage flake systems both live in the category — diligence which craft you’re buying.

**Out of scope (different models):**  
- Pure **flooring install** (carpet / LVP / hardwood replacement) ([flooring install](/models/flooring-install))  
- Pure **artificial turf / outdoor surfaces** project books ([artificial turf](/models/artificial-turf))  
- Pure **residential painting** ([residential painting](/models/residential-painting))  
- Pure **pressure washing / soft wash** without coating fulfillment ([pressure washing](/models/pressure-washing))  
- Pure **parking-lot striping / asphalt sealcoating** (Brian has covered EverLine-style parking maintenance separately — B2B lot contracts, different buyer and equipment)  
- Solo “I bought a kit and a Facebook ad” as the whole *company* story  
- Concrete *pouring* / flatwork GC as the identity (tear-out and new pour — different CapEx and risk)

**Chemistry honesty (say it once):**  
Homeowners say “epoxy.” Many modern pro books run **polyurea base + polyaspartic topcoat** (or hybrid systems) for cure speed, UV stability, and hot-tire resistance — and still market the *look* as epoxy flake. Decorative overlay systems are a cousin with stamps and stains. Diligence the **system**, not the Instagram label.

Named systems exist (garage-coating franchises, decorative-concrete concepts like Concrete Craft, independents, supplier-backed “dealer” programs, PE-curious consolidators). Treat them as **proof the category franchises and places** — Brian’s franchisor notes include coatings names in the home-based / ~$100k–$200k conversation — **not** as the point of this page.

## How money comes in

You sell **completed coating projects** — square footage of prepared concrete *plus* a resin system — not hours the homeowner sees on a timesheet, and not a free “I’ll coat your floor if you leave a review” hobby.

A single-car garage can land low-to-mid four figures.  
A two- / three-car flake package commonly sits mid-four.  
Patio + garage packages, metallics, and light-commercial SF climb mid-to-high four and into five figures.

Industry education (not Brian forecasts): many operators and public franchise chatter discuss residential garage work in roughly the **~$3k–$8k+** band depending on SF and tier; per-SF retail often framed roughly **~$5–$12+/SF** by system and metro (DIY kit pricing sits far below and is not your competitor’s cost structure). Brian’s concept notes’ **~$3k–$6k / one-day** framing is a useful *education* anchor — verify *your* market.

Revenue mix usually looks like some blend of:

| Channel | What it feels like |
|--------|---------------------|
| **Residential garage refresh** | Marketing + on-slab / in-home close. Before/after is the ad. |
| **Patio / outdoor living** | Weather windows; UV chemistry; HOA / neighbor visibility. |
| **Basement / interior** | Winter hedge; moisture testing is non-negotiable. |
| **Realtor / listing / flip** | Speed and photos; price-sensitive; repeat if you don’t peel in month three. |
| **GC / builder / garage-door / painter partner** | They bring the client; you stay niche on coatings. |
| **Light commercial / PM** | Bigger SF, insurance docs, night/weekend windows, thinner patience. |
| **Attach** | Crack repair, organization systems, wall coatings — small lines that save or kill jobs. |

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Close rate on qualified estimates** — system confidence + prep honesty, not “more leads” alone  
2. **Average ticket / SF mix** — garage-only vs patio package vs commercial SF vs metallic/quartz tier  
3. **Marketing CAC vs weather / season** — Facebook before-after and Google intent get loud; northern exterior books compress  
4. **Crew utilization** — billable coat days vs idle grinders, rain-outs, and “slab failed moisture” wasted trips  
5. **Materials discipline** — resin, flake, primers; waste and wrong chemistry kill margin fast  
6. **Prep honesty** — grind profile, crack repair, moisture mitigation are where amateurs die  
7. **Labor %** — skilled applicators (not “anyone with a roller”) and burdened cost  
8. **Warranty / rework reserve** — delamination and hot-tire claims are a product line whether you budget them or not  
9. **Cash terms** — deposit before grind day; don’t finance the customer’s garage on your Amex

Gross margin lives in **price − materials − labor − sales commission − warranty/rework**.  
Then overhead: van / trailer, grinders and dust collection, insurance, local marketing, PM/office, and (if franchised) royalty + brand fund + tech fees.

Industry shape (education only): healthy shops obsess over **estimate → sold → prepped → coated → collected → still bonded at month 12**, not vanity “pipeline” screenshots. A busy Facebook calendar with peeling floors is a review bomb with a truck payment attached.

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Housing stock ages. Garages become gyms, workshops, and showrooms. Oil and hot tires don’t stop. Outdoor living keeps selling “the patio should look finished.” Recoats and failed DIY kits create a second wave of demand.

When the economy is good, people upgrade flake, metallic, and patio packages.  
When the economy is soft, full outdoor living pauses first — **garage refresh, listing prep, and “make the slab look new without a pour”** often last longer than luxury custom hardscape.

That’s the demand story.

The constraint isn’t “concrete exists.”

It’s **trust + prep quality + chemistry discipline + warranty integrity** in *your* territory — on a **discretionary, visible, Instagram-friendly** purchase that still fails chemically when you skip the grind.

Unlike emergency plumbing, nobody’s garage “fails” at 11pm in a way that forces a same-week close every time.  
You still live in the gap between “we should do something about this floor” and “we signed, moved the cars, and stayed off it for 24 hours.”

## Micro: what actually drives one company

**Sales and fulfillment as one reputation**  
This isn’t storm canvass math. It’s consultative: measure, show samples, explain prep (not just flake color), set cure rules, get a deposit, protect the warranty language. Brian’s high-ticket home-services teaching applies: closing KPIs rhyme with paint / flooring / fencing; execution is chemistry and grinders.

**Prep is the product**  
The customer bought a photo. Your margin and your Google reviews live in surface profile, moisture testing, crack repair, and temperature / dew-point windows. Coat over dust, oil, or vapor drive and you buy a callback with interest.

**Moisture testing is not optional paperwork**  
Interior slabs and some garages fail quietly months later. Operators who skip meters to “save the sale” fund lawyers and chargebacks. Walking a bad slab is a skill.

**Chemistry / system choice**  
DIY epoxy kits, cheap “paint,” and pro polyurea / polyaspartic hybrids are not the same company. UV yellowing outdoors, hot-tire pickup, and cure windows separate Instagram from a business. Pick a supplier system, train it cold, and stop improvising brands mid-job.

**Weather & seasonality are co-owners**  
Exterior patio / driveway work needs temperature and humidity windows. Northern books that never build a garage / basement / commercial interior lane meet winter as a cash and crew crisis. Rain on grind day is a reschedule — not a “we’ll roll anyway” hero story.

**Sales vs install split**  
Selling outruns production fast when Meta ads work. Under-selling while you’re the only applicator caps the book at your two hands. Brian’s contractor staging applies: get a lead applicator early enough that estimates don’t die on the grinder.

**Easy-entry DIY kit noise**  
Big-box kits and weekend warriors set false price anchors. Your product is prep, insurance, dust control, warranty, and a floor that still looks right after summer tires. Racing kit pricing is how professionals go broke while Instagram still looks busy.

**Owner role (honest version)**  
Early stage: lead flow, estimates, prep QA, crew schedule, materials cash, reviews, warranty triage.  
Not “I bought a logo and went fishing.”  
Semi-absentee usually needs a mature multi-crew machine with real QA — not a greenfield hope.

**Systems**  
Estimating / SF tools, photo documentation, moisture logs, production calendar, warranty SOP, supplier ordering. Hero grinders don’t scale past their trailer calendar.

- What’s **garage vs patio vs basement vs commercial** mix last 24 months?  
- Median days from estimate → deposit → grind → coated → collected?  
- Moisture testing protocol — and walk-away rate on bad slabs?  
- Close rate by lead source (Google vs Meta vs referral vs partner)?

- Average residential ticket vs average commercial ticket?  
- Off-season plan if northern / wet — interiors, commercial, reserves?

- If buying a book: verified peel / claim history, equipment condition, and what’s *repeat referral* vs one-season Facebook fluff

What that means in plain English:

The *category* has durable, visible demand and can print attractive project margins when tickets are real, materials stay a fraction, and prep is honest.  
A *specific book* can look slammed every spring and still be broke — fat ad spend, skipped moisture tests, peeling warranties, exterior-only seasonality, or an owner who never got off the grinder.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

**1. Prep failures are the business**  
Skipped grind, contaminated slab, wrong profile, dust left in pores — the flake looks perfect on day one and peels on day ninety. Prep quality isn’t a step; it’s the company.

**2. Warranty claims arrive on a delay**  
Hot-tire pickup, delamination, yellowing, joint failure, “it bubbles after rain.” Lifetime language without reserves and documentation is marketing debt. You own the apology in the garage.

**3. Moisture testing (or the refusal to do it)**  
Vapor drive and damp slabs kill coatings slowly. Operators who don’t test (or who ignore the meter to save a sale) buy chargebacks. Walking away is a skill.

**4. Seasonality & weather are co-owners**  
Exterior patio / driveway windows compress in cold and wet markets. Humidity and dew point ruin cure days. Owners who annualize peak months meet winter (or monsoon season) as a cash and crew crisis.

**5. Sales vs install imbalance**  
Ads can fill a calendar fast. If grinders, applicators, and QA can’t keep up — or weather stacks the backlog — you bought churn and chargebacks with a trailer payment attached. Conversely, owner-only production caps revenue at your two hands.

**6. Easy-entry DIY kit competition**  
Home Depot / Amazon kits and weekend “epoxy guys” set false price anchors and flood Nextdoor with horror stories that stain the whole category. Trust, insurance, dust control, and a real warranty are the product — not racing kit pricing.

**7. Chemistry confusion / system shopping**  
Mixing brands mid-job, using interior epoxy outdoors, skipping primer, wrong topcoat for UV — callbacks with science attached. Train one system. Stop improvising.

**8. It’s a sales business wearing a respirator**  
If you can’t build a consultative close (or hire one) that explains prep and cure rules, the flake brochure doesn’t matter. Brian’s sales-heavy home-services anti-fit applies hard here.

**9. Crew scarcity and applicator skill**  
Decorative / coating application is niche. General laborers who can roll paint are not automatically grind-and-broadcast techs. Training is slower than a Meta campaign. Peak-season no-shows turn weather windows into missed revenue.

**10. Dust, access, and customer disruption**  
Cars must move. Dust collection matters indoors. Neighbors complain. Amateurs who “blow out the garage” create five-star enemies. Professional containment is cost — and differentiation.

**11. Underestimating materials + equipment reality**  
Diamond grinders, dust extractors, proper trailers, and resin inventory aren’t optional if you sell pro work. Under-equip and you only compete where the price war is worst.

**12. Commercial capability gap**  
Warehouses, clinics, and PMs pay when you have insurance, documentation, night windows, and floors that survive forklifts. Selling commercial you can’t fulfill creates reputation death faster than no marketing at all.

**13. Working capital on a “simple” flake story**  
Trailer note, insurance, launch ads, resin, and payroll burn while learning curves and rain hit. Undercapitalized launches stall when CAC and a warranty claim land the same month.

**14. Owner still on the grinder (or still the only closer)**  
If the company only works when you’re the estimator, the lead tech, and the Google-reply department, you own a job with a wrap — same staging truth as other project books.

**15. Attach-service distraction**  
Garage cabinets, organization, wall coatings, full patio rebuilds — profitable *when* you have process. Every new service is a new way to damage property and dilute the crew. Focus is a strategy.

**16. Franchise stack (if you go that route)**  
Royalty + brand fund + local ad minimums + tech fees + rules + territory vs playbook, supplier pricing, training, and peer network. Worth it only if the system fills calendars and trains prep — not because the wrap looks good on Instagram. Young systems can grow outlet counts fast; diligence Item 19 cohort quality and Item 21 franchisor health like an adult.

**17. Buying a tired peel book without claim math**  
Customer lists look like assets until you discover last year’s revenue was one-season Facebook garages and a warranty reserve of zero. Verify claim history and applicator retention — not “we’ve coated 2,000 floors historically.”

**18. Semi-absentee fantasy**  
Manager salary on a thin, seasonal, warranty-heavy book is how shops bleed. Semi-absentee usually needs a mature multi-crew machine with real QA — not a greenfield hope or one hot spring.

**19. Confusing “garages look ugly” with “easy money”**  
Timeless, visible demand is real. Your P&L still needs operators who can win the marketing war, the prep war, the weather war, and the DIY price war at the same time.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means concrete / garage floor coatings is a **real project-sales + prep + chemistry business** where **easy-entry kits create permanent noise** — and scale only shows up when prep quality, warranty discipline, and sales/install balance actually work.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re wired to sell *and* fulfill — estimates, crews, and grind-day quality control  
- You’re willing to obsess over prep and moisture, not just flake color  
- Marketing CAC puzzles don’t scare you (this category won’t let you ignore them)  
- Project home services (same energy as flooring / painting / turf) excite you more than weekly route hours  
- You’re capitalized for equipment, launch ads, weather gaps, and warranty reserves  
- If northern / wet: you can stomach (and plan for) off-season via interiors / commercial  
- Truck-by-truck / crew-by-crew scale in a loud DIY market sounds like a fight worth picking

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want passive / semi-absentee from month one  
- You refuse to market hard or recruit people who work in dust and chemicals  
- You’re allergic to moisture meters, dew-point charts, and “we have to walk this slab” conversations  
- You thought a big-box kit *was* the business plan  
- You’re undercapitalized for real grinders, insurance, CAC, and warranty reserves  
- You need predictable weekly recurring route revenue to sleep (pest, lawn treatment, senior care hours)  
- You’re looking for a craft job with a logo (you *being* the only applicator forever)  
- You’re actually shopping pure parking-lot striping / asphalt maintenance — related coatings word, different buyer and model

Franchise vs independent is a separate call.  
Same category either way: generate leads → estimate the slab honestly → prep like the warranty depends on it → coat → collect → stand behind the floor when summer tires hit.

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “garages look bad.”

You’re buying the right to run a **project machine** that turns ugly concrete into finished floors — if you can win trust against DIY noise, prep slabs honestly, balance sales with install capacity, survive weather and warranty, and keep chemistry from becoming a lawsuit.

Some people should start residential garage-heavy, master one system, and prove they can acquire jobs profitably before adding patio / commercial lanes.  
Some people should buy a tired local book (or a franchise resale) and install prep QA + warranty discipline — consolidators and franchise systems chase this category because the before/after demand is obvious.  
Some people should niche (commercial interiors, builder packages, patio-only decorative) once the core engine works.  
Some people should walk — and put the same capital into a vehicle with cleaner recurring economics, less warranty surface area, or less permanent kit-competitor noise.

The question isn’t “do garages look ugly?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a stressful coating company that only works when you’re the closer, the moisture meter, the lead applicator, and the warranty department?**

If you want help comparing doors (this model vs flooring install vs artificial turf vs exterior painting vs pressure washing vs others), that’s a conversation.
