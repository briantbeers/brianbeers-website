Both parents work.  
The infant can’t sit in a cubicle.  
Grandma is three states away — or already maxed.  
A nanny is $25–$35+/hour before overtime, taxes, and “what if she’s sick.”

Someone needs a **licensed room** with eyes on the baby all day — meals, naps, a playground that passes inspection — and a director who answers when the state walks in.

That’s a **daycare / preschool** center.

Not a kids clothing resale store (different page).  
Not after-school tutoring / learning center (different machine — enrollment packs, not infant ratios).  
Not adult day (same *facility + ratio + occupancy* brain, different license, different ages, different culture).  
Not a home daycare in a living room (related idea, much smaller capacity, different zoning and liability stack).

You’re running a **licensed early childhood facility**: classrooms by age band, mandated staff-to-child ratios, outdoor play, fire/life safety, background checks, and a census that either fills seats — or burns payroll on empty cribs.

Simple on a whiteboard.  
Hard in real life — because licensing timelines, infant ratios, teacher turnover, buildout CapEx, and the gap between a **waitlist PDF** and **paid occupied seats** show up before the model looks “demographic.”

Premium early-ed brands (Goddard / Primrose / Kiddie Academy–class systems) and indie centers sell the same physics. The logo is optional. The license math is not.

## What the business actually is

You’re a **local licensed childcare / preschool operator**: purpose-fit (or heavily TI’d) space, age-banded classrooms, ratio staffing, curriculum or play-based program, meals/snacks, outdoor play, and usually full-day care for working families — sometimes half-day preschool only, sometimes both.

**Two product shapes (don’t blur them):**

| | **Full-day daycare / childcare center** | **Preschool / early education program** |
|--|----------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------|
| **What you sell** | Full-day care (often 7a–6p), meals, naps, year-round for working parents | Structured school-readiness / play-based learning; may be half-day or school-year |
| **Who must be on site** | Director (credentialed per state) + ratio teachers/aides by age room | Same license stack in most states when care is provided; “school” branding doesn’t skip childcare rules |
| **Typical payer stack** | Private tuition + (often) subsidy / voucher seats; employer-assisted | More private tuition; some Pre-K / public partnership seats |
| **License feel** | Childcare license + fire/health; infant rooms drive the hardest ratios | Same facility rules if you’re licensed childcare; pure private school paths exist in some states — don’t assume |

Industry education puts the U.S. at a chronic **slot shortage** story: millions of children under 5, far fewer licensed slots than demand in many metros. That is the macro. Your P&L is still **licensed capacity × paid occupancy × blended tuition − ratio labor − facility − insurance**.

Typical service menu:

1. **Infant rooms** — cribs, bottle/feeding protocols, diapering, sleep rules, **tightest ratios**  
2. **Toddler rooms** — mobility, bites, language explosion, still ratio-heavy  
3. **Preschool / Pre-K** — curriculum, school readiness, looser ratios (still regulated)  
4. **School-age / before-after** (if licensed for it) — afternoon density, bus logistics  
5. **Meals / snacks** — kitchen or catering; allergy protocols; sometimes CACFP  
6. **Outdoor play** — fenced yard, fall surfacing, shade; square-footage rules  
7. **Parent communication** — apps, incident reports, tuition billing, “can you keep him until 6:15?”

Typical loop:

1. **Lead** — Google / tours, employer HR, pediatrician, realtor “new subdivision,” waitlist referrals, Facebook parent groups  
2. **Tour / enroll** — age fit, schedule days, deposit, handbook, immunization / forms  
3. **Classroom placement** — ratio seat available *that week*, not “someday on the waitlist”  
4. **Daily ops** — ratios held every hour; meals; naps; incidents logged; parents at pickup  
5. **Bill / collect** — weekly or monthly tuition; subsidy reimbursement lag if mixed  
6. **Retain / age-up** — infant → toddler → preschool transitions; sibling enrollments; summer softness

**Scope lock:** this page is the **center-based licensed facility** — classrooms, ratios, playground, license capacity.  
It is **not** adult day / companion day centers ([adult-day-centers](/models/adult-day-centers)) — same occupancy brain, different regs and culture.  
It is **not** kids clothing resale ([kids-clothing-resale](/models/kids-clothing-resale)).  
It is **not** tutoring / learning centers (separate backlog model — teacher packs and seasonality, not infant ratio law).  
It is **not** Montessori / specialty early-ed brands as a *separate* accreditation thesis (adjacent CapEx; own page someday).  
It is **not** pet daycare (different animal, different insurance — don’t giggle that into your diligence).

## How money comes in

You sell **enrolled child-weeks** (or months) of care — filled classroom seats that show up and pay — not billable home-care hours and not overnight residential beds.

Public cost-of-care education bands (national center-based averages, 2026-style writeups): roughly **~$1,230/month infants**, **~$1,080 toddlers**, **~$920 preschool**, with state spreads from roughly **~$650** to **$2,400+/month** depending on metro, ratios, and wages. Premium suburban early-ed centers often price *above* those medians. Subsidy / voucher rates can sit under private sticker — mix matters.

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Licensed capacity by age room** — infant seats are few and expensive to staff; preschool seats are denser  
2. **Paid occupancy** — enrolled *and* attending (or still billed under your policy) vs empty ratio slots  
3. **Age mix** — infant-heavy books raise labor %; preschool-heavy books raise density  
4. **Tuition × schedule** — 5-day full-time vs 3-day part-time changes revenue per classroom  
5. **Registration / supply / late-pickup fees** — real but secondary to tuition  
6. **Subsidy / employer / Pre-K partnership seats** — fill census; watch rate vs cost and paperwork lag  
7. **Summer / holiday density** — school-year preschool softens; full-day centers feel August differently

Gross margin intuition (education shape from operator / franchise guides — **not** a forecast):

- Labor is the monster (often discussed **~45–60%+** of revenue when ratios are real and wages aren’t fantasy)  
- Food, supplies, insurance, curriculum sit under that  
- Facility lease / debt service is fixed whether the infant room has 4 or 8 paid seats  
- Guides commonly cite roughly **~80–90%+** paid occupancy of *staffable* capacity as the healthy conversation zone; soft books with full ratio coverage feel “busy” and still lose money  
- **Waitlist ≠ occupancy** — a 40-name waitlist for infant rooms while preschool has empty seats is a different business than a full paid census

Cash reality: private tuition on autopay can be clean. **Subsidy reimbursement** and partnership seats can lag. Undercapitalized owners confuse “we’re licensed” with “the infant room is full *and* collecting.”

**Light contrast:** adult day sells **participant-days × per diem** with Medicaid/VA mix ([adult-day-centers](/models/adult-day-centers)). Daycare sells **child-months × tuition** with private-pay + subsidy mix. Same facility occupancy anxiety. Different regulators, different nap rooms, different lawsuit stories.

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Dual-income households are normal. Extended family is less available. Infant and toddler care is still a **physical presence** product — Zoom doesn’t watch a crawler.

Demand sits with **working parents** who need reliable full-day cover so careers don’t collapse at the first ear infection. Policymakers talk supply shortages; employers talk retention; parents talk waitlists.

Demand is not the scarce resource in many metros.

**Licensed capacity you can *build*, teachers you can *ratio*, and seats you can *fill at a tuition that covers wages* are.**

Policy moves the goalposts: state ratio tables, director credential rules, background-check systems, Pre-K expansion (partner or competitor), subsidy rate freezes, zoning that kills playgrounds. Same story as other regulated facility verticals — demography / dual-income is the backdrop; your P&L is local execution.

## Micro: what actually drives one center

**License capacity & square footage**  
States typically set max children off **indoor usable** space (education bands often roughly **~35 sq ft/child** indoors, plus outdoor play requirements often discussed around **~75 sq ft/child** — *verify your state*). Infant rooms need more gear and often stricter space. You can’t “market your way” past a 72-child license without TI + amendment + outdoor math.

**Staffing ratios (the silent CapEx)**  
Common education bands (state tables vary hard): roughly **1:3–1:4 infants** (some states allow 1:5–1:6), **1:4–1:6 toddlers**, **1:8–1:12 preschool**. NAEYC-style quality targets are often tighter than legal minimums. Ratio staff + **director** (often *not* counted the way you wish) means labor is semi-fixed by room. Empty seats in an open infant room still need coverage if any infants are present. Call-outs = compliance event, not just a schedule headache.

**Occupancy vs waitlist discipline**  
North-star isn’t “how long is the waitlist PDF.” It’s **paid enrolled seats vs staffable licensed capacity by classroom**, no-show / vacation policy, and age-up transitions. A waitlist of 60 for infants while you can’t staff Tuesday’s toddler room is a trap, not a trophy.

**Age-mix management**  
Infants drive tuition *and* labor. Preschool drives density. Centers that oversell infants without teacher pipeline drown. Centers that avoid infants to “keep labor easy” compete in the more crowded preschool band.

**Facility CapEx / lease**  
Child-height fixtures, diapering stations, egress, fire alarms, sprinklers, commercial kitchen or approved catering, **fenced outdoor play with fall surfacing**, parking for peak pickup. The “cheap strip-center” fantasy dies when the playground and infant-room sink requirements show up. Purpose-built or heavy TI is normal for scaled centers.

Industry / FDD education bands for **premium franchise early-ed** build-to-suit styles commonly span roughly **high-six to multi-million** all-in (leasehold TI vs land+building swings Item 7 wildly — public 2025–2026 FDD writeups for Goddard/Primrose-class systems often show roughly **~$0.7M–$1.5M+** lease scenarios and much higher when land/building is in). Indie conversions can be lower — or become money pits after licensing punch lists. Verify *your* state, landlord, and architect who has licensed childcare before.

**Teacher recruiting & retention**  
Same labor war as other care businesses — plus early-childhood credentials, CPR, ratios that forbid “just send one aide home,” and parents who interview you harder than a corporate recruiter. Turnover resets trust and training; a bad week of call-outs closes rooms (lost revenue) or breaks ratio (lost license risk).

**Liability & trust**  
Injury, allegation, missing child drill failure, allergen exposure, sleep-position noncompliance. One viral story empties a waitlist that took two years to build. Insurance is not a vibe; it’s a P&L line and a diligence item.

**Owner role (honest version)**  
Early stage: you live in licensing binders, classroom coverage boards, tuition delinquencies, and “the lead teacher quit.” Semi-absentee fairy tales collide with ratio law and parent Slack. Earn a real director bench before you disappear.

Brian’s pad-site point still applies as **site selection intuition**: early-ed centers show up where new housing and dual-income density are landing. That helps **demand**. It does not staff your infant room.

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

Education shape only — **not** a forecast for your market or your deal.

**Tuition benchmarks (public cost-of-care / guide bands):**

- National center-based education averages (2026-style): infants **~$1,230/mo**, toddlers **~$1,080**, preschool **~$920** (wide state/metro spread)  
- Premium early-ed private tuition in strong suburbs often discussed **higher** (operator/franchise education sometimes cites roughly **~$1,650–$2,400+/mo** by metro and age)  
- Subsidy / voucher reimbursement: often below private sticker — mix and paperwork lag matter

**Occupancy math (whiteboard, not a promise):**

- Licensed capacity 120 → **~85%** paid occupancy ≈ **~102** enrolled as a common *healthy* conversation starter (ramp centers live far below that for 12–24 months)  
- Illustrative: 90 enrolled × **$1,400** blended tuition × 12 ≈ **~$1.5M** revenue *shape* before ratio labor, food, lease, insurance, curriculum, bad debt  
- Soft-open reality from franchise/operator education: opening at **~40–60** enrolled against **140–180** licensed capacity is common talk — carrying full facility cost on half a census is the cash trap  
- Labor guides often discuss targeting roughly **~45–55%** of revenue on wages/benefits in healthy mature books — miss that and “full” still bleeds

**Startup / runway (industry education bands):**

- Home-based: commonly discussed **~$5k–$50k** (different model, different ceiling)  
- Small/medium indie center conversion: often **~$30k–$150k+** *before* heavy TI surprises; larger 50+ centers and full commercial TI commonly **$250k–$500k+**  
- Premium purpose-built / franchise early-ed: public Item 7 education often **~$0.7M–$1.5M+** leasehold paths; land+build paths much higher  
- Budget **months of payroll + lease** before census stabilizes; 12–24 month ramps appear repeatedly in category education  
- Contrast: Brian’s lighter senior / service doors (office + labor float) are a different check size than a licensed childcare plant

**Brian-adjacent notes (for stack context, not daycare P&Ls):**

- Goddard named on development walks as **early education demand proof** next to new housing/retail — site/market signal, not disclosed unit economics  
- Franchise idea sheet lists Primrose / Kiddie Academy / Goddard as **category examples** only

Your diligence questions should sound like:

- What’s licensed capacity **by age room** vs trailing **paid enrollment** (not waitlist length)?  
- Infant / toddler / preschool mix % and tuition by room?  
- Actual staff-to-child ratios by hour (not the handbook ideal)?  
- Teacher vacancy rate, credential mix, agency/sub % of labor?  
- Subsidy / voucher % of census and days-sales-outstanding on reimbursement?  
- Last licensing visit findings and open corrections?  
- Playground / outdoor sq ft vs license; what fails if you amend capacity?  
- Lease remaining term, TI amortization, personal guarantee, and exit if census stalls?  
- Incident / insurance claims history (redacted) — pattern or one-offs?  
- If franchised: does the system shorten licensing, hiring, and ramp — or only a lobby mural and a royalty?

Same brain you’d use buying any regulated facility business: seats, labor law, cash conversion — and whether *you* want that responsibility.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

This section is the point. Soft marketing skips it. Operators live it.

**1. Licensing & inspection burden**  
State childcare license, fire/health, director credentials, background checks, policy manuals, renewals. “We’ll tidy the binder later” is how openings slip six to twelve months.

**2. Staff-to-child ratios as a hard constraint**  
Hit call-outs or acuity wrong and you’re over ratio (illegal) or closing a room (revenue death). Labor *is* the product *and* the regulation.

**3. Occupancy — empty seats with full classroom staffing**  
You can’t send half a teacher home because two infants withdrew. Fixed ratio coverage meets soft census = quiet death.

**4. Waitlist theater**  
Long waitlists feel like product-market fit. Unconverted names, wrong age bands, and “maybe in September” don’t pay August rent.

**5. Teacher recruiting & wage pressure**  
Early childhood wages compete with retail and Amazon — while parents expect college-trained magic. Turnover resets ratio compliance and parent trust.

**6. Infant room economics**  
Highest tuition, tightest ratios, most regulatory landmines (sleep, feeding, SIDS protocols). Fill wrong and you buy beautiful losses.

**7. Facility CapEx & landlord risk**  
Playground, sinks, egress, sprinklers, square-footage math. Lease too short for TI payback. HVAC failure in July closes the only plant you have.

**8. Liability & reputation**  
Injury, allegation, lost-child drill, allergen event. One incident can torch years of waitlist equity. Insurance deductibles and attorney hours are real.

**9. Cash timing & delinquency**  
Payroll weekly/biweekly; some families pay late; subsidy reimbursements lag. Private-pay autopay softens it; mixed books punish thin balance sheets.

**10. Age-up & seasonal soft spots**  
May graduates leave; September scramble; summer camp mix if you offer it. Roster stability is a myth without a pipeline.

**11. Parent dynamics & communication**  
Guilt, custody schedules, “why does she have a bruise,” unpaid invoices, after-hours Slack. You’re in the emotion business with a facility shell.

**12. Food service & allergies**  
Health permits, cross-contact protocols, CACFP paperwork if you chase it. Not a side hustle for the floating aide.

**13. Competition & substitutes**  
Other centers, home daycares, nannies, family care, employer on-site, public Pre-K expansion. Differentiation is safety + reliability + teacher stability — not pastel logos.

**14. Owner intensity + key-person risk**  
Lose the director or lead infant teacher and the license stack wobbles. Absentee ownership fantasies collide with ratio law and 6:15 pickup.

**15. Franchise / brand fee drag (if any)**  
Royalties and brand funds only help if they shorten ramp, hiring, and licensing. A logo does not staff Tuesday’s toddler room.

**16. Confusing “childcare shortage” headlines with “your filled, collectible seats”**  
Same trap as adult day’s “Silver Tsunami” vs operable census. Demand ≠ staffable paid occupancy.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means daycare / preschool is a **regulated facility + staffing business** where ratio law and occupancy math decide whether the mission survives contact with payroll.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re willing to own a **physical licensed site** — inspections, playgrounds, landlords — not just a CRM  
- You can recruit and retain early-childhood teachers *and* a real director (or partner with someone who can)  
- Occupancy dashboards and ratio compliance energize you  
- You’re capitalized for TI + a soft-open ramp that can run **12–24 months**  
- Serving working families’ kids feels like meaningful work, not a marketing slogan  
- You’ve compared this door to lighter models (tutoring packs, kids resale, service businesses) and still want the facility stack

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You wanted Brian’s **lower-overhead** doors and thought “daycare” was the same lightness with more heart  
- You hate licensing, inspections, or parent conflict  
- You need passive income or a semi-absentee story from day one  
- Thin stomach for infant care risk, allegations, or custody drama  
- You’re undercapitalized for playground-grade TI + months of payroll before census  
- You’re actually shopping **adult day**, **tutoring**, or **kids resale** — different pages  
- You’re shopping **home daycare** capacity and calling it a center — different ceiling, different rules

Franchise vs independent is a separate call.  
Same physics either way: seats, ratios, tuition, facility. A brand (Goddard / Primrose / peers) is only worth the fee if it shortens licensing, staffing, and ramp — not because the lobby smells like crayons.

Many operators will correctly choose a **lighter** kids-adjacent or service business first and never buy a licensed center. That’s a valid read, not a lack of ambition.

## Risks (the short list)

- Soft occupancy + fixed ratios → structural losses  
- Wage inflation without tuition room → busy and broke  
- Failed inspection / license action → existential  
- Injury or allegation → liability + enrollment death spiral  
- Infant-room overbuild vs teacher pipeline → beautiful empty cribs  
- Subsidy mix / rate freezes → margin compression  
- Lease / TI mismatch → trapped capital  
- Key director / lead teacher departure → compliance and census risk  
- Waitlist vanity without conversion → false confidence  
- Franchise/affiliate stack (if any) → fees must buy real ramp leverage  
- Confusing shortage headlines with your collectible child-months

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “parents need childcare.”

You’re buying the right to fill **licensed classroom seats** — if you can pass inspections, hold ratios, recruit teachers, price tuition to wages, and survive the ramp without breaking payroll — while giving working families a real workday of care.

Some people should start (or buy) a **smaller** kids-adjacent door and stay asset-light on facilities.  
Some people should buy a tired center and fix census + staffing + tuition mix.  
Some people should build or franchise a premium early-ed plant where demographics and capital support it.  
Some people should walk — and put the capital into adult day, a trade, resale, or a different vehicle entirely.

The question isn’t “do dual-income parents need daycare?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a regulated facility job with ratio anxiety, CapEx gravity, and higher stakes when the lead infant teacher texts “I’m not coming in”?**

If you want help comparing doors (this model vs adult day, kids resale, tutoring when that page exists, or something outside child services), that’s a conversation.  
Not a pitch deck.
