Flat tire on the shoulder.  
Dead battery in a parking deck.  
Rollover on the turnpike at 2 a.m.

Somebody is getting paid to show up.

Here’s the part outsiders miss:

A real **towing / roadside / recovery** company is often **not** “buy one truck and print.”

It’s a **dispatch density × truck utilization × channel mix × 24/7 labor × insurance** business.

Flatbeds that hold value — and blow hydraulic lines on a Tuesday.  
Motor-club rates that fill the radio and starve the margin.  
Police rotation and impound yards that take years of relationships (and storage regs) to win.  
Cash calls that pay better when Google actually rings.

Same category that Brian publicly framed as a **~$60B** U.S. industry with “unlimited” breakdown demand — *and* that he shut down after ~**8 months** as Rocky’s Towing when trucks, insurance, and focus refused to cooperate.

Simple on a whiteboard: car breaks → truck hooks → drop → invoice.  
Brutal if you confuse “people need tows” with “this runs itself next to my other business.”

---

## What the business actually is

You’re running a **mobile recovery + roadside** operation that turns stranded / damaged / relocated vehicles into completed tow and service tickets — on a clock the customer (or the club, or the cop) sets.

Typical loop:

1. **Call / dispatch** — Google / maps, motor-club app, police rotation, property-management contract, shop/dealer wholesale, prior customer  
2. **Accept / ETA** — truck class available? driver awake? response-time SLA?  
3. **Arrive / secure** — cones, winch, dollies, flatbed or wheel-lift; scene safety on live roads  
4. **Transport / drop** — home, shop, dealer, auction, impound yard  
5. **Collect / document** — cash/card, club invoice (slow pay), municipal ticket, storage start clock  
6. **Truck back online** — fuel, wash, PM, next ping — or dead truck = dead revenue

**Scope lock for this page:** light-duty and heavy-duty **towing / roadside / recovery** — owned or leased trucks, dispatch (owner, manager, or 24/7 board), mix of **cash calls**, **motor-club / insurance roadside**, **police / municipal rotation**, optional **private-property / impound**, optional **fleet / commercial** accounts.

**Out of scope (different models):**  
- Pure **auto repair** shop economics (sibling page — Brian’s book)  
- Pure **quick lube** throughput (sibling page)  
- Dealership internal lot shuttles as the whole company  
- Pure **repo / collateral recovery** as the identity of the book (license-heavy, violence/risk profile — different diligence)  
- National motor-club *corporate* seats (AAA / Agero-class networks) as the business you buy — those are **channels**, not the operating company

Brian’s lived frame (documented on camera across the Rocky’s arc):

- Thesis: every broken car ends at a shop — own the tow, **get paid to acquire** the repair customer for his multi-unit branded shops  
- Reality: volume + 24/7 + club/police lock-in + truck uptime beat spreadsheet synergy  
- Public close: experiment lost money, fed some shop work, then **shiny-object** energy went back into the bays (“diamonds under your feet”)

Named systems / partners exist as **category proof**, not ads:

- **Motor-club / roadside networks** Brian named out loud: AAA, Allstate, Geico-class insurance roadside (plus the broader Agero / regional-club world operators chase)  
- **Local operators** as proof the craft scales with branding, heavy iron, and contracts (his on-camera sit-down with a large Philly operator — catchy 24/7 brand, named heavy trucks, corporate fleet + property work)  
- **250 Franchise Ideas** sheet: automotive rows are **repair / oil change / transmission / tire / glass** — **no towing franchise row**. Treat towing as largely **independent / asset-operator** territory with network contracts, not a Valvoline-style FDD prototype

Format fork you must name in diligence:

| Format | Feel |
|--------|------|
| **Light-duty flatbed** | Passenger cars / light trucks; most consumer + club volume; CapEx entry point; city maneuverability tradeoffs on big beds |
| **Light / medium wrecker (wheel-lift / hook)** | Tight streets, quick hooks, some private-property work; different skill + liability than a bed |
| **Heavy-duty / recovery** | Tractors, buses, pileups; six-figure+ truck CapEx; scarce operators; event economics that look nothing like a $125 hookup |
| **Roadside-only attach** | Lockouts, jumps, tire changes, fuel — often motor-club filler; thin alone, useful density on the same radio |
| **Impound / storage yard** | Rotation + non-consent + daily storage; facility, fencing, cameras, lien/regs — a second business glued to the trucks |

You’re not buying “cars break down.”  
You’re buying the right to keep **iron on the road** while someone else’s emergency becomes your utilization.

## How money comes in

You sell **completed responses**, not “hours on a timesheet” the stranded driver sees.

A call hits the board.  
You sell **truck + driver minutes** (and sometimes storage, winching, dollies, after-hours premiums).  
Unlike repair’s parts+labor ticket story, towing is often **rate card × distance × scene complexity × channel** — with **fuel, insurance, and downtime** as the silent COGS.

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Calls accepted per truck per day** — utilization, not “we got a lot of rings”  
2. **Average ticket by channel** — cash retail vs club flat rate vs rotation ordinance vs heavy recovery  
3. **Radius / deadhead** — Google Ads that send you 40 minutes across the metro murder fuel and clock  
4. **After-hours mix** — nights/weekends/weather pay; they also burn drivers and trucks  
5. **Channel mix** — cash / club / police / property / fleet / shop-wholesale  
6. **Storage attach** (if you have a legal yard) — days on lot can dwarf the tow — *if* regs and lien process don’t eat you  
7. **Uptime** — a truck with a blown hydraulic line is a logo on a driveway  
8. **Driver coverage** — 24/7 promise without a second shift is a marketing lie

Gross profit lives in **ticket − fuel − burdened driver − payment/club fees (− scene extras COGS)**.  
Then overhead: truck debt/lease, **insurance that doesn’t care about your vibes**, dispatch software, lot/yard, uniforms, marketing, repairs to the recovery equipment itself, and working capital for slow club/municipal pay.

Revenue mix usually looks like some blend of:

| Channel | What it feels like |
|---------|-------------------|
| **Cash / retail calls** | Best rate card when you can win Google/maps trust; CAC and radius discipline required |
| **Motor club / insurance roadside** | Volume + SLA pressure; thin contracted rates; slow pay common; backup-tier until you prove response |
| **Police / municipal rotation** | Relationship + equipment + yard + response rules; often ordinance rates; storage can be the real money |
| **Private property / HOA / apartment** | Steady if contracted; reputational and regulatory minefield (non-consensual rules vary hard by state/city) |
| **Fleet / corporate / dealer / auction** | Density and predictability when you win the account; price pressure; service-level politics |
| **Shop / wholesale** | Other repair/collision/dealerships who need moves — volume without consumer marketing |
| **Heavy / specialty recovery** | Sparse, high ticket, capital and skill moat — not a light-duty add-on you invent on a Saturday |

You’re a **logistics company wearing a light bar**.  
Not a repair shop. Not a passive “synergy bolt-on.”

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Vehicles fail in place. Accidents happen. Cities enforce. Clubs sell memberships that promise a truck.

Aging light-vehicle parc + miles driven + DIY decline on modern cars keep **breakdown and accident volume** in the water table — boom or soft landing. Weather spikes (ice, storms) are real demand pulses. EVs still get flat tires, still crash, still need flatbeds (sometimes *more* carefully) — they don’t erase the category the way they threaten oil-change counts.

When the economy is good, people still break down (and still crash).  
When the economy is bad, they **must** move the dead car off the road — and clubs still dispatch.

That’s the “essential / boring / 24/7” pitch adjacent to Brian’s auto essential-services framing — with a sharper operator asterisk: **demand is not the scarce resource**.

Brian’s on-camera constraint statement (launch docs): the issue isn’t creating demand; it’s **execution** — trucks, drivers, scheduling, maintenance, and the patience to earn club/police density.

Dense metros generate endless pings — and endless deadhead if you chase every Google click.  
Rural corridors can print on rotation + being the only serious iron for miles — if you can staff nights.  
Same category. Different operating reality.

Consolidators and large regional fleets chase density for a reason: shared dispatch, yard infrastructure, and contract desks beat one lonely flatbed that only works when the owner is awake — *when* utilization clears insurance and debt.

## Micro: what actually drives one company

**Truck class / CapEx (say it first)**  
Brian’s teaching and buying footage lives here: used light-duty commercial chassis with beds (Dodge **5500** / Ford **F550**-class in his buys), diesel preference for longevity, plan to hold a few years and resell (strong residual narrative — banks finance them for a reason). His documented pair: roughly **~$72k** all-in each, **~$152k** for two, with **100% financing** available in *his* credit story and bonus-depreciation tax framing called out on camera. Industry education bands sit wider: new light flatbeds often discussed roughly **~$58k–$150k+**; used clean beds commonly **~$60k–$120k**; **heavy wreckers** jump into **mid–high six figures** new. Wrong class for your streets (bed too long for Philly-narrow) or wrong class for your contracts (no heavy when the turnpike pays heavy) is a silent strategy error.

**Flatbed vs wrecker**  
Brian’s operator note: big beds handle most passenger work but struggle in tight city streets; classic **wrecker / hook** wins maneuverability. Heavy boom trucks are a different company.

**Insurance**  
His live numbers: on the order of **~$16k–$20k per truck per year** in auto/liability class premiums during the experiment — before you romanticize “one truck side hustle.” Scene risk (highway, weather, night) is why underwriters charge like that.

**Dispatch density + radius**  
One truck at 50% “turning work down” still isn’t a company. Multiple trucks cut deadhead *if* the map is tight. His Google-Ads-heavy phase sent trucks “all over,” burning fuel and hours — mathematically ugly with a small fleet on a vast area.

**Drivers + 24/7**  
Volume business. Low contribution per light tow means you need **many** completed calls — and coverage when clubs and cops call at 1 a.m. Driver quality (scene safety, reviews, not wrecking the bed) is the product.

**Channel access timeline**  
Cash/Google can start fast (he ranked #1 in a local pack for a while). **Motor clubs** and **police rotations** are relationship + paperwork + time — incumbents are “locked in for years.” He eventually got AAA as a **second-tier** option and still killed the line of business as a distraction. Spreadsheet assumption that clubs appear in month two is how you fund a loss.

**Fuel + maintenance as operating tax**  
Ride-along teaching: fuel discussed around **~10–15% of revenue** in their early ops chatter; gas truck ~**5 mpg**, diesel ~**7** in that anecdote — ugly either way when you’re deadheading. Hydraulic lines, radiators, brakes, transfer cases — recovery equipment fails in ways retail passenger shops aren’t set up to treat as a **fleet PM system**. Brian’s later lesson: retail repair process ≠ fleet checklist culture even when you “own shops.”

**Uptime math**  
At one point in the experiment they effectively had **one truck on the road** while others waited on parts/vendors — with dispatcher and manager payroll still running. Fixed people cost + idle iron is how volume businesses die quietly.

**Optional yard / impound**  
Rotation and property programs often want storage. Fencing, lighting, cameras, lien timelines, angry customers — high-margin *if* you operationalize compliance. Skip non-consensual/repo if you won’t underwrite the risk; operators Brian filmed still noted “a lot of money” lives on that side for people who will.

**Owner role (honest version)**  
Early: buying/financing trucks, insurance, hiring drivers, living on the radio, mopping hydraulic fluid at a dealership when a line blows on camera.  
At scale: dispatch board, contract desk (clubs, PDs, fleets), PM program, night shift bench — still not passive. Semi-absentee fantasies die on the first simultaneous busy night and broken bed.

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

### Illustrative unit math (Brian experiment + category education — not a forecast)

From the public Rocky’s close-out teaching (education only):

| Lever | Illustrative shape |
|-------|--------------------|
| Fleet in the experiment | Started **1** truck → grew toward a handful (≈**3** in the volume narrative) |
| Used light flatbed CapEx | ~**$72k** all-in each; **~$152k** for a documented two-truck buy |
| Insurance | ~**$16k–$20k**/truck/year called out on camera |
| Shop-feed during ~8 months | ~**$150k** repair work into his shops; ~**$60k** net after COGS/payroll/royalty *on that repair* |
| Towing-side loss (same window) | ~**$120k** class (drivers, manager, ads, repairs, insurance) |
| Net experiment result | ~**$50k–$60k** loss after netting shop feed — “not insignificant,” sized as a test against a ~**$50M** repair book |
| Early monthly anecdote | ~**$20k** tow work in a month / ~**$15k** repair feed in another month — shape, not a run-rate |
| Hookup price example | ~**$125** basic / ~**$85** discounted if dropped to his shop |
| Fuel | ~**10–15%** of revenue in ride-along chatter |
| Hydraulic hose PM example | Parts ~**$147** + labor → ~**$800** class to keep a bed online |

Thesis spreadsheet he later mocked with reality: **8 tows/day** × half converted to shop × ~**$1,000** ARO → fantasy thousands/day per truck → “millions” of repair feed. Actual: ads cost more, police nearly impossible short-term, insurance work hard, **conversion % and ticket** lower than the dream.

### Category CapEx / rate shape (education — verify locally)

Public ops / startup guides (order-of-magnitude only; markets and years move):

- **Light-duty** entry: often framed roughly **~$50k–$150k** equipment for a single-truck launch story; **two trucks + real yard** jumps hard (**~$120k–$250k+** class all-in chatter, higher with fenced storage)  
- **Heavy-duty** iron: commonly **~$250k–$650k+** new for serious recovery trucks — different balance sheet  
- **Motor-club light tow** contracted rates often discussed far below retail cash (club **~$40–$65** vs private **~$100–$200+** bands appear in startup guides — *your* metro card rules)  
- **Police rotation**: ordinance rates + **storage $/day**; incumbents may sit on **30–50%** revenue mix in mature books — and **6–18 months** (or years) to earn a slot  
- Industry EBIT chatter after real overhead often lands much thinner than “gross on a cash call” Instagram math

### Diligence questions that beat memorizing averages

- Calls accepted / completed **per truck per day** last 24 months (by channel, by hour)  
- Average ticket by **cash vs club vs rotation vs fleet vs heavy**  
- Deadhead miles; fuel as % of revenue; response-time SLA misses  
- Insurance premiums **per unit** + claims history; workers’ comp for drivers  
- Truck roster: age, miles, bed/wrecker/heavy mix, lien/loan balances, PM logs  
- Downtime days and why (hydraulics, body, DOT)  
- Driver roster, night coverage, turnover, CDL/endorsement reality where required  
- Motor-club contracts: rate card, tier (primary vs backup), pay terms (45–60 day net common), chargebacks  
- Police/municipal: on rotation? which PDs? ordinance rate sheet? yard requirements?  
- Storage: capacity, occupancy, lien/auction process, complaint/litigation history  
- Property contracts: consent rules, signage, community notice — or landmine  
- Radius map vs Google spend — are you buying unprofitable geography?  
- If “synergy with shops”: actual **drop-to-RO conversion** and ARO — not the spreadsheet  
- Owner hours on the radio; who runs nights when you’re gone

What that means in plain English:

The *category* has nondiscretionary emergency demand and can scale truck-by-truck when density and contracts clear insurance.  
A *specific* book can look busy on Google and still lose — fat premiums, thin club rates, deadhead, idle iron, or an owner using towing as a shiny object next to a better core business.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

This section is the point. Soft marketing skips it. Operators live it.

**1. Truck CapEx + uptime**  
Iron is the product. Used bargains come with hydraulic surprises. New iron is expensive. Downtime with payroll still running is how “volume businesses” bleed.

**2. Insurance that prices the danger honestly**  
Highway shoulders, night scenes, weather, winching — premiums like **mid-teens thousands per truck per year** in Brian’s live example are not a rounding error. Claims can end you.

**3. Drivers who will do the work (and not destroy the asset)**  
24/7 coverage, scene judgment, customer reviews, CDL where needed. Entry-level “anyone with a pulse” fantasies die on the first rollover recovery or the first ignored PM.

**4. Police / rotation politics**  
Slots are limited, relationship-heavy, equipment- and yard-gated. Newcomers hear “almost impossible” for a reason. Incumbents are locked in for years.

**5. Motor-club rate + SLA grind**  
Volume without margin. Backup-tier purgatory. Slow pay. Chargebacks when ETA slips. Clubs are a channel — not a favor.

**6. Cash vs contract mix**  
All-cash Google books die when CPC rises or a competitor outranks you. All-club books die on rate cards. Healthy operators underwrite the blend on purpose.

**7. After-hours reality**  
The money and the misery live at night and in storms. If you won’t staff it, don’t sell 24/7.

**8. Storage / impound regulation**  
Daily storage looks like free money until lien law, auction process, fencing standards, and angry owners show up. State/city variance is extreme.

**9. Non-consent / repo adjacency**  
Comment sections love the revenue. Operators who sleep well often refuse the gun-and-chase profile. Know which business you’re actually buying.

**10. Radius / deadhead from paid ads**  
Brian’s lesson: PPC can fill the board and still lose when trucks cross a metro for thin tickets.

**11. Fleet maintenance culture (even if you “own shops”)**  
Retail bays ≠ recovery-fleet PM. Specialty lines, transfer cases, DOT — vendors and checklists, or chaos.

**12. Light-duty vs heavy identity crisis**  
Buying a heavy truck for Instagram without heavy contracts is CapEx cosplay. Staying light forever while competitors own rotation/heavy can cap the book.

**13. Working capital / slow pay**  
Clubs and municipalities don’t fund your diesel the week you towed. Cash timing kills undercapitalized fleets.

**14. Distracted ownership / “synergy” theater**  
Brian’s public caution: towing *sounded* synergistic with 35–36 shops; execution distracted from diamonds already under the bays. Adjacent ≠ automatic.

**15. Confusing revenue with profit**  
Busy radios with 15% fuel, fat insurance, club rates, and one truck down is a stressful job with a light bar.

**16. Acquisition traps**  
Paying for “rotation access” that isn’t transferable. Ignoring yard lease landmines. Counting Google spikes as run-rate. Mixing repo economics into a consensual retail multiple.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means towing is a **real asset + dispatch + politics business**, not a recession lottery ticket with a winch.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re wired for **ops scorecards**: calls/truck/day, ticket by channel, fuel %, uptime %, insurance $/unit  
- You’re willing to live in **hiring drivers**, night coverage, and confrontation on scenes and lots  
- Truck CapEx, financing, and PM discipline sound like a craft — not a chore you’ll “outsource vibes” to  
- You’re patient enough to earn **club / rotation / fleet** density instead of assuming month-two lock-in  
- 24/7 essential services sound satisfying (storm nights included)  
- You’re fine owning a **boring emergency machine** instead of a shiny startup story  

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want passive / semi-absentee from month one with no dispatch bench  
- You’re undercapitalized for trucks, insurance, fuel float, and slow club pay  
- You hate landlords/yards, municipal paperwork, or standing on a shoulder at 1:40 a.m.  
- You need blank-slate creative invention (this is proven iron + relationships territory)  
- You’re actually shopping **auto repair**, **quick lube**, or pure **repo** — different pages, different risk  
- You’re buying towing mainly as a “synergy” side quest while a better core business needs your focus (Brian’s hard-won anti-pattern)

Franchise vs independent is mostly a red herring here — the category in Brian’s idea sheet isn’t a prototype FDD aisle.  
Same levers either way: **trucks, drivers, channels, uptime, insurance**.

## Risks (the short list)

- Idle or broken trucks + fixed payroll → busy-looking broke  
- Insurance spike or uninsurable claim  
- Club rate compression / backup-tier forever  
- Never clearing police rotation / yard requirements  
- Impound/lien compliance miss → fines, lawsuits, seizure drama  
- Driver turnover → SLA collapse and reviews death  
- Deadhead Google geography  
- Heavy CapEx without heavy contracts (or light fleet in a heavy-demand corridor)  
- Distracted ownership chasing synergy while core ops need the laser  
- Paying seller hope for “relationships” that leave with the seller

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “cars break down.”

You’re buying the right to run a **dispatch-and-iron machine** that turns emergencies and contracts into completed responses — if you can keep trucks online, staff the clock, win a channel mix that isn’t all thin club work, and know your numbers cold while insurance and politics stay honest.

Some people should buy a tired light-duty book with real call logs and install dispatch + PM discipline.  
Some people should build density truck-by-truck in one metro — cash first, clubs second, rotation when earned.  
Some people should partner with a serious operator for shop feed instead of owning the light bars (Brian’s post-exit path toward referring work).  
Some people should walk — and put the same capital into repair bays, a cleaner recurring route, or a vehicle with less 2 a.m. liability.

The question isn’t “do people need tow trucks?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a stressful job under a light bar on a truck that breaks when you need it most?**

If you want help comparing doors (this model vs auto repair vs quick lube vs other essential operators), that’s a conversation.  
Not a pitch deck.
