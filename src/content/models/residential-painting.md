Houses fade. Trim peels. Kids mark walls. HOAs send letters. Buyers walk a listing and flinch at chalky siding or 2009 beige.

Someone has to quote it, schedule a crew, buy the paint, protect the furniture (or beat the weather), and stand behind the finish when a spot peels or a door edge chips six months later.

That’s **residential painting** — **interior and exterior** under one ownership machine.

Not a paint aisle.  
Not “I’ll brush your porch on Saturday.”  
A **quote → crew → materials margin** business that lives on **estimating accuracy, crew capacity, schedule density, and trust**.

Simple on a whiteboard.  
Brutal if you confuse “people need paint” with “this runs itself.”

---

## What the business actually is

You sell residential paint jobs across two related lanes:

| Lane | What it feels like |
|------|---------------------|
| **Interior** | Year-round in most markets. In-home sales. Occupied-home friction (furniture, pets, kids, smell). Faster cycles; more smaller tickets (rooms, turnovers) plus whole-home packages. |
| **Exterior** | Weather-bound. Prep-heavy. Peak spring–fall in most markets. Bigger single tickets. Public failure mode (the whole street sees it). |

Many healthy books run **both** — interior hedges winter and rain; exterior spikes ticket size in peak season. Some operators lean one-first and add the other later. Same category family; different operating day.

Typical loop:

1. **Lead** — Google, yard signs, past customers, realtor / GC / flooring / roofer referrals, neighborhood marketing  
2. **Estimate** — measure, photograph, judge prep, access, substrate / room condition; interiors often sold *in the home*  
3. **Quote / close** — fixed project price, timeline, material tier, color / sheen, deposit  
4. **Schedule** — crew + materials + a window that holds (weather outside; sleep schedules inside)  
5. **Produce** — prep is most of the hours; rolling paint is the visible part; cleanup is half the reputation  
6. **Collect** — deposit / progress / final  
7. **Warranty** — touch-ups and callbacks; your name stays on the walls and the curb appeal

**Scope lock for this page:** contractor / franchise that **sells and installs** residential paint jobs (interior + exterior). Light commercial may appear as adjacent fill — see [commercial painting](/models/commercial-painting) for facility / B2B bid cycles.  
Not a paint manufacturer or big-box retailer.  
Not “I own a ladder and a brush” solo craftsman as the whole company (craft job ≠ this page’s ownership math).

Brian’s teaching frame on the category:

- Painting sits in the **high-ticket, sales-driven home services** stack (with roofing, fencing, flooring, cabinets) — project tickets often framed in the **$5k / $10k / $30k+** conversation depending on scope  
- It’s a **people** business that happens to put paint on houses — he doesn’t need to love painting to own the machine  
- Asset-light painting territories often land roughly in the **~$100k–$200k** startup conversation (system-specific; verify Item 7)  
- Named systems exist (CertaPro, Five Star, That 1 Painter / ResiBrands family, others). Treat them as proof the category franchises — not as the point of this page

## How money comes in

You sell **jobs**, not hours on a timesheet the customer sees.

A single residential exterior can land anywhere from low-four to mid-five figures depending on size, stories, prep, material, and market. Interiors range from a few hundred dollars for a room refresh to mid-five figures for a whole-house package.  
Brian’s public teaching has used **~$5,000** as a plain-English “average job” example when talking lead offers and neighborhood marketing — useful for intuition, not your forecast. Interior books often stack **more smaller tickets** plus occasional whole-home packages.

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Close rate on estimates** — estimating skill + sales process (often in-home for interiors), not “more leads” alone  
2. **Average job size / attach** — trim, ceilings, decks, second coat quality, related rooms / exterior  
3. **Crew utilization** — sold production days vs rain, no-shows, half-day sprawl, and waiting on materials  
4. **Materials discipline** — paint + sundries are real money, but usually **not** the majority of the job cost; waste and wrong product still kill jobs  
5. **Callbacks / warranty** — one bad peel or cut-line on a street of Google reviewers  
6. **Mix** — interior vs exterior vs light commercial fill; exterior-only in a northern winter without a hedge is a cash-flow story

Gross margin lives in **price − materials − production labor − sales commission − warranty**.  
Then overhead: office/PM, software, insurance, trucks/branding, local marketing, and (if franchised) royalty + brand fund.

Brian’s contribution-margin teaching on a **painter territory** (education shape, not a guarantee):

- Variable stack can look like roughly **~65%** of each revenue dollar (materials, contractors/crew, royalties, commissions, credit-card fees)  
- What’s left — roughly **~35% contribution** — fills the monthly fixed-cost “bucket” before anything is real profit  
- Illustrative materials in that teaching landed around the **low-teens %** of sales; royalty + brand fund often framed in the **mid-single digits + a couple points** (system-specific)

Industry shape outside Brian (education only): labor/prep dominates direct cost more than the can of paint; healthy operators watch **crew days, weather windows, and occupied-home rework** as hard as “revenue.”

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Housing stock ages. Sun and rain don’t stop. Tastes change. Landlords turn units. Buyers, HOAs, and listing photos keep forcing refresh decisions.

When the economy is good, people refresh and upgrade.  
When the economy is tight, deferred maintenance eventually shows up as peeling paint and scuffed interiors — demand doesn’t vanish; ticket size and mix shift.

The constraint isn’t “houses exist.”  
It’s **estimating accuracy + crew capacity + trust + schedule density** in *your* territory.

Interior’s calendar edge: **the sky doesn’t cancel the week**.  
Exterior’s ticket edge: curb appeal and weather peaks.  
Same category. Different operating reality — best books usually understand both.

## Micro: what actually drives one company

**Sales + estimating**  
Residential painting is a project sale. Bad estimates (missed prep, wrong access, optimistic weather or “one day” room math) become angry customers or zero margin. Interiors lean harder into face-to-face home estimates and color decisions; exteriors lean into substrate judgment and weather honesty.

**Crew network**  
W-2 painters, 1099 crews, or a hybrid — all versions live or die on people who show up, prep properly, protect the house, and don’t ghost mid-job. Brian’s published launch notes on multi-territory painting: the biggest early fear was **finding high-quality painters**; capacity before ego.

**Schedule density**  
Exteriors: rain day ≠ free day. Interiors: half-day room jobs across town destroy utilization. Stack geographically, hedge weather with indoor work, and be honest about job duration.

**Lead engine**  
Digital ads help. So do boring tactics Brian teaches: past-customer follow-up (more rooms; exteriors come back years later), yard signs, door hangers with a *real* offer relative to ticket size, and referral partners who already walk into the same houses.

**Owner role (honest version)**  
Early stage: lead flow, estimating standards, sales leadership, production coordination, cash.  
Multi-unit versions hire operators so the owner is vision + people + capital — still not fishing from day one.  
Absentee with a weak sales + crew machine is how you buy a logo and a headache.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

**1. It’s a sales + estimating business wearing coveralls**  
If you can’t build a pipeline and quote accurately, the sprayers don’t matter.

**2. Weather feast / famine (exterior) vs thin-pipeline myth (interior)**  
Exterior peaks hard. Interior hedges weather — it does **not** hedge a dead January pipeline.

**3. Crew capacity and quality**  
No painters = no production. Bad painters = callbacks that erase three good jobs — worse when people live with the work every morning.

**4. Prep underestimation**  
The customer sees color. Your margin lives in scraping, patching, caulking, priming, and masking.

**5. Occupied-home friction + public failure modes**  
Furniture, pets, kids, VOC smell indoors; overspray and peeling outdoors. Communication separates pros from one-stars.

**6. Utilization vs promises**  
You sold a finish date. Then it rains — or selections stall. Density and honesty matter.

**7. Lead cost inflation + price competition**  
Google gets expensive. “Buddy with a brush” underbids. Past-customer and referral muscles matter.

**8. Compliance & liability**  
Heights, lead-safe rules on pre-1978 homes, workers’ comp, property damage. Boring until it isn’t.

**9. Owner dependency**  
Sales energy and estimating standards don’t clone easily. Semi-absentee pitches in sales-heavy home services are a common trap.

**10. Confusing revenue with profit**  
A booked summer (or a month of rooms) can still be thin after commissions, rain days, drive time, callbacks, and fixed costs.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means residential painting is a **real project-sales + labor-schedule business**, not a weather lottery ticket or a beige-wall lottery ticket.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re wired for sales leadership, estimating discipline, and pipeline follow-up  
- You’re okay managing crews (or a production manager) and weather / occupied-home reschedules  
- You can hold a quality standard while moving volume  
- You’re capitalized for seasonality *and* thin marketing weeks  
- High-ticket home services excite you more than recurring hourly routes

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want passive / semi-absentee from month one  
- You hate selling and hate hiring sellers  
- You’re looking for predictable weekly route revenue (think pest, lawn, senior care hours)  
- You’re undercapitalized for a wet spring, a dead winter, or a slow pipeline month  
- You want to *be* the painter on the ladder as the whole company  
- You’re actually shopping facility / bid-cycle [commercial painting](/models/commercial-painting) as the primary book

Franchise vs independent is a separate call.  
Same category either way: quote, crew, materials, reputation — inside and outside the house.

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “people hate peeling paint” or “people hate beige walls.”

You’re buying the right to run a **project-sales machine** that turns housing stock, trust, and crew capacity into finished interiors and exteriors — if you can quote, staff, schedule, and collect.

Some people should build interior-first for year-round tempo and add exterior as a peak-season ticket.  
Some people should build exterior-first and add interior as a hedge.  
Some people should run both as one residential painting book with intentional mix.  
Some people should walk — and put the same capital into a vehicle with cleaner recurring economics.

The question isn’t “do houses need paint?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a stressful schedule that only works when the sky cooperates, the crew shows up, and every color decision finally sticks?**
