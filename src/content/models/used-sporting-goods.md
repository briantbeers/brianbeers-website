Kids quit hockey.  
Parents quit paying full retail for the next kid’s skates.

Garages fill with last season’s cleats, bats that “might get used again,” treadmills that became coat racks, and golf bags nobody wants to list on Marketplace for three weeks.

That’s the used sporting goods **buy-sell-trade** model.

Not a big-box aisle of brand-new everything.  
Not a Facebook free-for-all in a driveway.  
Not a charity dump bin (that’s [clothing bins](/models/clothing-bins) — wholesale pounds, opposite energy).

A retail store that **buys** (and often trades) gently used sports and fitness gear from people walking in…  
and **sells** it — usually mixed with some new merchandise — to other people walking in.

Same door. Two jobs.  
Ticket sizes from a $8 baseball to a $400 treadmill.

Same engine as [used clothing resale](/models/used-clothing-resale) and [kids clothing resale](/models/kids-clothing-resale) — different seasons, different bulk, different “fashion” risk on gear that can look obsolete overnight.

---

## What the business actually is

You’re running a curated secondhand sporting goods store — often with a **new + used** mix under one roof.

Racks of cleats and athletic apparel.  
Walls of bats, sticks, clubs, skates.  
Floor space for bikes, ellipticals, weight benches — the bulky stuff that makes “I’ll just sell it online” a lie people tell themselves.

Sellers bring trunks of outgrown youth gear and forgotten home-gym equipment.  
You pay cash or store credit for what you want.  
You clean it. Tag it. Turn it.  
Buyers hunt value for the next season, the next kid, or the next fitness kick.

The loop:

**Buy (or trade) inventory from the public → price it → sell it → buy more.**

Often with a **new-goods** lane alongside used: accessories, basics, and in-season items you still need on the shelf when the trade-in river runs thin.

Two flavors exist in the wild:

1. **Buy-outright / trade (take title)** — cash or credit leaves when you buy; cash returns when it sells. Bad buys are *your* problem. Stronger systems lean this way so the floor is owned inventory, not a consignment hostage situation.
2. **Classic consignment** — you hang someone else’s gear and split when it sells. Lower cash out the door up front. Slower turns, messier seller drama, and your “eye” (or your software) still has to protect the floor.

Brian’s framing on the adult clothing sister model is blunt: this family of businesses works best when it’s **not** classic consignment — you take title, you own the turn.

Category proof (not the point of the page): large franchised buy-sell-trade sporting goods systems — think Play It Again Sports–style — exist because the physics are real. Youth sports outgrow. Fitness gear gets abandoned. Families want cash today and cheaper gear tomorrow. Independents and other brands play the same game with different software and less brand awareness.

You’re not inventing sports.  
You’re moving gear that already exists — faster than a garage sale, cleaner than Marketplace flaking, cheaper than walking into a big box for everything new.

## How money comes in

Gross margin lives in the **spread** — and in the **mix**.

You buy a youth hockey stick for $15.  
You sell it for $45.

You take a treadmill on trade for $120 in store credit.  
You sell it for $350.

You also sell a new pair of batting gloves at a thinner margin because the customer is already in the store and needs them *this weekend*.

Multiply by thousands of SKUs — and by whether it’s August (back-to-school / fall sports) or February (hockey dump / spring reset).

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Buy right by sport + season + local demand** — perfect gear in the wrong climate month is dead capital on wheels  
2. **Ticket-size mix** — low-ticket volume (balls, apparel, accessories) pays the lights; mid-ticket (bats, skates, clubs) pays the rent; high-ticket (fitness machines, bikes, full kits) can make or break a month — and destroy cash if they don’t move  
3. **Price for speed** — inventory that sits is cash stuck in polyurethane and steel  
4. **Markdown with discipline** — end-of-season gear becomes trash (or a fire-sale that trains customers to wait) if you hesitate  
5. **Two-sided traffic** — sellers *and* buyers. Empty floors kill sell-through. Full floors with no shoppers kill cash  
6. **Trade vs cash** — store credit often pulls the next purchase; cash buys may need tighter pricing discipline

In disclosed buy-sell-trade sporting goods systems, **gross profit** (sales minus COGS, *before* rent/labor/royalty) often lands near the **low-to-mid 50s percent** of sales — meaning COGS roughly mid-to-high 40s. That’s lower gross-margin *percent* than pure apparel resale in many disclosures, partly because **new** inventory sits in the mix and equipment COGS behave differently than thrift racks.

Then the real P&L swings hit:

- **Labor** at the buy counter and on the floor (evaluations take time; bulky moves take bodies)  
- **Rent** for a box big enough for treadmills *and* cleat walls  
- **Shrink** (theft, damage, “it walked out on a bike”)  
- **Markdowns** when seasons flip  
- **Marketing** to keep both sides of the door busy  
- **Royalty / ads / software** if you’re in a franchise stack

You’re not a youth-sports influencer.  
You’re a **turns** company wearing a sports brand.

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Kids keep growing. Adults keep quitting hobbies. Equipment keeps getting expensive.

Every spring a new class of parents needs baseball gear.  
Every spring another class dumps last year’s sizes.

Good times → families buy new → garages overflow → they need to sell.  
Tight times → families hunt value → they need to buy used.

Supply and demand both show up. Just for different reasons.

Add the clothing contrast lightly:

Adult used clothing rides fashion cycles and thrift-as-trend.  
Kids clothing resale rides **biology** — outgrow is mandatory.  
Used sporting goods rides **both** — kids outgrow gear *and* sports go in and out of season, while fitness equipment goes in and out of *motivation*.

Youth sports fees are high. Brand-new skates are high. A used treadmill that “almost never got used” is still a real product if you buy it right.

That’s the engine. Not vibes. Not one Olympic spike (those help a month — they don’t build the store).

## Micro: what actually drives one store

A single location lives or dies on a few boring things:

**Trade area / sports density**  
Enough families in youth leagues, enough gyms and golfers, enough climate that matches what you stock. A hockey-heavy northern suburb is a different machine than a baseball/softball southern strip — same model, different buy desk.

**Location / lease**  
Strip or power center with parking and loading access. Room for bulky fitness. Rent you can survive when January is soft and August is chaos.  
(Retail box math — same quiet killer as clothing resale.)

**Buy desk skill (or system)**  
Mom-and-pop used sports shops often live on the owner’s “eye.” Fragile. One buyer leaves, quality tanks — or you overpay for dead brands.

Stronger versions use **software / playbooks** to tell staff:

- which categories turn in *this* market  
- what to pay by condition, age, and demand  
- what to price for speed  
- when to markdown  
- what inventory is actually worth right now

Taste still helps.  
Guesswork shouldn’t be the whole company.

**Seasonal merchandising**  
Adult thrift can hide a wrong buy in “vintage.”  
Kids thrift cannot hide a wall of size 6 coats in May.  
Sporting goods cannot hide a wall of hockey in July in a warm market — or snowboards in April.

**Ticket mix discipline**  
A floor full of $12 accessories looks busy and still can’t cover rent.  
A floor full of $800 ellipticals looks impressive and freezes cash.  
The job is balance.

**Labor at the buy counter**  
Peak hours = parents with trunks wanting evaluations *now*. Labor scales with activity. Schedule wrong and you either lose sellers or bleed payroll.

**Inventory as working capital**  
Opening inventory isn’t decoration — it’s product you’ll sell. In this category, disclosed opening inventory bands often sit **heavier** than apparel-only concepts (six figures is normal in large systems) because equipment costs more to put on the floor. Underbuy and the store looks dead. Overbuy and cash is trapped in off-season steel.

**Owner involvement**  
Same lesson as the adult clothing live deal breakdown: remote / distracted ownership shows up in the numbers. Same model. Different execution. Different outcome.

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

Franchise brands in this category that publish an Item 19 will show you **ranges** — sales and gross profit, not a guarantee of what hits your bank account after rent, labor, debt, and paying yourself.

Shape from one large buy-sell-trade sporting goods system’s recent disclosure (education only — Play It Again Sports–style category proof — always pull the current FDD for any brand you’re actually considering):

- **Average unit:** roughly **~$1.17M** gross sales, with average **gross** profit around **~$625k** (~**53%** gross margin before operating expenses)  
- **Median sales:** closer to **~$1.0M** (average gets pulled by the right tail)  
- **Top quartile:** closer to **~$2.1M** sales (and ~**$1.1M** average gross profit dollars)  
- **Bottom quartile:** can sit near **~$530k** average sales — still a real store, thin room for rent/labor/markdown mistakes  
- **Royalty:** in that family, often around **~5%** of gross sales (plus modest marketing / software fees — read Item 6; local co-op can stack)  
- **New build investment band:** mid-six figures all-in — think roughly **~$350k–$460k** in a recent Item 7, driven hard by fixtures, POS, buildout, **opening inventory (~$100k–$120k)**, rent deposits, and working capital  
- **Mature vs new:** older stores in the pack often show higher average sales than brand-new cohorts — ramp is real; first-year stores can look mid-$800ks while mature cohorts clear $1.2M+ averages in the same disclosure set

What that means in plain English:

The *model* can work.  
A *specific store* can be a dog or a rocket.

Your job in diligence isn’t to memorize the average.  
It’s to ask: **where does this unit sit in the pack, and can I move it?**

Same spreadsheet brain you’d use on adult used-clothing or kids resale:

- location / sports density / parking / climate fit  
- system quality (buy tools, training, brand awareness)  
- growth potential from *this* baseline  
- multi-unit proof (do operators already stack 2–3–4?)  
- existing vs new build math  
- is it worth *your* cash vs a different vehicle entirely

## Biggest challenges

Say these out loud before you fall in love with the hockey wall.

**Seasonality (the whole game)**  
Fall sports, winter sports, spring baseball, summer camps, holiday fitness resolutions — demand spikes and dies on a calendar *and* a climate. Wrong season mix freezes cash in the heaviest SKUs you own. A warm winter or a cancelled league season can turn “safe” inventory into markdown fuel.

**Buy desk judgment**  
You’re not hanging jeans. You’re pricing condition, brand, model year, safety norms, and local demand in thirty seconds while a dad waits with a trunk full of gear. Overpay once on a treadmill and you’ve eaten a week of accessory profit. Underpay chronically and sellers vanish to Marketplace. Training + software + owner spot-checks matter more than a sports-fan personality.

**Space / bulk**  
Cleats hang. Ellipticals don’t. You need square footage, backroom, and often a truck-friendly load-in. Space you don’t fill still costs rent. Space you fill with slow high-ticket gear costs rent *and* cash.

**Fashion of gear (yes, gear has fashion)**  
Last year’s bat tech. “Wrong” skate brand for the local travel team. Fitness machines that look dated. Helmet standards and style cycles. Youth apparel graphics. You’re not as fashion-volatile as [used clothing resale](/models/used-clothing-resale) — but you’re not immune. Dead brands die harder when they’re also heavy and hard to ship.

**Big-box / Amazon / Marketplace**  
Dick’s, Academy, Scheels, Amazon, SidelineSwap, Facebook Marketplace — buyers price-check you; sellers list without driving to your strip center. Your edge is **cash (or credit) today, curation, try-on/try-swing, and a store that doesn’t flake**. If you’re slower and colder than Marketplace and more expensive than a big-box clearance aisle with no service, you lose both sides of the door.

**Lease / buildout**  
Mid-four-thousand to six-thousand sq ft ranges are common teaching sizes in this category. Rent that looks fine at peak season looks brutal in the soft months. TI, fixtures, and cameras aren’t optional theater. Lease terms are not a footnote.

**Labor at the buy counter**  
Evaluations are the product. Slow buys = angry sellers. Fast, sloppy buys = trash on the floor. Peak Saturdays need bodies who can say no without killing the brand.

**Theft / shrink / damage**  
Open retail, small accessories, busy weekends, bulky items that roll. Shrink sits inside COGS in serious disclosures for a reason.

**New vs used mix tension**  
New goods fill holes and attach add-ons — and compress margin percent if you lean too hard. Used goods are the identity — and starve the floor if sellers dry up. Balance is the job.

**Adult / kids clothing contrast, one line:** apparel resale fights style cycles and size walls; sporting goods fights **season calendars, bulky working capital, and price-transparent big-box competition** for the same bat.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means used sporting goods is a **real retail business**, not a “I like sports” side hustle.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You like retail energy (people, product, pace) and don’t mind sports-adjacent chaos  
- You’re willing to live in inventory turns, season flips, and labor schedules  
- You’re fine owning a local brand that wins on execution, not a “set it and forget it” app  
- Multi-unit or sister pairing with apparel/kids resale sounds exciting, not exhausting  
- You can say **no** at the buy desk without needing to be the coolest parent in the league

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want pure passive income  
- You hate managing people / part-time turnover  
- You’re allergic to leases, theft risk, cash handling, and buy-counter conflict  
- You need a blank-slate creative project (this is a playbook business, not an art project)  
- You think “everyone plays sports” replaces trade-area and climate math  
- You confuse loving sports with loving **markdown calendars**

Also: [estate sales](/models/estate-sales) / household liquidation sometimes touch fitness gear in one-off clearouts — different model. Resale retail is **ongoing turns**, not a three-day house sale. [Clothing bins](/models/clothing-bins) are wholesale textile routes — not this.

## Risks (say them out loud)

- **Wrong buy** → racks (and floors) of gear in the wrong season/sport  
- **Slow turns** → cash frozen in inventory — especially high-ticket fitness  
- **Labor creep** → volume up, profit flat  
- **Lease / buildout** → the quiet killer of “affordable” retail deals  
- **Distracted ownership** → same brand, bottom-quartile results  
- **Marketplace + big-box leakage** → sellers skip you; buyers price-check you to death  
- **Season / weather / league risk** — demand can move without your permission  
- **Franchise stack** (if you go that route) → royalties + rules; you trade freedom for a system — make sure the system is worth it  
- **Disclosure footnotes** → “gross profit” is not what hits your personal bank account after you pay rent, staff, the bank, and yourself

None of that means walk away by default.  
It means diligence is the product.

---

## The ownership point

You’re not buying hockey sticks.

You’re buying a machine that converts **outgrown seasons and abandoned hobbies** into cash flow — if you run the buy desk, the turns, the mix, and the labor.

Some people should start one.  
Some people should buy a tired one and fix the season mix.  
Some people should pair it with apparel or kids resale and catch a family’s whole closet-and-garage lifecycle.  
Some people should walk — and put the down payment into a different vehicle entirely.

The question isn’t “do you like sports?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or just a busier version of a job with more cleat smell?**

If you want help comparing doors (used sporting goods vs kids/adult clothing resale vs something outside retail), that’s a conversation.
