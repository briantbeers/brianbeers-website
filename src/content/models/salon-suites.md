She’s done taking 40–50% of her book for a front desk that never booked her.  
He’s got a massage table, a loyal Friday list, and zero interest in managing a reception drama.  
Somebody wants a **private door**, a sink that works, parking that doesn’t feel sketchy after dark in January — and a weekly rent that stays the same whether she does twelve clients or twenty.

Someone needs a **suite landlord**: subdivided commercial space, shared amenities, clean common areas, and a lease that treats beauty professionals like tenants — not commission employees.

That’s a **salon suites** company.

Not a traditional salon where *you* employ the stylists and own the client book fights.  
Not [med-spa](/models/med-spa) clinical injectables under a medical director (sometimes a *tenant category* inside suites — totally different oversight stack if *you* operate it).  
Not [fitness-gym](/models/fitness-gym) memberships and churn (same *facility + fixed plant* brain — different buyer and labor).  
Not [property-management](/models/property-management) residential doors (same *landlord / occupancy / lease ops* brain — different asset class and licensing).

You’re running a **beauty real-estate ops** business: suites occupied × weekly (or monthly) rent − master lease / debt − utilities − amenities − light management − the empty rooms during lease-up and the quiet weeks when three stylists ghost you for a competitor’s free month.

Simple on a whiteboard.  
Hard in real life — because **buildout CapEx**, **occupancy**, **lease structure**, **amenities race**, **stylist recruiting**, and **landlord vs operator identity** show up before the lobby looks like a beauty magazine.

Sola / Phenix / Image Studios–class brands and strong indie suite buildings sell overlapping physics. The logo is optional. The utilized suite and the master lease are not.

## What the business actually is

You’re a **local salon-suite landlord / operator**: you control (lease or own) a retail or office box — often roughly **~4,500–7,000+ sq ft** education chatter for a multi-suite plant — then **TI / build out** private rooms (often ~**20–40** suites depending on plan), plumbing for wet services, HVAC, lighting, security, shared amenities (laundry, break room, reception or lobby, Wi-Fi, sometimes booking / access tech), and you **rent suites to independent beauty professionals**.

They run *their* businesses inside your walls: hair, barber, nails, lashes, brows, wax/sugar, spray tan, esthetics, massage, makeup, sometimes teeth whitening / medspa-adjacent tenants — mix varies hard by brand and market. You generally **do not** take a cut of their service tickets. You collect **rent** (often weekly) and keep the building usable.

**Two product shapes (don’t blur them):**

| | **Suite landlord (this page)** | **Traditional salon / booth-rental hybrid** |
|--|--------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|
| **What you sell** | Private suite + amenities + professional environment | Hair/beauty *services* (or booth rent inside *your* salon brand) |
| **Primary constraint** | **Suite occupancy × rent collected − fixed plant** | **Stylist labor / commission + retail + front desk** |
| **Labor feel** | Low: many stabilized sites run with **zero or near-zero W-2 staff** (tenants open/close) | High: payroll, tips politics, walk-offs take the book |
| **CapEx feel** | Heavy: plumbing every room, corridors, finishes — often **high-six to low-seven figures** all-in | Mid: stations + inventory; less “apartmentize the box” |
| **Cash shape** | Recurring rent; lease-up is the cliff; full = waitlist game | Ticket volume; no-shows; commission drama |

Brian’s published framing (YT “14 Businesses…” + Notion newsletter / franchisor notes) puts this squarely as **real estate + amenities**: find a box, convert it into roughly **~30 individual suites**, rent room-by-room to beauty pros who **pay every week**, and once you’re full you’re “kind of done” — except waitlist, turnover, and keeping women comfortable walking in after dark. You **don’t make money from the haircut**. You make money by **leveraging the asset**.

Typical menu:

1. **Suite rent** — the spine (weekly cadence common; size / wet vs dry / corner premiums)  
2. **Amenities included or à la carte** — laundry, lobby, security, Wi-Fi, utilities caps, parking, sometimes backbar / retail lockers  
3. **Lease-up / recruiting** — the sales motion that fills dark suites  
4. **Turnovers / make-ready** — paint, plumbing snags, lock changes, photos, next tenant in  
5. **Optional attach** — premium suites, double suites, short-term / pop-up, event days, branded common-area partnerships  
6. **Not the same company:** operating a med spa, wax membership shop, or spray-tan flagship *as the landlord* — those can be *tenants* (Hello Sugar / goGlow–class brands literally launch *inside* suites in Brian’s adjacency content)

Typical loop:

1. **Win the box** — site selection, LOI, TI negotiation, drawings, construction, certificate of occupancy  
2. **Pre-lease / soft open** — beauty-pro outreach, open houses, referral from existing tenants, brand lead flow (if any)  
3. **Lease the suite** — agreement, deposit, insurance certs, license checks where required, keys / access  
4. **Operate the plant** — cleaning common areas, repairs, utility management, security, neighbor conflicts, rent collection  
5. **Retain / replace** — when someone leaves for cheaper rent next door, refill from waitlist  
6. **Stabilize** — high occupancy + low drama + amenities that still feel worth the premium

**Scope lock:** this page is the **salon-suite landlord model** — real estate + amenities + suite occupancy + low labor vs traditional salon.  
It is **not** a commission salon where you own the client experience end-to-end.  
It is **not** [med-spa](/models/med-spa) as *your* clinical P&L (tenant medspa ≠ you underwriting injectables).  
It is **not** [fitness-gym](/models/fitness-gym) membership economics.  
It is **not** residential [property-management](/models/property-management) — though the *ops brain* (occupancy, leases, turns, after-hours comfort) rhymes hard.  
It is **not** “passive income with a logo and no lease-up work.”

Named proof points exist across the category: **Sola**, **Phenix / Phoenix**, **Image Studios**, **Salons by JC**, and strong local indie buildings. Treat logos as **category proof** — not as the point of this page, and not as a substitute for TI math, occupancy reality, and whether *you* want to be a beauty-pro landlord for a decade.

Independents, franchisees, and multi-site developers can all sit here. Same umbrella. Wildly different CapEx and lease-up scars.

## How money comes in

You sell **occupied suite-weeks** (and the amenity story that justifies the rent) — not Instagram lobby tours and not the tenant’s retail tickets.

Education rent / occupancy bands (national category chatter; metros sit higher; secondary markets sit lower — **verify your trade area**):

- Weekly suite rents often discussed roughly **~$150–$500+/week** in smaller / lower-cost markets, **~$250–$800** mid-tier, and **~$500–$1,500+** in expensive metros; many suburban / mid-size plants chatter around roughly **~$275–$500+/week** blended  
- Facilities often need roughly **~70–80%+ occupancy** to cover fixed plant (master lease / debt, utilities, insurance, light ops) — every suite above that threshold is where the model *feels* like leverage  
- Stabilized multi-suite plants in franchise Item 19–class education summaries often show **gross rent collections** in the mid-**hundreds of thousands** annually when occupancy is real — spread is enormous; silent non-reporters matter  
- Brian’s Image Studios–class education framing (newsletter Item 19 band tables; franchisor notes): open lean (~**40%** chatter), breakeven often framed around ~**55–60%**, ramp toward full; high-occupancy cohorts cited with ~**$500k** average revenue and ~**$217k** after “key expenses” **before debt** in one disclosed high-occupancy cut — *shape, not your forecast*

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Occupied paying suites** — not “signed LOIs” and not “toured last month”  
2. **Average rent per suite × collection discipline** — discounts, free months, and slow payers are silent margin leaks  
3. **Lease-up speed** — months of dark suites while debt / master rent still hits  
4. **Turnover / vacancy days** — every empty week is fixed-cost exposure  
5. **Amenity premium vs commodity rent race** — why someone pays you instead of the cheaper building three miles away  
6. **Tenant mix** — all-hair buildings cannibalize; diversified beauty services (Brian’s call notes: category often ~**50/50** hair vs everything else in stronger luxury mixes vs ~**80%** hair in many generic suites)  
7. **Labor lightness that stays light** — the day you hire a full front desk *and* chase every late rent *and* become the stylists’ therapist, you reinvented a salon with worse margins

Gross rent *looks* simple on a napkin.  
Then buildout overruns, TI that never funded, utility spikes (hot water is not theoretical), amenity arms races, and a three-month lease-up that missed the model show up.

You’re not a glamorous salon owner.  
You’re a **specialty commercial landlord** whose tenants blow dry hair for a living.

**Light contrast (same neighborhood, different check):**

| Model | Who pays you | What you “sell” | Overhead feel |
|-------|--------------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Traditional salon** | Clients (tickets) | Services + retail; stylists as labor | Payroll, walk-offs, front desk |
| **Med spa** | Patients (cash-pay treatments) | Clinical chair hours under oversight | Injectors, medical director, CAC, devices |
| **Fitness gym** | Members (dues) | Access / classes | Rent + churn + trainers/managers |
| **Residential PM** | Property owners (fees) | Door ops + trust accounting | After-hours, vendors, licensing |
| **Salon suites** | Beauty-pro tenants (rent) | Private suite + amenities + safe plant | Buildout CapEx; occupancy; lease-up; light ops |

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Licensed beauty pros keep wanting **independence** — keep 100% of their service dollars, set their hours, brand themselves — without buying a full salon buildout alone. Clients keep wanting **private rooms** and a less chaotic experience than a packed open floor. The category professionalizes because the *tenant demand* is real: stylists, nail techs, estheticians, massage therapists, and adjacent beauty operators are a large, recurring renter class in every metro with a cosmetology ecosystem.

Good times → premium rents, waitlists, amenity upgrades, multi-suite expansions.  
Tight times → free-month wars, occupancy dips, tenants consolidating hours, landlords who underwrote 95% forever meet 70%.

Add the sister-model contrast lightly:

[Property-management](/models/property-management) rides **doors under management + sticky fees + trust ops**.  
Salon suites rides **suites under lease + sticky weekly rent + beauty-tenant ops**.  
Same “I’m the middle layer between real estate and humans who create drama” emotion. Different plumbing and different Friday-night vibe.

[Fitness-gym](/models/fitness-gym) and [med-spa](/models/med-spa) sit nearby as **health-wellness facility** cousins — membership/clinical retail vs **landlord** math. Don’t underwrite them as the same company because the lobby smells like shampoo.

That’s the engine. Not one ribbon-cutting Reel of marble counters.

## Micro: what actually drives one building

**Buildout CapEx (the career-defining line)**  
Converting a vanilla box into wet suites is where the money goes: plumbing stacks, electrical, HVAC, fire/life safety, finishes, locks, laundry, exterior signage. Education bands for branded multi-suite projects often land roughly **~$700k–$2M+** all-in before/after TI; Brian’s YT framing cites roughly **~$1M–$2M**; his newsletter Item 7–class band for one luxury system cites roughly **~$832k–$1.7M**; franchisor notes chatter **~$900k–$1.3M** build with meaningful cash down even when financed. **Landlord TI allowances** can swing equity required by six figures. Underwrite the *construction* as the product launch — not the logo.

**Occupancy (the unit of truth)**  
You’re not scaling “beauty TAM.” You’re scaling **paying suite-weeks**. Lease-up from dark to breakeven to full is the whole early game. Stabilized operators live on refill speed when someone leaves. Waitlists only help if they’re real and convertible.

**Lease structure (master + suite)**  
Your **master lease** (or debt if you own) is the fixed cliff. Suite agreements need deposits, insurance, license proof, use clauses, late fees, and exit rules that survive a tenant who stops paying after a breakup with their best client. Length, escalators, exclusives, restoration, personal guarantees — this is commercial real estate diligence wearing a salon mirror.

**Amenities race**  
Security cameras, well-lit parking, laundry that works, strong Wi-Fi, nice lobby, coffee, booking tech, “luxury” finishes — each amenity is CapEx and OpEx. Skipping them loses tenants to the building that didn’t. Overbuilding them for a B-minus trade area is how you finance a hotel lobby nobody’s rent pays for.

**Stylist / beauty-pro recruiting**  
Your “sales team” is often *you* at open houses, Facebook groups, cosmetology school relationships, and referrals. Competition is the suite building down the road offering a free month — and the traditional salon waving a signing bonus. Occupancy is a recruiting problem disguised as a real-estate problem.

**Landlord vs operator identity**  
The model breaks when you start “helping” with their Instagram, mediating client refunds, or quietly employing people to run the front like a salon. Brian’s education pitch loves **near-zero employees** and tenants with keys. That only works if you accept you’re a **landlord**, not the creative director of thirty micro-salons.

**Tenant mix & cannibalization**  
An all-hair floor is thirty people competing for the same clients and complaining about each other’s prices. Diversified services (wax, nails, massage, esthetics, etc.) often stabilize culture and referrals — Brian’s notes contrast luxury mixes vs hair-heavy commodity suites.

**Site / trade-area**  
Daytime traffic, parking, visibility, proximity to licensed pros and clients with disposable income, and night-time *safety feel* matter. A Class-A office park and a tired strip both exist in this category — they attract different rent and different tenants.

**Owner involvement (honest version)**  
Early: site, construction punch lists, recruiting, collections, vendor coordination.  
Later: multi-site leverage is possible (Brian’s notes chatter one manager covering multiple buildings in strong systems) — but only after occupancy and processes exist. Semi-absentee myths die in month four of lease-up.

**Adjacent stack**  
Cleaning, plumbing, locksmith, security, landscaping, signage — you don’t need to own them. You need them *fast* when a suite floods on Saturday.

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

Treat public blogs, FDD Item 7 / Item 19 secondary writeups, Brian’s newsletter tables, and “owner income” ranges as **shape**, not your forecast. Always pull current FDDs (if branded), construction bids, TI letters, master-lease terms, and trailing occupancy for *this* address.

**Operating intuition (education — not forecasts):**

- Money event = **rent collected on occupied suites**, not tenant service volume  
- Breakeven occupancy often discussed roughly **~55–80%** depending on rent level, debt, and amenity load — Brian’s Image Studios–class framing often cites open lean / BE mid-50s–60% / push to full  
- High occupancy is where the model’s “labor-light leverage” story is true; sub-70% with heavy debt is a different personality  
- Debt service is frequently **outside** marketing “key expense” tables — model it yourself  
- Multi-site leverage exists in education chatter; one overloaded human and three half-full buildings is not leverage

**Startup / CapEx education bands (category, wide):**

- Multi-suite buildouts commonly **~$700k–$2M+** all-in; luxury / larger plants climb  
- Cash down often discussed roughly **~$250k–$550k+** even with financing / TI — undercapitalized lease-up is how projects stall  
- Working capital: months of master rent / debt / utilities / marketing while suites fill  
- Franchise / brand paths add fees + system + sometimes RE help — still not a substitute for local recruiting and construction reality  
- Buying an existing **stabilized building** is a different check (diligence on occupancy quality, deferred maintenance, tenant concentration, and whether rents are real vs founder friends)

Your diligence questions should sound like:

- Trailing **occupied paying suites** by month (24 months) — not “capacity”?  
- Average rent / suite, concessions, delinquency, eviction history?  
- Tenant mix — hair % vs diversified services? Top tenants as % of rent roll?  
- Master lease / debt service / TI remaining / restoration obligations?  
- Amenities OpEx and CapEx roadmap — what’s included vs nickel-and-dimed?  
- Lease-up curve if new — pre-leases, deposits, realistic months to BE?  
- Who recruits tenants today — owner hours still glued to Facebook groups?  
- Insurance requirements for tenants; your GL / property / loss-of-rents?  
- If franchised: royalty structure on *your rent* vs theater; what the system actually does for site, build, and fill  
- Bonus depreciation / tax stories (Brian discusses them with Image Studios–class buildouts) — **not** a reason to buy a bad occupancy plan; counsel / CPA required; not advice

Same brain you’d use buying any specialty commercial plant: fixed costs, lease-up, collection, and whether *you* want thirty beauty-pro landlords’ phone numbers in your pocket.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

This section is the point. Soft marketing skips it. Operators live it.

**1. Buildout CapEx & construction reality**  
Plumbing every suite is not a weekend TI. Overruns, delayed COs, value-engineering that cheapens the “luxury” pitch, and contractors who’ve never built wet beauty rooms will eat your model. CapEx is the product. Miss it and occupancy math is fiction.

**2. Occupancy / lease-up cliff**  
Dark suites while the master lease and debt clear every month. Optimistic “we’ll be 90% by month six” decks meet slow recruiting and a competitor’s free-rent special. Breakeven occupancy is a number you feel in your checking account.

**3. Lease structure (master + suite agreements)**  
A bad master lease (rent bumps, weak TI, personal guarantee, restoration) can make a full building still feel broke. Weak suite leases make collections theater. This is commercial RE with shampoo — lawyer up.

**4. Amenities race**  
The building across town adds a better lobby, laundry that doesn’t eat socks, and night security that actually monitors. You either match CapEx/OpEx or discount rent. Racing amenities without raising rent is a slow bleed; refusing amenities is a fast vacancy.

**5. Stylist / beauty-pro recruiting**  
Your inventory doesn’t sell itself. Cosmetology ecosystems, referral loops, open houses, and reputation among pros decide fill rate. If you hate B2B sales to independent operators, you will hate this business — even if you love “real estate.”

**6. Landlord vs operator identity**  
The fatal blur: you start running their businesses, staffing a salon-style desk “just to help,” or mediating every client complaint. Labor creeps back in. Margins look like a salon’s. You’ve bought the harder job.

**7. Tenant turnover & vacancy days**  
Pros leave for cheaper rent, a breakup with a booth neighbor, burnout, or a traditional salon signing bonus. Make-ready speed and waitlist quality decide whether turnover is a hiccup or a hole.

**8. Rent collection & delinquency**  
Weekly cadence helps feedback — until someone stops paying and still has clients in the chair. Soft landlords become unpaid partners. Hard landlords without process become reputation problems in a gossipy trade.

**9. Hair-heavy cannibalization**  
Too many identical services on one floor → internal competition, drama, and tenants who blame *you* for soft books. Mix matters.

**10. Utility & maintenance load**  
Hot water, HVAC for blow-dryers, clogged sinks, laundry machines, after-hours lockouts. “No employees” still means vendors and sometimes *you* on Saturday.

**11. Safety / night comfort (especially for women tenants and clients)**  
Brian’s YT framing is blunt: provide a clean space people are comfortable going to when it’s dark. Lighting, cameras, access control, parking — non-negotiable in many markets. Fail this and recruiting dies regardless of rent.

**12. Site / trade-area mistakes**  
Wrong co-tenancy, no parking, invisible box, weak density of licensed pros, or a market already saturated with suite landlords. Site selection software and brand maps help; they don’t invent demand.

**13. Competition & free-month wars**  
Suite density is rising in many metros. Commodity rent races destroy the premium story. Differentiation is finishes, safety, culture, and ops — not only $/week on a Facebook ad.

**14. Financing / TI dependency**  
Models that only work with max TI and perfect SBA terms are fragile. When the landlord’s allowance shrinks or rates move, equity checks balloon.

**15. Tax / depreciation stories as the thesis**  
Bonus depreciation and related structures show up in Brian’s Image Studios–class education (with explicit “not financial advice”). Buying a weak occupancy plan for a write-off is how people own an empty marble hallway. The investment has to stand on rent collected.

**16. Confusing “beauty is booming” with “your suites stay occupied at a rent that clears debt after amenities”**  
Same trap as every demand-story industry. Macro vanity ≠ your Monday delinquency report.

**17. Thinking zero employees means zero work**  
Labor-light ≠ effort-light during lease-up, turns, and collections. Passive myths die on a holiday weekend flood in suite 12.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means salon suites is a **real-estate + amenities + occupancy** business where buildout, lease structure, and tenant recruiting decide whether the “no employees” brochure survives contact with the bank.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You think like a **landlord / developer** more than a creative salon director  
- You’re capitalized for heavy TI / buildout + months of soft occupancy — or you’re buying a stabilized book with clean rent rolls  
- You can recruit beauty pros (or partner with someone who can) without becoming their boss  
- Lease docs, collections, vendors, and amenity CapEx energize you more than cutting hair  
- You’re willing to hold landlord identity when tenants push you to “just run it like a salon”  
- You’ve compared this door to [property-management](/models/property-management) (residential fee ops), [fitness-gym](/models/fitness-gym) (membership facility), and [med-spa](/models/med-spa) (clinical retail) and still want **suite rent** as the product

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You wanted a semi-absentee “beauty prints money” story from month one of a dark building  
- You’re undercapitalized for construction + lease-up  
- You actually want to **operate** a salon, med spa, wax shop, or gym — different underwriting  
- You hate conflict, collections, or construction punch lists  
- You’re allergic to commercial lease risk and personal guarantees  
- You thought “no employees” meant “no phone and no Saturdays” during fill and turns

Franchise vs independent vs buying a stabilized indie building is a separate call.  
Same physics either way: CapEx, occupancy, leases, amenities, recruiting. A brand is only worth the fee if it shortens site, build, and fill — not because the corridor photographs well.

Many operators will correctly choose a **lighter** beauty door (single suite as a *tenant*, mobile beauty, or a different facility model) first. That’s a valid read, not a lack of ambition.

## Risks (the short list)

- Buildout overruns / delayed CO → cash crunch before first rent  
- Slow lease-up / sub-breakeven occupancy with debt still due  
- Bad master lease / weak TI / restoration surprises  
- Amenities CapEx race without rent power  
- Can’t recruit / keep quality tenants → permanent vacancy holes  
- Landlord–operator blur reintroduces labor and drama  
- Delinquency + weak lease enforcement  
- Hair-cannibalized tenant mix → culture and churn  
- Utility / flood / safety incidents  
- Free-month competition compressing rents  
- Tax-driven purchase without occupancy thesis  
- Royalty / brand drag without fill or RE leverage  
- Confusing industry boom headlines with utilized suites after debt

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “everyone needs a haircut.”

You’re buying the right to run a **suite-occupancy machine** — if you can underwrite and survive the buildout, structure leases that don’t sink you, fill and refill rooms with beauty pros who pay on time, keep amenities worth the premium, and stay a **landlord** when the easier emotional move is to become a salon boss again.

Some people should develop one building, learn lease-up scars, then duplicate.  
Some people should buy a tired suite property and fix occupancy, collections, and amenity reality.  
Some people should rent a *single suite as a tenant* (Hello Sugar / goGlow–class adjacency) and never take the landlord CapEx.  
Some people should walk — and put the capital into [property-management](/models/property-management), [fitness-gym](/models/fitness-gym), [med-spa](/models/med-spa), a trade, or a different vehicle entirely.

The question isn’t “do stylists want independence?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a high-CapEx commercial lease with thirty tenant relationships, an amenities arms race, and a bank that doesn’t care that suite 7’s best colorist moved to the cheaper building across the highway?**

If you want help comparing doors (this landlord model vs other health-wellness facilities vs real-estate-ops paths), that’s a conversation.  
Not a pitch deck.
