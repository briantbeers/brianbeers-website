The doorbell cam is dead and the app won’t open.  
A realtor’s buyer wants cameras, locks, and an alarm that actually talks to someone at 2 a.m.  
A new-build owner wants lights, shades, Wi-Fi, video, and security controlled from one phone — not six apps and a drawer of remotes.

Someone has to **consult**, **sell a scope**, **pull low-voltage (and sometimes coordinate line-voltage)**, **program the system**, **train the homeowner**, and decide whether money comes from **the install**, **the monitoring residual**, or both.

That’s **smart home / security install**.

Not a big-box Ring kit as a side hustle.  
Not “I hang cameras on weekends” as the ownership model.  
Not a pure software company.

A local company that turns **fear, convenience, and home value** into **sold systems + installed labor + (often) a recurring monitoring book** — and lives or dies on lead economics, tech quality, contracts, and what DIY already ate.

Brian’s lesser-known franchises list ends with this category for a reason:

Adoption is rising.  
Technology keeps changing.  
There still aren’t “a lot of companies” doing full-home automation well in most markets — *and* that does not mean the residual myth pays the bills in month three.

Simple on a whiteboard.  
Brutal if you confuse “people buy cameras” with “I own a residual machine.”

## What the business actually is

You’re a **consult → sell → install → (often) monitor / maintain** company wearing a smart-home or security brand.

Two cousins live under the same roof on this page — same trucks, different P&L stories:

| Lane | What it feels like |
|------|---------------------|
| **Security / alarm + cameras** | Fear and insurance-adjacent demand; panels, sensors, video, locks; often a **monitoring residual** (or a one-time “self-monitor / local only” sale). National dealer / franchise energy (Vivint-style, ADT-dealer-style, Guardian / SafeStreets-style) *or* indie alarm shops. |
| **Full smart-home / home automation** | Lights, shades, audio/video, Wi-Fi networks, climate scenes, security as *one layer* — consult-heavy, tech-churn-heavy, higher design theater. Brian’s Saavi / Savvy Homes teaching: go in, show what’s possible, install, maintain, upgrade as the stack evolves. |

Most real companies mix both. Diligence which one actually funds the week.

Brian’s teaching loop for the automation end of the category (and what installers actually book):

1. **Lead / consult** — in-home or virtual; walk rooms; map what already exists (old alarm, Ring cams, builder Wi-Fi junk)  
2. **Design & propose** — cameras, alarm, locks, lighting/shades/AV/network as the scope allows; good / better / best; financing common on bigger tickets  
3. **Close & contract** — equipment, labor, monitoring term (if any), early-termination / equipment buyout language, chargeback / cancelation rules  
4. **Install & program** — mounts, cable or wireless, hubs, app, scenes; punch list; homeowner training  
5. **Activate / monitor / service** — central station or platform onboarding; service calls; upgrades when cameras die or Wi-Fi changes  
6. **Retain or replace** — residual book health *or* one-time install + service-plan attach if you don’t own the monitoring

Menu shape in the category:

- **Burglar / intrusion alarm** — panels, contacts, motions, glass-break, cellular communicators  
- **Video** — doorbells, outdoor cams, NVR / cloud, video verification (where offered)  
- **Smart locks / access** — residential and light multifamily / small commercial  
- **Smart devices / scenes** — lights, thermostats, shades/blinds motors, garage, water sensors  
- **Network / Wi-Fi** — the silent failure mode; bad Wi-Fi = “your system sucks” reviews  
- **AV / whole-home automation** — higher-end integrator lane (Brian’s Saavi framing: audio, video, security, Wi-Fi, blinds/shades, lighting)  
- **Commercial / light commercial** — cameras, access, alarm for offices, retail, HOA common areas (different sales cycle; often denser RMR)  
- **Takeovers / takeovers-of-monitoring** — existing hardware, new monitoring or reprogram — often cheaper CAC than a full new install

**Scope lock for this page:** residential (and light commercial) **smart-home / electronic security** company that **generates leads, sells systems, installs, and optionally owns or shares monitoring residuals**. Franchise, dealer, or indie integrator — same category physics.

**Out of scope (different models):**  
- Pure DIY ecommerce / Amazon kit reseller with no install muscle  
- Pure **guard / manned security** (labor shift model — different page someday)  
- Pure **line-voltage electrical** as the whole company (panels, EV, generators — see **electrical**; smart-home wiring often *touches* that trade)  
- Handyman hanging a doorbell cam (see **handyman** — adjacency, not this P&L)  
- Pure MSP / cybersecurity consulting for SMBs (related fear story — different delivery)  
- National brand HQ as a career path (you’re evaluating *local ownership / dealer / franchise / indie*, not a corporate AE job)

**Electrical / handyman adjacency (don’t confuse them):**  
Licensed electricians own **line-voltage** upgrades; many smart installs are **low-voltage** plus platform programming — until you need a new circuit, a panel, or an inspector. Handymen may mount a cam; they rarely own monitoring contracts, false-alarm ordinances, or central-station relationships. Crossing license lines is how shops meet boards, insurers, and lawsuits.

Named systems exist across the spectrum: national security brands and dealers (Vivint-style, ADT-dealer-style, Guardian, SafeStreets, and peers), indie alarm shops, and full-home automation franchises / integrators (Saavi / Savvy Homes as Brian’s published example). Treat them as **proof the category franchises, dealers, and professionalizes** — not as the point of this page. Brian’s public **250 Franchise Ideas** sheet puts **Smart Home Installation** next to Vivint / SafeStreets / Guardian as names that exist.

Brian’s ownership frame that maps here:

- You’re not buying “cameras are popular.” You’re buying a **sales + install + (maybe) residual** machine.  
- Recurring monitoring *can* be the asset — **if** you actually own clean contracts, low attrition, and CAC that fits LTV. Residual folklore without that stack is a hobby.  
- Tech love is required for the automation lane (his Saavi closer: you have to *love* technology and showing people what’s possible). Security-sales lanes can skew more “closer + install crew” — still not passive.  
- Semi-absentee from month one is fantasy while you’re the demo, the closer, and the callback tech.

## How money comes in

You sell **installed systems** and, in many models, **monthly monitoring / service** — two cash qualities that get falsely treated as one.

| Mix | What it feels like |
|-----|---------------------|
| **Install / equipment / project labor** | Upfront cash (or financed). Hardware COGS, truck time, programming, punch. Project economics — must resell next month. |
| **Monitoring / RMR / residuals** | Monthly fees per account; often wholesale central-station cost underneath. Compounding *if* attrition stays sane and you keep the contractual rights. |
| **Service / upgrade / battery / camera refresh** | Truck rolls, add-on devices, Wi-Fi rescue, “add two more cams.” Keeps the book alive between new sales. |
| **One-time / self-monitor / no residual** | Cleaner cancelation politics; weaker exit story; you’re closer to a project contractor. |
| **Dealer / franchise residual splits** | You may *earn* a residual without *owning* the account book the way an indie RMR shop does. Diligence who owns the customer on paper. |

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Lead → demo → close** — this category eats marketing; weak process = expensive tire-kickers  
2. **Average sold system** — doorbell-only vs whole-home security vs automation package  
3. **Monitoring attach + retention** — sold residual that cancels in month four is vanity RMR  
4. **Who owns the residual** — you, the franchisor, the platform, the dealer agreement  
5. **Install quality / first-trip completion** — callbacks and “won’t arm” tickets destroy margin and Google  
6. **Tech utilization** — windshield, reschedules, “homeowner not home,” programming rabbit holes  
7. **Chargeback / cancelation / cooling-off discipline** — financed deals and door-to-door energy make this existential in some channels  
8. **Mix** — all DIY-priced camera jobs with no residual vs all long-cycle automation with thin lead flow  
9. **Commercial / takeover attach** (when earned) — denser RMR, different sales muscle

Gross margin lives in **price − equipment − install labor − sales commission − central-station / platform fees − warranty / truck rolls − chargebacks**.  
Then overhead: lead gen (often brutal), CRM, licensing/bonds/insurance, office/dispatch, training, vehicle wraps, and (if franchised/dealer) royalty / brand / lead fees / residual haircuts.

Industry shape (education only, not Brian forecasts): monitoring accounts are often discussed in roughly the **tens of dollars per month** per residential account (platform and package dependent); wholesale monitoring pass-through is a small slice of that when outsourced. Install tickets range from **consumer doorbell/camera packages in the low hundreds–low thousands** up through **multi-thousand security packages** and **five-figure+ whole-home automation**. Valuation folklore in the alarm world still centers on **multiples of monthly RMR** for clean monitored books — while install-heavy integrators trade more like contractors on **EBITDA**. Confusing those two exit stories is how buyers overpay for a van full of cameras with no contracts.

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Homes keep adding **connected devices**.  
Crime fear, package theft, insurance conversations, and “I travel for work” keep **cameras and alarms** in the budget for a slice of households.  
New builds and remodels create **integration moments** (network, lighting, shades, locks).  
Old systems go obsolete; cellular communicators replace aging paths; cameras fail; Wi-Fi gets rebuilt — **service and refresh** never fully stop.

Brian’s Saavi framing: growing market, not crowded with great full-home operators, and systems that can add perceived value when a house sells.  
His electrical teaching also names **smart-home** work as an upgrade attach next to panels, generators, and EV — demand exists adjacent to the trades.

Macro does **not** erase DIY giants, national ad budgets, or the fact that a $99 doorbell is now a default grocery-aisle purchase.

## Micro: what actually drives one company

**Sales machine**  
Appointments set, show rates, close rates, average ticket, financing mix, attach rate to monitoring. Without this, “residuals” never start.

**Lead economics**  
Google / LSA / SEO, brand, realtor / builder / insurance agent referrals, home shows, door-to-door or event teams (channel-dependent), takeover campaigns, past-customer upgrades. National brands spend fortunes; locals win on **trust, niche (new construction, luxury automation, commercial cameras), and referral density** — not on outspending ADT/Vivint-style budgets.

**Install / programming quality**  
Straight cams, labeled cables, documented passwords, trained homeowners, clean app experience. Ugly installs become chargebacks and one-star reviews.

**Contract & compliance posture**  
Alarm business licenses, electrician relationships where required, false-alarm ordinances, permits, consumer-contract rules, TCPA / marketing consent if you run outbound. Boring paperwork is the moat DIY doesn’t care about — until the city does.

**Residual / platform stack**  
Central station or monitoring partner, dealer agreement terms, attrition reporting, who can port the account. Residual myths die in the fine print.

**People**  
Closers who can demo without lying; techs who can program *and* talk to homeowners; CS that saves cancels. Tech churn (employees *and* platforms) is the operating tax.

**Owner time**  
Early stage: pipeline, hiring, QA on installs, chargeback fights, licensing. Not “set residual and sail.”

**Systems**  
CRM, proposal tools, scheduling, inventory of kits, password vaults / customer documentation, review engine, cancel-save playbooks.

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

**Startup / investment shape (education):**

- Brian’s broad home-services franchise conversations often land roughly **~$100k–$200k** in related teaching — *category* band, not this model’s quote. Smart-home / security dealer and franchise launches vary hard with **vehicle, demo kit inventory, licensing, launch marketing, and working capital for chargebacks / install float**.  
- Public franchise aggregator reads for commercial-leaning electronic security concepts (education shape only) sometimes show mid-six-figure Item-7 neighborhoods; residential dealer / franchise paths can sit lower or higher depending on inventory and marketing requirements. Always verify current FDD / dealer agreement.  
- Independents trade royalty for building brand, monitoring relationships, and recruiting themselves — CapEx and CAC are still real.  
- Royalty / brand / lead / technology fees (if franchised or dealer) are system-specific; residual **splits** can matter more than the headline royalty.

**Operating intuition (industry education, not forecasts):**

- Residential monitoring is commonly discussed around roughly **~$20–$60+/month** per account depending on video, cellular, and package — not a universal price.  
- Wholesale central-station costs are often a **few dollars to low teens** per account per month in outsourced residential bands (commercial/video verification higher) — the spread is the residual story *before* CAC, attrition, and overhead.  
- Clean monitored books are often talked about in **~35×–50×+ monthly RMR** acquisition folklore (quality, attrition, commercial mix, and contract terms move that hard). Install-heavy integrators are more often framed on **~4×–8×+ EBITDA**-style contractor math.  
- DIY packages train consumers that “security” can be **sub-$300 hardware + cheap or optional monitoring** — your CAC and close rate must target buyers who want **professional install, integration, or reliable response**, not the Costco impulse cam.  
- Attrition bands discussed in industry writing often sit roughly **high-single-digit to low-double-digit %/year** residential (better shops and commercial mixes can do better) — “residuals forever” without a cancel-save muscle is fiction.

Diligence questions that matter more than memorizing averages:

- Install revenue vs monitoring revenue last 12 months? Gross margin by stream?  
- Who **owns** the RMR on paper (you vs franchisor vs platform)? Portability on exit?  
- Monthly attrition / cancel reasons? Chargeback rate on financed deals?  
- CAC by channel vs average first-year gross profit (install + residual)?  
- Close rate on set demos? Average sold system? Monitoring attach rate?  
- First-trip completion / callback rate? “Won’t connect / bad Wi-Fi” ticket volume?  
- Tech / closer vacancy and time-to-hire? Certification / licensing requirements locally?  
- False-alarm / municipal permit posture? Insurance and bonding?  
- If dealer/franchised: residual split, lead fees, brand fund, required marketing, non-competes  
- Platform / equipment roadmap — what bricks in 24 months?

What that means in plain English:

The *category* can stack project cash **and** a compounding monitoring book.  
A *specific* company can look “recurring” on a pitch deck and still be a high-CAC install shop with rented residuals and a chargeback problem.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

**1. CAC that doesn’t fit the residual myth**  
National brands and DIY ads set consumer expectations. Local paid search for “home security near me” is expensive; tire-kickers who want a $99 cam will burn your closer’s calendar. If your cost to acquire a monitored account exceeds early gross profit (install margin + months of residual), you’re buying vanity RMR.

**2. Monitoring residual myths**  
“Recurring” is not “passive,” not “owned,” and not “worth 40×” just because a podcast said so. Residuals that you don’t contractually own, that cancel after the promo, or that sit on a platform you can’t port are **rented hope**. Diligence ownership, attrition, and who the customer thinks they bought.

**3. Tech churn — people *and* platforms**  
Installers and programmers leave. Hubs get discontinued. Apps change. Cameras brick after a firmware cycle. Brian’s automation teaching celebrates “upgrade as new things come along” — that is also your **callback and retraining tax**. Love of technology without change management is how owners live in attics.

**4. DIY Ring / Nest / big-box competition**  
A huge slice of “I want cameras” demand is already satisfied by self-install kits and optional cheap monitoring. You’re not competing for every doorbell. You’re competing for **integrated systems, professional install, insurance-meaningful monitoring, complex properties, and customers who hate DIY failure modes**. Pitch the wrong buyer and you lose on price every time.

**5. Licensing, ordinances, and line-voltage edges**  
Alarm contractor licenses, monitoring business licenses, municipal alarm permits, false-alarm fee regimes, and “when does this become electrical work?” vary by state and city. Ignoring that isn’t scrappy — it’s how you fund someone else’s fine or lawsuit. Handyman energy dies here.

**6. Chargebacks, cooling-off, and financed-deal blowups**  
Long contracts + equipment subsidies + aggressive outbound channels create cancelation and payment disputes. One bad month of chargebacks can erase a quarter of install profit. Contract clarity, demo honesty, and save teams are operating systems — not legal afterthoughts.

**7. Lead gen dependency / appointment quality**  
This is a **sales calendar** business. Feast/famine on demos, no-shows, and “just curious” appointments destroy utilization. Referral and builder/realtor channels help; they don’t replace process.

**8. Install quality and homeowner training debt**  
A system the customer can’t arm is a cancel waiting to happen. Password chaos, dead batteries, and “which app?” support tickets are margin leaks.

**9. Wi-Fi / network as the silent villain**  
Half the “security company failed” reviews are network problems. If you won’t own network hygiene (or partner cleanly), your brand absorbs the blame.

**10. Residual split / dealer fine print**  
Some paths pay you a residual without giving you an asset you can sell cleanly. Some claw back residuals on cancel. Some own the customer relationship. Read the agreement like it’s the business — because it might be.

**11. False alarms and response credibility**  
Police and ordinance friction, verification requirements, and “cry wolf” history damage retention and municipal goodwill. Video verification and discipline cost money; skipping them costs the book.

**12. Hiring closers and techs who don’t create liability**  
High-pressure sales cultures can juice month-one revenue and nuke chargebacks and reviews. Techs who can’t communicate create support hell. Values hiring matters in a trust category.

**13. Working capital on install float + marketing burn**  
Equipment, payroll, and lead spend hit before residuals mature. Undercapitalized shops look busy and bounce when chargebacks and slow seasons stack.

**14. Semi-absentee fantasy**  
A mature book with managers, QA, and a save team can create owner leverage. A greenfield with you as demo + closer + programmer is a job with a residual spreadsheet.

**15. Franchise / dealer stack (if you go that route)**  
Fees + brand rules + lead programs vs training, monitoring platform, and peer playbooks. Worth it only if the system improves **close rate, install QA, and residual quality** — not because the wrap looks national.

**16. Scope creep into “we do everything smart”**  
Lighting, shades, AV, network, alarm, solar-adjacent toys — each SKU adds training and warranty surface. Brian’s full-automation story is exciting; undisciplined menus bankrupt calendars.

**17. Confusing this with electrical or handyman**  
Line-voltage upgrades and punch-list hanging are adjacent skills, different licenses, different tickets, different residual stories. Buy the wrong vehicle and you inherit the wrong problems.

**18. Exit-story confusion**  
Buyers pay RMR multiples for **clean monitored contracts**. They pay contractor multiples for **install revenue**. Selling yourself “recurring” while living on project cash is how diligence ends early.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means smart-home / security install is a **real lead-gen + install + (optional) residual business** — not a passive camera annuity and not a Ring reseller with a logo.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You like **consultative selling** and can demo without overpromising  
- You’re willing to grind **lead quality and close process**, not just “buy ads”  
- You’re honest about **contracts, licensing, and residual ownership** — the unsexy half of the model  
- Technology change energizes you (especially in the automation lane) *or* you’ll hire people who stay current  
- You can target customers DIY can’t serve well (integration, response, complexity, commercial)  
- You’re capitalized for **CAC + install float + early attrition/chargebacks**, not just the franchise/dealer fee  
- Control via a sales-and-service machine beats a pure craft job or a pure stock portfolio story for you

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want passive / semi-absentee from month one  
- You think “residuals” means you can skip sales forever  
- You’re allergic to consumer contracts, chargebacks, and municipal paperwork  
- You’ll only compete on price with DIY kits  
- You freeze when platforms deprecate hardware or a tech quits mid-schedule  
- You’re actually shopping **electrical service** economics (panels/EV) or **handyman** dispatch — adjacent, not this page  
- Your only “why” is a multiple-of-RMR screenshot with no attrition math

Franchise vs dealer vs independent is a separate call.  
Same category either way: generate qualified demand → sell a scope the customer understands → install clean → activate what you actually own → retain the book → survive tech and channel change.

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “everyone wants cameras.”

You’re buying the right to run a **lead-gen + install company** that may also compound a **monitoring residual book** — if you can win the right customers against DIY and national spend, install systems people can live with, stay licensed, survive chargebacks, and own (or honestly account for) the recurring piece.

Some people should lean **security + monitoring** and obsess over attrition and contract quality.  
Some people should lean **full-home automation** (Brian’s Saavi-shaped lane) and accept tech churn as the product.  
Some people should start as a **dealer / franchise** for platform and playbooks — and still diligence residual ownership like it’s the asset.  
Some people should stay **indie integrators** and win on luxury / builder / commercial niches.  
Some people should walk — and put the same capital into **electrical**, **handyman**, or another vehicle with a calendar and risk shape they can actually own.

The question isn’t “do houses want smart devices?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a stressful sales-and-callback company wearing a residual story that only works if CAC, contracts, and tech discipline all hold?**
