Grease that builds until a fire marshal cares.  
A hood that has to be clean **before** the kitchen opens for lunch.  
A restaurant that only notices you when the sticker is expired — or when the duct catches.

How long do you wait — and who shows up on a **night contract**, not a daytime pressure-wash route with a Facebook lead?

There’s a lot of things a kitchen can put off.  
“Fail the fire inspection / lose insurance / shut the line” is not one of them when the AHJ (authority having jurisdiction) walks in with a depth gauge.

That’s **commercial kitchen hood cleaning**.

Not a janitorial crew wiping stainless for shine.  
Not pure **fryer-oil filtration** routes (Filta-style adjacency — same commercial kitchen door, different primary job).  
Not a one-off “we’ll degrease your hood once” weekend side hustle.

A local **B2B compliance + night-crew** company that wins recurring exhaust contracts, staffs greasy height/chemical work after hours, and lives or dies on **whether documentation, labor, insurance, and account concentration hold — month after month**.

Simple on a whiteboard: dirty hoods → scheduled clean → sticker / report → renew.  
Brutal if you confuse “fire code makes them buy” with “easy sticky money.”

## What the business actually is

You’re a **commercial kitchen exhaust / hood cleaning** company — selling **compliance contracts**, not bi-weekly house cleans or generic facility mopping.

Typical menu (general commercial exhaust — not residential maid or pure janitorial):

1. **Full kitchen exhaust / hood system cleaning** — the spine: hood, filters, ducts, fans cleaned to a fire-code standard (NFPA 96 / local adoption), with photos and reports  
2. **Filter exchange / soak programs** — recurring filter cycles that keep you in the kitchen between deep cleans  
3. **Access / light technical work** — hinge kits, access panels, fan belts, consulting that turns a cleaner into a kitchen-exhaust partner  
4. **Adjacency attach** — steam / vapor degreasing, conveyor oven cleaning, pad / dumpster-area wash, select pressure washing (some books; diligence what’s core vs noise)  
5. **Multi-site / chain accounts** — same brand, many kitchens; pricing and QA get harder, stickiness and concentration risk both rise

Typical loop:

1. **Lead** — outbound to restaurant / facility / property managers, health & fire-inspection fallout, referrals, brand sales (franchise-fed), prior customers expanding sites  
2. **Walkthrough + scope** — hood count, duct access, cooking volume / fuel type, roof access, after-hours windows, what’s *in* vs *out* of the contract  
3. **Bid / present** — price per clean or annualized contract against incumbents; documentation quality is part of the product  
4. **Win + schedule** — lock frequency to inspection/cooking cadence; keys / alarms / night access; startup photos  
5. **Staff the nights** — crews who climb, chemically degrease, and work over hot lines after close  
6. **Document + retain** — stickers, written reports, before/after photos for the fire marshal and the insurance file  
7. **Collect / renew / expand** — filter programs, sister sites, light repairs; re-price when wage and chemical costs move

**Scope lock for this page:** branded (or independent) **commercial kitchen hood / exhaust cleaning** built on **B2B recurring contracts**, night (and sometimes early-morning) crews, compliance documentation, and retention of sticky but price- and access-sensitive kitchen accounts. Fryer-oil filtration and general commercial janitorial appear as **adjacent doors** — call them out in diligence; don’t collapse them into “we clean restaurants.”

**Out of scope (different models):**  
- Pure **commercial janitorial / office night crews** (trash, restrooms, floors — **[commercial janitorial](/models/commercial-janitorial)**)  
- Pure **residential cleaning**  
- Pure **pressure washing / soft wash** exterior routes (**[pressure washing](/models/pressure-washing)**) — some hood companies attach pad wash; different sales spine  
- Pure **restoration** (water/fire — different channel and capital — **[restoration](/models/restoration)**)  
- Pure **fryer-oil filtration** as the whole company story (Filta-style — same kitchen door, different primary COGS and crew skill)  
- Solo “I degrease hoods cash after midnight” as the whole *company* story

**Light contrast with commercial janitorial (don’t collapse the two):**

| | **Commercial janitorial** | **Commercial hood cleaning** |

| Customer | Buildings / facility managers | Commercial kitchens / F&B ops + facilities |

## How money comes in

You sell **fulfilled compliance visits / annualized service on a book of commercial kitchen accounts**, not “hours on a timesheet” the customer sees as a maid visit — and not a one-time degrease that never returns.

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Contract book size** — active kitchens × frequency × price per clean (or annualized contract value)  
2. **Frequency mix** — solid-fuel / high-volume kitchens pull more visits than low-volume churches or seasonal lines (NFPA-style inspection cadence education below)  
3. **Account retention / bid loss** — sticky *if* you show up documented; one lost multi-unit chain punches a hole payroll can’t instantly shrink  
4. **Labor % by account** — burdened night wages vs contract price; overtime, no-shows, and “kitchen still open” delays quietly kill the book  
5. **Attach mix** — filter programs, light repairs, steam, pad wash vs pure hood cleans  
6. **Price discipline** — racing the uninsured low bidder fills vans and empties margin *and* raises your insurance story  
7. **Sales cycle + pipeline** — kitchen decision-makers, GM turnover, and corporate procurement can stretch closes; working capital must survive the wait  
8. **Documentation as product** — photos, reports, stickers aren’t paperwork theater — they’re why the customer renews when the marshal asks

Gross margin lives in **contract price − tech labor (burdened) − chemicals / disposal (− truck / lift / access costs)**.  
Then overhead: specialized vehicles, **insurance that actually covers grease / height / chemical / property damage**, workers’ comp, training/certification, CRM / scheduling, sales payroll or lead vendors, and (if franchised) royalty + brand/MAP + tech fees.

Revenue mix usually looks like some blend of:

| Channel | What it feels like |
|--------|---------------------|
| **Scheduled hood / exhaust cleans** | The spine. Compliance-driven. Sticky if documented. |
| **Filter exchange / soak programs** | Higher visit frequency; keeps relationship warm between deep cleans. |
| **Light technical / access / repairs** | Margin and trust when staffed; not every book can do it safely. |
| **Steam / vapor / oven / pad attach** | Useful attach; feast/famine if it becomes the identity. |
| **Multi-site / chain portfolios** | Scale + **concentration risk**; one decision-maker can add or cut many kitchens. |

Industry shape (education only): Brian’s Kitchen Guard feature cites corporate locations generating **over ~$7M in 2023 sales across two sites** in that brand’s Item 19 teaching frame — *their* numbers, not your target. Public FDD aggregator reads for large hood brands (HOODZ peer set) often discuss **six-figure average unit sales** bands and **high-single to low-double-digit royalty** stacks — always pull the **current** FDD. Healthy operators watch **visits completed vs scheduled**, **report turnaround**, **tech utilization**, **account concentration**, and **insurance cost as % of revenue** as hard as “new logos won.” Compliance demand is real. **Night labor + liability + concentration are the tax.**

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Commercial kitchens still produce grease-laden vapor. Exhaust systems still accumulate combustible deposits. Fire codes, insurers, and local marshals still care — whether the tenant is a QSR, hotel banquet kitchen, school cafeteria, hospital dietary, casino, or senior-center kitchen.

When the economy is good, kitchens cook harder and need cleaner systems more often.  
When the economy is tight, restaurants **still can’t legally ignore grease depth and expired stickers** as easily as they can skip a lobby polish — **closures and bankruptcies are the real demand shock**, not “we’ll just stop cleaning forever.”

That’s why Brian groups commercial kitchen services with other “boring / essential / recurring” B2B lines on the franchise-idea sheet: the need doesn’t vanish; **your crew, your insurance, and your concentration can**.

The constraint isn’t “hoods get greasy.”

It’s **reliable night crews who can work height + chemicals + documentation + honest account-level pricing + working capital through a B2B sales cycle** in *your* metro’s restaurant density.

Easy entry (ladder + degreaser + a low sticker) keeps competition loud at the bottom. Systems, certifications acceptable to the AHJ, show-up rate, photo reports, and insurance that survives a claim are how a *company* separates from a person with a truck and a midnight key.

Restaurant churn / concept failure is a real macro headwind. Operators who lean **institutional kitchens** (schools, healthcare, hospitality, corporate campuses) alongside independents treat that as portfolio design — not as “restaurants always refill.”

## Micro: what actually drives one company

**Compliance cadence + documentation**  
NFPA 96 education (commonly adopted / referenced by local fire codes): inspection frequency scales with cooking volume — roughly **monthly** for solid-fuel, **quarterly** for high-volume (24-hour / charbroil / wok teaching examples), **semiannual** for moderate-volume, **annual** for low-volume (churches, day camps, seasonal, many senior centers in the standard’s examples). Cleaning triggers when grease exceeds depth limits — in busy kitchens, clean and inspect often travel together. Your product is **being the vendor whose report and sticker the kitchen trusts when the marshal shows up**.

**Night-crew recruiting, quality & retention**  
Brian’s blunt framing across service businesses: **people are the #1 challenge**. Hood work adds nights, grease, chemicals, roofs, and “the line still needs to open at 10 a.m.” Culture and stable routes beat “we’ll pay a dollar more” — until a competitor does both *and* carries better insurance.

**Height + chemical + access reality**  
Ladders, roof hatches, fans, duct access panels, caustic chemistry, slip hazards, hot equipment. This is not office vacuuming with a badge. Training, PPE, and “who is allowed on that roof” are operating systems — not HR fluff.

**Insurance that matches the work**  
GL, workers’ comp, and specialty coverages for property damage / pollution / height are not optional theater. One claim that your policy doesn’t actually cover is existential. Amateurs undercutting you on price often undercut you on coverage too — until the claim finds the grown-up with the wrap.

**Contract sales (not “post and pray”)**  
You’re selling to GMs, owners, regional facility directors, and sometimes procurement. Relationships, inspection fear, and proof beat a pretty logo. Long closes and GM turnover are normal. A book built only on inbound “hood cleaning near me” is fragile.

**Account-level math & concentration**  
Company-wide “labor looks fine” can hide one underpriced high-volume kitchen that eats three easy accounts. Multi-unit chains feel like scale until one RFP or bankruptcy removes 20% of revenue overnight.

**Owner role (honest version)**  
Early stage: pipeline, walkthroughs, pricing, hiring for new routes, cash, customer saves, and often backup night / documentation hero.  
Not “I bought a compliance logo and went fishing.”  
Same ownership muscle elsewhere: **hire the people who climb; run the business that keeps accounts *and* crews**.

**Control spectrum (if franchised)**  
Named category systems (**HOODZ**, **Kitchen Guard**, **Filta** and peers) sit in the broader market as recognizable commercial-kitchen service architectures — exhaust-led, multi-stream exhaust, or oil-led with kitchen attach. Use them as **category maps for diligence**, not endorsements. Read the current FDD: who sells, who services, who owns the customer at renewal, what royalties / tech fees do to net, and whether “recurring” is contractually real or just route hope.

**Systems**  
Scheduling that matches cooking volume, photo/report workflows, chemical inventory, key/alarm control, claim escalation, and re-bid calendars. Guesswork doesn’t scale past one heroic night tech.

A *specific book* can look full every night and still be broke — or quiet because it can’t staff the wins it already sold.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

**1. Hiring and retaining night crews who will do height + grease + chemicals**  
Brian’s #1 across service ops — louder at 1 a.m. on a greasy roof. Demand without reliable people is missed cleans, angry GMs, failed stickers, and an owner on the ladder.

**2. Chemical / height / access risk is the job, not a rare edge case**  
PPE, roof protocols, duct access, caustic chemistry, slip and burn hazards. One serious injury or property-damage event rewrites your insurance and your reputation.

**3. Insurance is expensive — and amateurs still undercut you**  
You carry real coverage; the low bidder often doesn’t. Price pressure from underinsured competitors is both competition *and* why facility managers are skeptical until the claim hits.

**4. Contract sales are slower and more political than “Google ads for pressure washing”**  
You’re selling compliance and trust to operators who change GMs, blame vendors for failed inspections, and shop price when corporate sends an RFP. Pipeline discipline matters.

**5. Account concentration**  
One multi-unit chain or one big institutional campus can be most of the book. Sticky until the RFP, the bankruptcy, or the regional vendor swap.

**6. Restaurant / kitchen churn**  
Concepts fail. Hours cut. Ownership flips. Your “recurring” revenue is only as stable as the kitchens still cooking — and still paying.

**7. Missed cleans / bad documentation = churn *and* liability**  
Customers leave when stickers lapse or reports don’t show up for the marshal. Systems that catch misses are the retention product.

**8. Scope creep without change orders**  
“Can you also do the second hood / the new fryer line / the dumpster pad?” starts as helpful and ends as free night work. Contracts that don’t get re-cut when kitchens remodel are silent margin death.

**9. Wage + chemical + disposal inflation vs stale contract pricing**  
Multi-year agreements that don’t escalate as fast as costs quietly convert sticky revenue into a nonprofit.

**10. Scheduling around kitchen close times**  
Crews wait; kitchens run late; overtime appears. Utilization math is brutal when half the metro closes at once.

**11. Easy-entry noise at the bottom**  
Anyone with a ladder and a low number can *look* like a competitor by Monday. You carry training, insurance, disposal discipline, and photo standards. Differentiate or race to the bottom.

**12. Sales engine that outruns staffing**  
A hot closer (or franchise lead flow) can fill a book fast. If hiring and QA can’t keep up, you bought churn (and maybe a royalty) with a night shift attached.

**13. Certification / AHJ acceptance ambiguity**  
Who is “acceptable” to the local fire authority varies. Marketing claims that outrun what your market’s marshals accept are a diligence landmine.

**14. Owner still on the roof (or still the only closer / documentation backup)**  
If the company only works when you’re the emergency tech and the bid desk, you own a job with a wrap — Brian’s hire-the-tech frame applies here too.

**15. Semi-absentee fantasy on thin early books**  
Manager salary on a thin contract base is how shops bleed. Semi-absentee usually needs layered ops and a mature book — not month-one lifestyle.

**16. Franchise stack drag (if you go that route)**  
Royalty + MAP + tech fees on a liability-heavy, labor-heavy P&L. Worth it only if the system fills profitable kitchens and helps you keep crews — not because HOODZ / Kitchen Guard / Filta (or any peer) looks familiar on a brochure.

**17. Buying a tired book without account-level P&Ls and claims history**  
Customer lists look like assets until post-close re-bids, open claims, and wage gaps reveal you bought revenue without margin. Verify contracts, insurance, documentation quality, and concentration — not “we’ve cleaned 10,000 hoods historically.”

**18. Confusing “fire code forever” with “easy sticky money”**  
Timeless compliance demand is real. Your P&L still needs operators who can win the labor war, the insurance war, the sales war, and the documentation war at the same time — often at night.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means commercial hood cleaning is a **real contract + night-labor + liability + concentration business** where **compliance revenue only shows up as wealth when kitchens stay open, crews show up, reports land, and insurance doesn’t eat the year** — and low differentiation means systems and price courage do the hard work brand fantasy won’t.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re wired to hire, schedule, and lead field crews (Brian’s hire-the-tech lens) — including **nights**, grease, and height — and respect that culture beats wage-only retention  
- B2B contract puzzles (scope, compliance proof, account P&Ls, renewals) excite you more than daytime residential route charm  
- You’re okay competing on documentation, professionalism, and reliability in a market with loud underinsured low bidders  
- Matching **kitchens and crews** at the same time sounds like the job — because it is  
- Liability and insurance diligence feel like adult work you’re willing to own  
- Building layers (lead tech → ops) so you’re not forever on the roof sounds like the point  
- Comparing **exhaust-led vs oil-led vs multi-stream kitchen service** structures sounds like diligence you want to do with eyes open

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want passive / semi-absentee from month one  
- You refuse to recruit or manage people who climb greasy systems after midnight  
- You’re allergic to insurance, claims, and “soft” labor issues and hoped for a pure tech/product business  
- You need high-margin one-off project spikes and hate recurring contract rhythm (cabinets / roofing energy ≠ this day-to-day)  
- You’re undercapitalized for specialized vehicles, insurance, chemicals, and a B2B sales cycle  
- You want pure **office janitorial** economics — that’s the [commercial janitorial](/models/commercial-janitorial) spine, not this one  
- You’re looking for a craft job with a logo (you *being* the only tech forever)  
- You’re chasing a brand brochure without stomach for night ops and concentration risk

Franchise vs independent is a separate call.  
Same category either way: win the kitchen → staff the nights → clean and document to standard → retain the account **and** the crew → keep labor and insurance honest → compound the book.

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “hoods get greasy.”

You’re buying the right to run a **compliance-contract + night-crew machine** that turns fire-code need into a renewing B2B kitchen book — if you can win scopes without racing underinsured amateurs, staff greasy height work people trust after hours, deliver documentation that survives the marshal, and keep labor + insurance from eating the margin everyone soft-pedaled in the brochure.

Some people should start greenfield with a handful of kitchens and prove account-level math before adding trucks.  
Some people should buy a tired local exhaust book (or a franchise resale) and install documentation + recruiting + re-pricing discipline.  
Some people should compare **exhaust-led systems** (HOODZ / Kitchen Guard peer set as category maps) against **oil-led kitchen service** (Filta peer set) and **independent** builds — and pick the job they actually want.  
Some people should walk — and put the same capital into [commercial janitorial](/models/commercial-janitorial), [pressure washing](/models/pressure-washing), [restoration](/models/restoration), or another vehicle with a labor or liability surface they can actually sleep with.

The question isn’t “do kitchens need hoods cleaned?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a stressful night-grease company that only works when you’re the roof backup, the bid desk, and the documentation department on thin, concentrated contracts?**
