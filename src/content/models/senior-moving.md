Mom’s moving into assisted living on Thursday.

The kids flew in for one weekend.  
Half the furniture goes to the new place.  
Half goes to siblings’ basements three states away.  
The china set nobody wants still has to leave the house so the realtor can list.

That’s **senior / residential moving**.

Not placement (you’re not matching facilities).  
Not an estate sale (you’re not taking a cut of what sells).  
Not junk removal (you’re not paid to throw the life away — you’re paid to **carry the keepers**).

Labor + trucks.  
Quotes, crews, padding, stairs, elevators, and the moment someone says “that scratch wasn’t there.”

Simple on a whiteboard.  
Hard in real life — because physical work, damage risk, summer chaos, and partner dependency all show up on the same invoice.

---

## What the business actually is

You’re a **local move operator**: estimate the job, staff the crew, move the goods, collect the check, hope the claim file stays quiet.

This page covers **senior transition moves** and everyday **residential** (and light commercial) local moving — the book that placement advisors, realtors, estate-sale operators, and Google all feed.

Typical menu:

1. **Local residential moves** — apartment / house to house; hourly or flat packages  
2. **Senior / downsizing moves** — keepers into AL / IL / memory care / kids’ homes; slower pace, more packing, more emotion  
3. **Packing / unpacking / setup** — often the margin and the trust play on senior jobs  
4. **Loading / unloading only** — DIY trucks, PODS-style containers, storage  
5. **Small commercial / office** — nights and weekends; different insurance and elevator politics  
6. **Storage attach** (some books) — warehouse or partner facility; capital and utilization story of its own  
7. **Junk / haul-away attach** (some dual brands) — different P&L (disposal); don’t pretend it’s free margin

Typical loop:

1. **Lead** — Google / LSA, brand call center, realtor, placement advisor, estate-sale / senior-move-manager partner, facility discharge, prior customer  
2. **Survey** — in-home or video; stairs, long carries, specialty items, parking, building rules  
3. **Quote / deposit** — hourly vs binding; what’s *not* included; valuation / coverage options  
4. **Dispatch** — trucks + crew sized to the job; peak season math gets ugly  
5. **Move day** — protect, load, transport, unload, basic placement  
6. **Collect / follow-up** — card on site; claim window; review ask  
7. **Optional attach** — pack-out, setup Grandma’s new room, haul leftovers to junk partner, refer estate sale for what stays behind

**Scope lock:** this is **moving as the company** — trucks, crews, utilization, claims.  
It is **not** senior placement / referral (facility success fees — different page).  
It is **not** estate sales / household liquidation (commission on sell-through — different page).  
It is **not** in-home caregiving.  
It is **not** pure junk removal (haul-to-landfill economics — adjacent; see junk-removal).  
It is **not** interstate van-line agent complexity as the whole story (different capital, authority, and claim stack — mention only as diligence if the book has it).

Brian’s teaching frames that touch this category:

- Newsletter *A Modern Moving Company*: people keep moving (upsizing, downsizing, apartments, offices); industry is **fragmented**; average ticket can sit around **~$2k** in his education pass; named systems prove the category franchises  
- Same piece: corporate proof unit (one modern brand) cited **first-12-month** numbers as education — revenue / operating income / margin shape, not your forecast  
- Same piece: largest legacy brand’s **metro** units cited around **~$3.2M** average sales (2022-era teaching) — category ceiling intuition, not a promise  
- *Print money senior* stack puts **Estate Sales & Relocation** next to placement: sort → **relocate keepers** → sell / donate / trash the rest — movers often sit in that film as partners or as the truck half of a transition brand  
- Transition franchises (Caring Transitions–type category proof on YT) sometimes **bundle** move + setup + sale; pure movers sell the transport; don’t confuse the P&Ls  
- Named systems exist: **Two Men and a Truck**, **College Hunks** (junk + moving dual books), senior **move managers** / transition brands, and newer “experience” movers. Treat logos as **proof the category franchises** — not as the point of this page

## How money comes in

You sell **completed moves**, not “hours on a timesheet” the customer romanticizes — even though labor hours (and overtime) are how you deliver.

Pricing shapes you’ll see:

- **Hourly** local (crew size × hours + travel / materials)  
- **Flat / binding** for surveyed local jobs  
- **Packing** as separate line items (senior books lean here)  
- **Valuation / coverage** upgrades (and the claim culture that follows)  
- **Long-distance / interstate** (if you offer it) — weight or container pricing; different beast

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Booked jobs** — phone answered, survey done, deposit taken, calendar filled  
2. **Average ticket / mix** — studio vs whole house; senior pack-heavy vs “labor only”  
3. **Crew + truck utilization** — billable move hours vs empty windshield, no-shows, and “waiting on the elevator”  
4. **Labor % (burdened)** — wages, OT, workers’ comp, uniforms; the silent margin killer  
5. **Claims cost** — repairs, replacements, goodwill; reputation is a marketing line item too  
6. **Referral partner engine** — placement, realtors, estate sales, facilities, move managers  
7. **Seasonality hedge** — commercial, storage, packing, junk attach, winter promotions — or you pray for May–August forever

Gross margin lives in **price − crew labor − fuel − materials − claims (− commissions)**.  
Then overhead: truck leases / notes, commercial auto + cargo + liability, dispatch / call handling, warehouse if you store, marketing, software, and (if franchised) royalty + brand/MAP + tech.

This is a **utilization + trust + claims** business.  
Not a facility waitlist fee. Not a commission on Grandma’s china.

**Light contrast (same aging stack, different check):**

| Model | Who pays you | What you “sell” | Overhead feel |
|-------|--------------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Placement** | Usually the facility (success fee) | A move-in that sticks | Lean; relationship / tours |
| **Estate sale** | Commission off household sell-through (+ fees) | Liquidation of stuff | Lean capital; **heavy labor days** |
| **Moving** | Family / estate (job fee) | Safe transport of keepers | **Trucks, crews, damage risk** |

Brian puts relocation next to placement and estate sales for a reason: the family who just placed Mom often needs the house emptied *and* the keepers delivered. Partnership, not the same P&L.

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

People move. Always.

Upsizing. Downsizing. Divorce. Job change. Apartment churn. Office resets.  
And the **Silver Tsunami** Brian named in the senior thread: millions aging into facilities and smaller footprints — every placement creates a **pile of keepers with a deadline**.

Housing turnover and senior transitions feed local movers even when national long-haul headlines cool.

Demand for *someone trustworthy with a truck and a crew* is structural.

The constraint isn’t “are there moves?”

It’s **labor you can schedule + trucks you can afford + claims you can survive + partners who send work in the slow months** in *your* market.

National brands exist. Independents exist. Dual junk+moving brands exist. Senior move managers exist (often referring *to* movers or bundling lightly).  
Same category. Different operating reality.

## Micro: what actually drives one territory

**Lead partners**  
Placement advisors, senior realtors, estate-sale / transition operators, facility move-in coordinators, elder-law adjacent pros, prior families.  
No partner engine → feast/famine on Google and price shoppers who will burn you on claims.

**Survey honesty**  
Stairs, long carries, pianos, marble tops, HOA / dock rules, “we’ll pack ourselves” that becomes “help us pack on move day.” Under-scoped jobs eat labor and create damage.

**Crew supply**  
Reliable movers who show up, lift safely, and don’t treat the house like a demolition site. Peak summer hiring is a meat grinder. Training shortcuts show up as claims in August.

**Truck CapEx / utilization**  
Box trucks are the factory. Under-fleet → you sell days you can’t run. Over-fleet → notes eat January. Maintenance, wraps, insurance, and downtime are real.

**Dispatch discipline**  
Two small jobs across town with a third “quick add” is how you miss evening cutoffs and generate bad reviews. Density beats heroics.

**Claims culture**  
Document condition. Train padding. Resolve fast. Operators who fight every scratch buy lawyer hours and one-star reviews. Operators who over-pay every soft claim train customers to file.

**Owner role (honest version)**  
Early stage: you *are* estimating, hiring, riding jobs, handling angry texts at 9pm.  
Semi-absentee fairy tales are how people buy a truck and a Facebook page.  
Brian’s modern-moving newsletter framed franchisee-style ownership as **culture + driving the business + managing the manager** — not as “never touch a truck” from day one. Earn that.

**Senior niche skill (optional but powerful)**  
Pace, packing, labeling, room setup, coordinating with facilities, patience with adult children. Commodity hourly movers lose these jobs to operators who understand the transition stack.

**Adjacent stack**  
Placement, estate sales, junk removal, in-home care, senior transport. You don’t need to own them. You do need to know who you send overflow to — and who sends *you* keepers.

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

**Operating intuition (education — not forecasts):**

- Brian’s newsletter education pass put a **~$2k average ticket** as a category feel for modern local moving — mix and metro move this hard  
- Category operator benchmarks often push **crew labor under ~30% of revenue** as a healthy target; many books run higher when OT and poor scoping creep in  
- **Claims** discipline conversations often aim **under ~1% of revenue** — spikes usually mean training / survey failure, not bad luck alone  
- **Seasonality:** industry chatter commonly frames **~40–60% of annual volume** in the late-spring / summer window — winter tests working capital and partner channels  
- Dual junk+moving public P&L shapes (education): truck labor often lands high-**20s to ~30%** of revenue in mature disclosure tables — useful for intuition, not your budget

**Startup / investment shape (education — verify current FDD Item 7 / indie quotes):**

- Brian’s *A Modern Moving Company* (2023-era teaching) cited one system’s single-territory band roughly **~$140k–$197k** and two-territory roughly **~$165k–$282k** all-in — office in an industrial area, truck financing packages exist; **verify current docs**  
- His Notion franchisor card for that same modern brand later showed investment roughly **~$131k–$191k** (card fields drift; never memorize one screenshot)  
- Legacy brand public aggregator reads for a large national mover often land roughly **mid-five to low-mid six figures** Item 7 depending on fleet size (e.g. bands discussed ~**$90k–$250k+**) — system-specific  
- Dual junk+moving brands can sit higher all-in once multiple trucks and dual marketing are real  
- Independents can start leaner (one truck, insurance, crew, wrap) and still get crushed by claims or seasonality without reserves

**Illustrative disclosure / teaching shapes (not your forecast):**

- Brian cited one corporate proof unit’s **first 12 months**: roughly **$678k** revenue, **$116k** operating income, **~17%** net margin — *their* launch, *their* market, *their* year  
- Same newsletter: metro units at the largest legacy brand averaged about **$3.2M** sales (2022 teaching) — top-end category shape, wide spread underneath  
- Dual-brand public Item 19-style tables show **wide** mature vs early-unit spreads — same logo, different execution

Your diligence questions should sound like:

- Jobs per week in peak vs off-peak? Average ticket? Packing % of revenue?  
- Labor % (burdened) and OT pattern last 24 months?  
- Claims $ and % of revenue — and average days to close a claim?  
- % of revenue from top 3 referral partners (placement / realtor / estate)?  
- Truck count, age, lease vs own, revenue per truck per week?  
- Survey close rate by lead source?  
- Senior / facility mix vs commodity apartment churn?  
- Insurance: cargo limits, valuation offerings, workers’ comp mod?  
- If franchised: royalty + brand fund + call-center / tech fees — do they actually win jobs and protect brand trust?  
- Owner hours on truck vs on hiring / estimating — is this a transferable company?

Same brain you’d use buying any field-service business: utilization, labor, claims, partner concentration, and whether *you* want to live inside other people’s move-day stress for years.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

This section is the point. Soft marketing skips it. Operators live it.

**1. Labor is the product — and the bottleneck**  
Trucks don’t move sofas. People do. No-shows, turnover, and summer wage wars turn a full calendar into refunds and one-star reviews.

**2. Damage claims**  
One broken antique, one scratched floor, one “missing” box can erase the job’s margin and the partner who sent you. Documentation and training beat charm.

**3. Seasonality**  
May–August can carry the year. January can starve you while leases and insurance still clear. If your book is 100% residential peak, you’re a weather + school-calendar business.

**4. Referral dependency**  
Placement and realtor partners send beautiful senior jobs — until they switch vendors or retire. Concentration risk is real. Google-only books compete on price with every truck in town.

**5. Truck CapEx and maintenance**  
Notes, wraps, tires, downtime, commercial auto. Under-trucked you can’t scale; over-trucked quiet Tuesdays hurt. This is not “laptop and a phone” overhead.

**6. Under-scoped surveys**  
“Should be a 4-hour job” becomes 9 hours with OT, a second trip, and a tired crew making mistakes. Honesty at survey is profit protection.

**7. Peak-season quality collapse**  
You hire fast in April. August claims spike. Training time disappears exactly when volume peaks — the structural trap of the category.

**8. Workers’ comp and injury**  
Stairs, wet driveways, heavy lifts. Injury rates in physical move work are not theoretical. Safety culture is a P&L line.

**9. Price war with uninsured amateurs**  
Craigslist crews undercut. You carry insurance, payroll tax, and real claim exposure. Racing them is how professionals go broke.

**10. Cash timing vs deposits**  
Deposits help; big jobs still front labor and fuel. Chargebacks and “Dad said he’d pay” family fights happen on senior moves.

**11. Building / facility friction**  
Elevator reservations, certificates of insurance, loading docks, HOA rules. Miss one form and the crew sits unpaid.

**12. Scope creep into estate / junk**  
Families want the whole transition. Pure movers who fake liquidation or landfill economics inherit problems they didn’t underwrite — partner instead.

**13. Long-distance / interstate temptation**  
Bigger tickets, uglier claims, authority, and broker games. Local density businesses die chasing glamorous highway revenue without systems.

**14. Owner stuck on the truck**  
No estimators, no dispatcher, no recruiting → you bought a physically exhausting job with a logo. Brian’s ownership frame cares whether you can eventually **manage the manager**.

**15. Franchise stack (if you go that route)**  
Royalty + brand fund + required marketing vs call center, playbook, and hiring help. Worth it only if the system wins jobs *and* protects trust — not because the truck wrap looks friendly.

**16. Confusing “people always move” with “your trucks are full”**  
Macro demand is the backdrop. Your company is surveys, crews, utilization, and claims.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means senior / residential moving is a **real labor + CapEx + claims business**, not a demographic lottery ticket.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re okay with field-service ops — hiring, dispatch, ride-alongs, angry Saturday texts  
- You can build a local partner network (placement, realtors, estate / transition, facilities)  
- You’re calm in emotional family / senior settings  
- You’re capitalized for trucks, insurance, and slow seasons — or intentional about a lean one-truck ramp  
- You want a senior-industry vehicle **with** physical ops (contrast: placement’s light overhead)  
- You’re willing to live in survey accuracy, claims discipline, and labor % weekly — not annually

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want pure desk / relationship economics with no crew drama (look at **placement**)  
- You want commission-on-stuff economics without truck CapEx (look at **estate sales**)  
- You hate hiring and hate conflict  
- You’re looking for passive / “hire a manager and fish” from month one  
- You’re undercapitalized for truck notes + off-season burn  
- You’re actually shopping **junk removal** as the whole business (disposal P&L) and calling it moving  
- You can’t handle damage disputes or facility paperwork

Franchise vs independent is a separate call.  
Same category either way: labor, trucks, utilization, claims, partners.  
Franchise trades fee/royalty for playbook and brand — only if the system helps you win moves *and* staff them cleanly.

## Risks (the short list)

- Peak volume with green crews → claims spike  
- Off-season with fixed truck / insurance cost → cash crunch  
- Partner concentration → revenue cliff  
- Labor % drift / OT culture → busy and broke  
- Underinsured cargo or sloppy valuation → existential claim  
- Owner-on-truck forever → no transferable enterprise  
- Royalty drag without lead/system advantage  
- Thinking “Silver Tsunami” substitutes for dispatch skill  
- Blurring into junk / estate ops you didn’t price or insure for

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “people always move” or “aging population.”

You’re buying the right to stand between a family’s deadline and a pile of keepers — and get paid when the truck leaves full and arrives intact — if you can hire, survey honestly, utilize assets, and settle claims like an adult.

Some people should start with one truck and grind realtor / placement relationships.  
Some people should buy a tired book with trucks and a partner list and fix labor + claims.  
Some people should walk — and put the same capital into placement, estate sales, junk, a trade, or a different vehicle entirely.

The question isn’t “will there be more downsizers?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a summer of overtime and a winter of truck payments that only works when someone else’s crisis fills your calendar?**

If you want help comparing doors (this model vs estate sales vs placement vs junk vs others), that’s a conversation.  
Not a pitch deck.
