Drawers full of dead phones.  
Drawers full of chargers for phones that don’t exist anymore.

Someone upgrades.  
Someone’s kid cracks a screen.  
Someone needs cash *today* and doesn’t want to ship a device to a stranger and wait two weeks for a mail-in quote that “regrades” down after they open the box.

That’s the electronics / device **buy-sell** model.

Not a big-box carrier store pushing a three-year plan.  
Not a Facebook Marketplace handshake in a parking lot.  
Not a charity e-waste dump.

A retail counter (sometimes a small strip box) that **buys** phones, tablets, laptops, consoles, wearables — working, cracked, or “for parts” — from people walking in…  
and **sells** them — in-store, online, or both — to other people who want a device without paying full retail for brand-new.

Same door. Two jobs.  
Ticket sizes from a $40 accessory to an $800 laptop.

Same *family* as [used clothing resale](/models/used-clothing-resale), [kids clothing resale](/models/kids-clothing-resale), and [used sporting goods](/models/used-sporting-goods) — **buy from the public, price for speed, turn inventory** — except the SKU can brick itself overnight, get remotely locked, get stolen twice, and lose 20% of its sell-through value because Apple announced something on a Tuesday.

---

## What the business actually is

You’re running a curated secondhand **device** shop — often with a heavy **online** sell-through lane.

Glass cases of phones and tablets.  
A back counter for testing, wiping, grading, and listing.  
Maybe a wall of consoles, headphones, cameras, smartwatches.  
Sometimes a repair / parts lane. Sometimes not.

Sellers bring junk-drawer tech and last year’s flagship.  
You ID the person (secondhand / pawn-adjacent rules are real in a lot of markets).  
You test. You grade. You price.  
You pay cash or store credit for what you want.  
You wipe data. You list. You ship or hang it. You turn it.

The loop:

**Buy (or trade) devices from the public → test / grade / wipe → price → sell (retail + online) → buy more.**

Three flavors exist in the wild:

1. **Local buy-sell-trade retail** — walk-in cash today, sell in-store and/or online. Stronger systems lean on **pricing software** so staff aren’t guessing Samsung market prices from memory.
2. **Mail-in / app / kiosk buyback** — Gazelle / Decluttr / ecoATM-style category proof that households *will* sell devices for cash. Different CapEx, different trust problem, different labor. Not the same as owning a strip-center door.
3. **Indie phone / console / “we buy phones” shops** — same physics, thinner brand, more owner eye, more Wild West on fraud and grading.

Brian’s framing on the adult clothing sister model still applies here: this family works best when you **take title**, you own the turn, and bad buys are *your* problem — which is why **testing discipline** and **live pricing** matter more than a neon “WE BUY PHONES” sign.

Category proof (not the point of the page): national mail-in and kiosk players exist because drawer inventory is real. Franchised and independent **local** buy-sell electronics stores exist because a lot of people still want cash *today*, a human face, and someone who wipes the phone in front of them. Independents and other brands play the same game with different software and less brand awareness.

You’re not inventing phones.  
You’re moving devices that already exist — faster than Marketplace flaking, cleaner than a pawn vibe, cheaper than walking into a carrier store for everything new.

## How money comes in

Gross margin lives in the **spread** — and in whether the device you bought is still worth what your spreadsheet said when you paid cash.

You buy a phone for $180.  
You sell it for $280 online three days later.

You buy a cracked unit for $40 “for parts.”  
You harvest / repair / wholesale it for $90.

You take a laptop on trade for $200 store credit.  
You sell it for $450 after testing and a wipe.

You also sell a $25 case or charger because the buyer is already in the store and needs it *tonight*.

Multiply by thousands of serial numbers — and by whether it’s launch week (supply flood + price cliff) or mid-cycle (calmer, thinner volume).

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Buy right against a live market** — yesterday’s “fair” buy is today’s underwater inventory when wholesale comps move  
2. **Test / grade before cash leaves** — cosmetic lies; function and lock status decide whether you own an asset or a brick  
3. **Price for speed** — devices that sit are cash stuck in lithium and glass, *and* they’re depreciating while they sit  
4. **Channel mix** — in-store retail tickets vs online marketplaces vs wholesale / parts. Many modern versions of this model lean hard online for sell-through while the storefront acts as a **collection + trust** machine  
5. **Two-sided traffic** — sellers *and* buyers. Empty glass kills sell-through. Full glass with no listings / no shoppers kills cash  
6. **Trade vs cash** — store credit can pull the next purchase; cash buys need tighter pricing discipline and tighter fraud controls

In disclosed retail used-electronics systems, public Item 19s often show **gross sales** only — not a neat gross-margin % like apparel. Treat that as a diligence flag, not a vibe. Your real P&L still lives in:

- **COGS / buy discipline** (the whole game)  
- **Labor** at the counter *and* in the back (testing, wiping, listing, packing)  
- **Returns / warranty / regrades** (online especially)  
- **Marketplace fees / shipping / chargebacks**  
- **Rent** for a small box that still needs security and cameras  
- **Shrink / theft / cash risk**  
- **Royalty / ads / tech fees** if you’re in a franchise stack

You’re not a gadget influencer.  
You’re a **turns + risk-control** company wearing a phone case.

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Devices keep getting expensive. Upgrade cycles keep happening. Drawers keep filling.

Every fall a new class of phones ships.  
Every fall another class of “still fine” phones becomes trade-in bait.

Good times → people upgrade → junk drawers overflow → they need to sell.  
Tight times → people hunt value → they need to buy used / refurbished.

Supply and demand both show up. Just for different reasons.

Add the clothing / sporting contrast lightly:

Adult used clothing rides fashion cycles.  
Kids resale rides **biology** — outgrow is mandatory.  
Sporting goods rides seasons and abandoned hobbies.  
Electronics rides **upgrade cycles + breakage + price transparency** — and a secondary market that already proved itself at national mail-in / kiosk scale (Gazelle / Decluttr / ecoATM-style) even when local retail is a different vehicle.

Phones are a necessity for work and life. Carrier “new forever” pricing is high either way. A tested, wiped, warrantied used device is a real product if you buy it right.

That’s the engine. Not vibes. Not one viral TikTok unboxing (those help a weekend — they don’t build the store).

## Micro: what actually drives one store

A single location lives or dies on a few boring things:

**Trade area / device density**  
Enough households with mid-to-high device ownership, enough turnover, enough people who will drive for cash today instead of mailing it. Dense suburbs and urban corridors behave differently than thin rural boxes — same model, different buy desk.

**Location / lease**  
Often a smaller strip box than apparel or sporting goods (category teaching sizes commonly sit under ~2,000 sq ft; some “collection” spokes go smaller). Destination energy can work in a B location — but cameras, lighting, and cash/device security are not optional theater.  
(Retail box math — same quiet killer as clothing resale, just usually a smaller check.)

**Buy desk + testing system (or chaos)**  
Mom-and-pop “we buy phones” shops often live on the owner’s eye and a few bookmark tabs. Fragile. One buyer leaves — or one iCloud-locked phone slips through — and the week is gone.

Stronger versions use **software / playbooks** to tell staff:

- what the live retail / wholesale comps say *right now*  
- what to pay by model, storage, carrier lock, cosmetic grade, and function  
- what to price for speed across channels  
- when to markdown or wholesale out  
- how to log ID / serials for secondhand compliance

Taste still helps.  
Guesswork shouldn’t be the whole company — especially when the SKU can get remotely killed after you paid cash.

**Back-of-house labor (the invisible factory)**  
Front smiles sell trust. Back benches make the margin: test, wipe, grade, photo, list, pack, answer “not as described” tickets. Understaff the back and inventory piles up as “bought but not sellable.”

**Online execution**  
If your model leans online for sell-through, shipping speed, listing accuracy, returns handling, and fee math are the store. A pretty glass case with dead listings is a museum.

**Inventory as working capital + cash on hand**  
Opening inventory isn’t decoration — it’s product you’ll sell. In this category you also float **buy cash**. Underbuy and the counter looks dead. Overbuy (or overpay into a price cliff) and cash is trapped in last week’s comps.

**Owner involvement**  
Same lesson as the adult clothing live deal breakdown: remote / distracted ownership shows up in the numbers — especially when fraud, testing shortcuts, and stale pricing compound quietly. Same model. Different execution. Different outcome.

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

Franchise brands in this category that publish an Item 19 will show you **ranges** — usually **gross sales**, not a guarantee of what hits your bank account after buy mistakes, returns, rent, labor, marketplace fees, debt, and paying yourself.

Shape from one large retail used-electronics buy-sell system’s recent disclosure (education only — PayMore-style category proof — always pull the current FDD for any brand you’re actually considering):

- **Franchise units (full-year cohort):** average gross sales roughly **~$999k**, median closer to **~$914k** (2025 calendar year, dozens of stores in the pack)  
- **Mature units (open 24+ months):** average closer to **~$1.19M** (ramp is real; new stores and mature stores are not the same animal)  
- **Range in that pack:** lows can sit near **~$360k**; highs can clear **~$1.8M+** — same brand, wildly different doors  
- **Royalty / fee stack:** often around **~5%** of gross sales royalty, plus brand marketing / tech / local ad commitments that can stack into several more points — read Item 6 like an adult  
- **New build investment band:** roughly **~$140k–$270k** all-in in a recent Item 7 (Brian’s public ballpark on the category has sat ~**$150k–$260k**) — driven by franchise fee, fixtures/counters, tech, opening inventory / buy cash, deposits, and working capital  
- **Footprint:** teaching sizes often **~700–2,000 sq ft** strip / vanilla-box; some markets run hub-and-spoke (bigger listing/shipping hub + smaller buy spokes)

What that means in plain English:

The *model* can work.  
A *specific store* can be a dog or a rocket.

Your job in diligence isn’t to memorize the average.  
It’s to ask: **where does this unit sit in the pack, and can I move it?**

Same spreadsheet brain you’d use on adult used-clothing, kids resale, or sporting goods:

- location / device density / security / parking  
- system quality (pricing tools, testing SOPs, fraud controls, online playbooks)  
- growth potential from *this* baseline  
- multi-unit / hub-and-spoke proof (do operators already stack 2–3–5?)  
- existing vs new build math  
- is it worth *your* cash vs a different vehicle entirely

Also say this out loud: many disclosures in this niche show **sales**, not **owner earnings**. If someone waves an AUV and skips returns, warranty burns, chargebacks, and buy errors — they’re selling a story, not a P&L.

## Biggest challenges

Say these out loud before you fall in love with the glass case.

**Fraud / stolen / locked devices (the career-ender class)**  
Activation locks. Carrier blacklists. Serials that don’t match. “My cousin’s phone.” ID theater. Devices that test clean at 2 p.m. and brick at 9 p.m. Secondhand / pawn reporting rules vary by city and state — ignore them and you don’t just lose a phone, you inherit a compliance problem. Software helps. Process helps. A bored teenager with a cash drawer is not a fraud department.

**Testing labor (the invisible tax)**  
Every unit needs function checks, cosmetic grading, data wipe, photos, listing copy, packaging. Skip a step and you eat returns. Do every step slowly and labor eats the spread. Peak hours = people wanting cash *now* while the back room is still clearing yesterday’s queue. This is not “hang a hoodie and hope.”

**Price volatility (the weekly cliff)**  
New launches, carrier promotions, refurbished floods, wholesale comps swinging overnight. You can buy “right” at noon and be wrong by Friday. Apparel fashion risk is real; device **comp risk** is faster and more public. If your buy tool is stale, you’re inventory-funding someone else’s margin.

**Warranty / returns / “not as described”**  
Online buyers expect pristine. Used devices are not pristine. Battery health, mic issues, Face ID weirdness, “it was dropped after you shipped” disputes — chargebacks and return shipping can erase three good buys. A short store warranty sounds friendly until it becomes a second COGS line.

**Theft / shrink / cash risk**  
High-value, pocketable SKUs. Busy counters. Cash buyouts. Employee shrink and customer shrink both show up. Cameras, serial logging, dual-control cash, and limited on-hand buy cash aren’t paranoia — they’re the job. Founder chatter in this category obsesses over cash handling for a reason.

**Online competition (you don’t own the customer’s comparison tab)**  
Swappa, eBay, Facebook Marketplace, carrier trade-in promos, Amazon renewed, Gazelle / Decluttr-style mail-in quotes, Best Buy trade-in, indie phone shops down the road. Sellers price-check you. Buyers price-check you. Your edge is **cash today, trust, wipe-in-front-of-them, speed, and curation** — not “we also have phones.” If you’re slower than mail-in and colder than Marketplace and more expensive than a graded online listing with free returns, you lose both sides of the door.

**Repair / parts temptation**  
Broken buys can be margin *or* a graveyard of half-finished boards. Repair skill is a moat until it becomes a time sink that blocks listing velocity. Decide if you’re a turns shop with optional repair — or a repair shop that also buys. Mixing them without systems creates zombie inventory.

**Lease / buildout (smaller, not free)**  
Lower square footage than sporting goods doesn’t mean free. Security hardware, fixtures, POS, and working capital still add up. A cheap lease in a sketchy pocket can poison seller trust and insurance conversations.

**Compliance / KYC / local secondhand rules**  
ID capture, hold periods, police reporting, data sanitization standards — boring until they’re not. “We’re just a cool tech thrift” is not a legal strategy.

**Apparel / sporting contrast, one line:** clothing fights style cycles and size walls; sporting goods fights seasons and bulk; electronics fights **fraud, testing labor, warranty burns, and price charts that move while you’re at lunch**.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means device buy-sell is a **real retail + risk business**, not a “I like phones” side hustle.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You like retail energy *and* back-room process (people up front, systems in back)  
- You’re willing to live in inventory turns, live pricing, and fraud controls  
- Tech-adjacent detail doesn’t bore you — serials, grades, channel fees, shipping SLAs  
- You’re fine owning a local brand that wins on execution + trust, not a “set it and forget it” app  
- Multi-unit or hub-and-spoke (collection spokes + listing hub) sounds exciting, not exhausting  
- You can say **no** at the buy desk without needing to be the coolest person in the strip center

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want pure passive income  
- You hate managing people / part-time turnover / cash controls  
- You’re allergic to leases, theft risk, chargebacks, and buy-counter conflict  
- You need a blank-slate creative project (this is a playbook + compliance business, not an art project)  
- You think “everyone has old phones” replaces trade-area, software, and testing math  
- You confuse loving gadgets with loving **return tickets and price cliffs**

Also: [estate sales](/models/estate-sales) / household liquidation sometimes touch devices in one-off clearouts — different model. Resale retail is **ongoing turns**, not a three-day house sale. Carrier stores and pure repair shops are adjacent energy — not this.

## Risks (say them out loud)

- **Wrong buy / bad grade** → cash stuck in locked, dead, or overpaid devices  
- **Price cliff** → comps move after you paid; margin evaporates  
- **Fraud / stolen goods** → inventory loss + legal / reputational heat  
- **Warranty / return / chargeback bleed** → online volume up, profit flat  
- **Labor creep in testing / listing** → volume up, profit flat  
- **Theft / cash handling failure** → the quiet killer of “small” retail  
- **Lease / security buildout** → still real money in a “simple” box  
- **Distracted ownership** → same brand, bottom-quartile results  
- **Marketplace + mail-in + carrier trade-in leakage** → sellers skip you; buyers price-check you to death  
- **Franchise stack** (if you go that route) → royalties + rules + tech fees; you trade freedom for a system — make sure the system is worth it  
- **Disclosure footnotes** → “gross sales” is not what hits your personal bank account after you pay for bad buys, returns, rent, staff, the bank, and yourself

None of that means walk away by default.  
It means diligence is the product.

---

## The ownership point

You’re not buying phones.

You’re buying a machine that converts **junk-drawer upgrades and cracked-screen urgency** into cash flow — if you run the buy desk, the testing, the pricing, the channels, and the fraud controls.

Some people should start one.  
Some people should buy a tired one and fix the grading / online muscle.  
Some people should pair it with apparel or sporting-goods resale and catch a household’s closet *and* tech drawer.  
Some people should walk — and put the down payment into a different vehicle entirely.

The question isn’t “do you like gadgets?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or just a busier version of a job with more serial numbers?**

If you want help comparing doors (electronics buy-sell vs used clothing vs sporting goods vs something outside retail), that’s a conversation.  
Not a pitch deck.
