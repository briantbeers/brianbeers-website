Houses fade. Trim peels. HOAs send letters. Buyers walk a listing and flinch at chalky siding.

Someone has to quote it, schedule a crew, buy the paint, beat the weather, and stand behind the finish when a spot peels six months later.

That’s **exterior painting**.

Not a paint aisle.  
Not “I’ll brush your porch on Saturday.”  
A **quote → crew → materials margin** business that lives and dies on **labor schedules and weather windows**.

Simple on a whiteboard.  
Brutal if you confuse “people need paint” with “this runs itself.”

---

## What the business actually is

You sell exterior paint jobs (and often adjacent exterior work: decks, fences, trim packages, soft-wash prep, light commercial exteriors).

Typical loop:

1. **Lead** — Google, yard signs, door hangers, past customers, realtor / GC / roofer referrals, neighborhood canvass  
2. **Estimate** — measure, photograph, judge prep (scrape, caulk, prime), access (ladders, lifts), substrate condition  
3. **Quote / close** — fixed project price, timeline, material tier, color decisions, deposit  
4. **Schedule** — crew + materials + a weather window that actually holds  
5. **Produce** — prep is most of the hours; rolling paint is the visible part  
6. **Collect** — deposit / progress / final  
7. **Warranty** — callbacks and touch-ups; your name stays on the curb appeal

**Scope lock for this page:** contractor / franchise that **sells and installs** exterior paint jobs (residential focus; light commercial adjacent).  
Not a paint manufacturer or big-box retailer.  
Not pure interior (faster cycles, less weather — related model, different operating reality).  
Not “I own a ladder and a brush” solo craftsman as the whole company (craft job ≠ this page’s ownership math).

Brian’s teaching frame on the category:

- Painting sits in the **high-ticket, sales-driven home services** stack (with roofing, fencing, flooring, cabinets) — project tickets often framed in the **$5k / $10k / $30k+** conversation depending on scope  
- It’s a **people** business that happens to put paint on houses — he doesn’t need to love painting to own the machine  
- Asset-light painting territories often land roughly in the **~$100k–$200k** startup conversation (system-specific; verify Item 7)  
- Named systems exist (CertaPro, Five Star, That 1 Painter / ResiBrands family, others). Treat them as proof the category franchises — not as the point of this page

## How money comes in

You sell **jobs**, not hours on a timesheet the customer sees.

A single residential exterior can land anywhere from low-four to mid-five figures depending on size, stories, prep, material, and market.  
Brian’s public teaching has used **~$5,000** as a plain-English “average job” example when talking lead offers and neighborhood marketing — useful for intuition, not your forecast.

Revenue mix usually looks like some blend of:

| Channel | What it feels like |
|--------|---------------------|
| **Residential exterior** | Weather-bound. Prep-heavy. Peak spring–fall in most markets. |
| **Interior (if you take it)** | Weather hedge. More “in homes all day.” Different sales rhythm. |
| **Light commercial** | Bigger tickets, longer cycles, after-hours / access constraints. |
| **Repeat / referral** | Past customers paint more rooms later; exteriors come back years later when tastes or wear change. |

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Close rate on estimates** — estimating skill + sales process, not “more leads” alone  
2. **Average job size / attach** — trim, decks, second coat quality, related exterior  
3. **Crew utilization** — sold production days vs rain, no-shows, and waiting on materials  
4. **Materials discipline** — paint + sundries are real money, but usually **not** the majority of the job cost; waste and wrong product still kill jobs  
5. **Callbacks / warranty** — one bad peel on a street of Google reviewers  
6. **Mix** — exterior-only in a northern winter without a hedge is a cash-flow story, not a vibe

Gross margin lives in **price − materials − production labor − sales commission − warranty**.  
Then overhead: office/PM, software, insurance (yours), trucks/branding, local marketing, and (if franchised) royalty + brand fund.

Brian’s contribution-margin teaching on a **painter territory** (education shape, not a guarantee):

- Variable stack can look like roughly **~65%** of each revenue dollar (materials, contractors/crew, royalties, commissions, credit-card fees)  
- What’s left — roughly **~35% contribution** — fills the monthly fixed-cost “bucket” before anything is real profit  
- Illustrative materials in that teaching landed around the **low-teens %** of sales; royalty + brand fund often framed in the **mid-single digits + a couple points** (system-specific)

Industry shape outside Brian (education only): labor/prep dominates direct cost more than the can of paint; healthy operators watch **crew days and weather** as hard as “revenue.”

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Housing stock ages. Sun and rain don’t stop. Buyers, HOAs, and landlords keep forcing curb-appeal decisions.

When the economy is good, people refresh and upgrade.  
When the economy is tight, deferred maintenance eventually shows up as peeling paint and failed listings — demand doesn’t vanish; ticket size and mix shift.

That’s the “essential-ish home services” pitch.

The constraint isn’t “houses exist.”

It’s **estimating accuracy + crew capacity + weather windows + trust** in *your* territory.

Sunbelt markets stretch the exterior season.  
Northern markets punish exterior-only books every winter.  
Same category. Different operating reality.

## Micro: what actually drives one company

**Sales + estimating**  
Exterior painting is a project sale. Bad estimates (missed prep, wrong access, optimistic weather) become angry customers or zero margin.  
Brian’s fit teaching: if you’re good at sales, high-ticket project models (including painting) can leverage that. If sales interactions give you a panic attack, this stack will feel like punishment.

**Crew network**  
W-2 painters, 1099 crews, or a hybrid — all versions live or die on people who show up, prep properly, and don’t ghost mid-job.  
In Brian’s published launch notes on building a multi-territory painting book: the biggest early fear was **finding high-quality painters**; the operating thesis was a small number of **good crews per territory** against a serious revenue target — same idea as any production home service: capacity before ego.

**Weather & schedule density**  
Rain day ≠ “free day.” Crews, trucks, and promised finish dates don’t care that the sky changed.  
You win by stacking jobs geographically, having indoor/alternate work, or capitalizing for idle weeks.

**Lead engine**  
Digital ads help. So do boring tactics Brian teaches in home services: past-customer follow-up, yard signs, door hangers with a *real* offer relative to ticket size, and referral partners who already walk into the same houses (roofers, GCs, realtors, turf, etc.).

**Materials margin**  
You buy paint and sundries; you sell a finished exterior. Spread exists — but it’s usually smaller than the labor story. Manufacturer programs and waste control matter; “shopping the brand on the can” is not the P&L.

**Owner role (honest version)**  
Early stage: lead flow, estimating standards, sales leadership, production coordination, cash.  
Multi-unit “CEO” versions hire a regional operator so the owner is vision + people + capital — still not fishing from day one.  
Absentee with a weak sales + crew machine is how you buy a logo and a headache.

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

Ticket intuition:

- Residential exteriors: often **low four figures → mid five** by market / size / prep  
- Brian teaching example: **~$5,000** average-job framing for offer math and neighborhood marketing  
- National “typical house” indexes in public 2026 writeups often cluster mid-four figures — always verify *your* metro

Contribution / cost shape (Brian painter-territory teaching — education only):

- Variable ~**65%** → contribution ~**35%** before fixed  
- Materials illustrative ~**10–13%** of sales in that walkthrough  
- Royalty + brand fund often discussed around **~6–7% + ~2%** in franchise examples — **read the FDD**, not a blog

Startup / investment bands (category education):

- Brian’s repeated asset-light home-services band for painting-type concepts: roughly **~$100k–$200k** per territory  
- Public Item 7 aggregators for large painting franchisors often span roughly **~$80k–$320k** all-in depending on system and employee vs sub model (e.g. widely cited CertaPro / Five Star ranges — always pull current FDD Item 7)  
- Notion franchisor cards for individual brands can show tighter bands; treat them as **starting points to verify**, not gospel

Unit economics to diligence (ask these, don’t memorize averages):

- What’s the **exterior vs interior vs light commercial** mix last 24 months?  
- Close rate by lead source?  
- Average job size and deposit policy?  
- Materials % and burdened crew cost used in bids?  
- Crew utilization / rain-day policy?  
- Callback / warranty cost as % of revenue?  
- Working capital for off-season payroll and marketing?  
- If franchised: royalty + brand fund + required local spend; territory size and multi-unit rules (some systems culturally cap owners at a few territories)

What that means in plain English:

The *category* can work because tickets are real and materials aren’t the whole cost.  
A *specific office* can look busy all summer and still starve in March — or sell more than it can staff after a dry stretch of weather.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

**1. It’s a sales + estimating business wearing coveralls**  
If you can’t build a pipeline and quote accurately, the sprayers don’t matter.

**2. Weather feast / famine**  
Exterior peaks hard. Quiet / wet / frozen months expose whether you have interior mix, commercial work, or cash reserves.

**3. Crew capacity and quality**  
No painters = no production. Bad painters = callbacks that erase three good jobs. Hiring and keeping crews is the factory.

**4. Prep underestimation**  
The customer sees paint color. Your margin lives in scraping, caulking, priming, and masking. Miss prep → eat labor.

**5. Utilization vs promises**  
You sold a finish date. Then it rains four days. Communication and schedule density separate pros from Google one-stars.

**6. Callbacks / warranty / reputation**  
Peeling, flashing misses, overspray on the neighbor’s car. Exterior work is public. Reviews compound.

**7. Lead cost inflation**  
Google and lead mills get expensive. If you don’t own past-customer and referral muscles, CAC eats contribution.

**8. Price competition**  
Low differentiation. “Buddy with a brush” underbids. Winning on cheapest gallon is a race to the bottom.

**9. Compliance & liability**  
Heights, ladders, lifts, lead-safe rules on pre-1978 homes, workers’ comp. Boring until it isn’t.

**10. Materials swings and waste**  
Paint costs move; wrong sheen / wrong volume sits in the truck. Small % line, big when sloppy.

**11. Owner dependency**  
Sales energy and estimating standards don’t clone easily. Semi-absentee pitches in sales-heavy home services are a common trap in Brian’s teaching on the broader stack.

**12. Franchise stack (if you go that route)**  
Royalty + rules + territory limits vs playbook, brand, and peer network. Worth it only if the system helps you win jobs and crews — not because the truck wrap looks sharp.

**13. Confusing revenue with profit**  
A booked summer can still be thin after commissions, rain days, callbacks, and winter fixed costs.

**14. Scaling past the founder**  
Multi-crew / multi-territory needs PMs, sales managers, and crew leads. Otherwise you own a stressful estimating job with a logo.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means exterior painting is a **real project-sales + labor-schedule business**, not a weather lottery ticket.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re wired for sales leadership, estimating discipline, and pipeline follow-up  
- You’re okay managing crews (or a production manager) and weather-driven reschedules  
- You can hold a quality standard while moving volume  
- You’re capitalized for seasonality (or intentional about interior / commercial hedge)  
- High-ticket home services excite you more than recurring hourly routes

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want passive / semi-absentee from month one  
- You hate selling and hate hiring sellers  
- You’re looking for predictable weekly route revenue (think pest, lawn, senior care hours)  
- You’re undercapitalized for a wet spring or a dead winter  
- You want to *be* the painter on the ladder as the whole company (craft job ≠ this page’s model)  
- You’re actually shopping pure interior-only — related, but a different weather and sales rhythm

Franchise vs independent is a separate call.  
Same category either way: quote, crew, materials, weather, reputation.

---

## Risks (the short list)

- Sold more than you can produce in the weather window  
- Exterior-only book in a long winter  
- Crew no-shows / quality gaps → callbacks  
- Underpriced prep  
- Lead dependency on one paid channel  
- Liability / lead-safe miss  
- Royalty drag without lead/system advantage  
- Thinking “houses need paint” substitutes for operator skill

---

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “people hate peeling paint.”

You’re buying the right to run a **project-sales machine** that turns housing stock, trust, and crew capacity into finished exteriors — if you can quote, staff, schedule weather, and collect.

Some people should build exterior-first and add interior as a hedge.  
Some people should lean multi-territory with an operator seat and treat it like other home-services portfolios.  
Some people should walk — and put the same capital into a vehicle with cleaner recurring economics.

The question isn’t “do houses need paint?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a stressful schedule that only works when the sky cooperates and the crew shows up?**
