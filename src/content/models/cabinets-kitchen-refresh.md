The kitchen is where the money hides.

Not because everyone *must* remodel — they don’t.  
Because when they decide the oak from 1998 has to go, the ticket is real, the decision is emotional, and the project touches the room they live in every day.

That’s **cabinets / kitchen refresh**.

Not a lumber yard.  
Not a full general contractor who also “does kitchens.”  
Not “I paint cabinet doors on weekends” as the whole company.

A **design-to-install pipeline**: consult → sell a scope → order millwork / surfaces from partners → schedule install → stand behind the finish when a door warps or a seam shows.

High ticket.  
Long sales cycle.  
Partner-dependent production.

Simple on a whiteboard.  
Brutal if you confuse “people hate ugly kitchens” with “this runs itself.”

---

## What the business actually is

You sell kitchen (and often bath vanity) **cabinet work** — and frequently the attach that completes the look: countertops, hardware, sometimes light backsplash / related surfaces — without becoming a 20-trade remodel circus.

Typical product ladder in the category (operators mix differently):

1. **Refresh / refinish** — paint or recoat existing doors & boxes (lower ticket, faster cycle)  
2. **Reface** — new doors, drawer fronts, veneers, hardware; keep sound boxes (mid ticket, short install)  
3. **Replace** — new cabinets (stock / semi-custom / custom); layout may change  
4. **Cabinets + counters** — the “finish the room” ticket many niche systems prefer  
5. **Bath vanities / selective rooms** — same engine, smaller or adjacent tickets

Typical loop:

1. **Lead** — Google / SEO, showroom walk-in, past customer, realtor / GC referral, home show, neighborhood marketing  
2. **Consult / measure** — in-home or showroom; photos, linear feet of uppers/lowers, box condition, layout wish list  
3. **Design & propose** — good / better / best; 3D or catalog boards; deposit or paid design fee in stronger shops  
4. **Close** — financing, selections locked, change-order rules written *before* millwork is ordered  
5. **Order** — millwork partner / factory / national program; counters often a second supply chain  
6. **Wait** — lead times (often weeks; custom longer) while you manage expectations  
7. **Install** — W-2 crew or vetted subs; protect the home; punch list  
8. **Collect / warranty** — finals, reviews, callbacks; your name stays on the most-photographed room in the house

**Scope lock for this page:** company that **sells and delivers** cabinet / kitchen-refresh projects (residential focus; light multifamily / GC partner work adjacent). Design + project management + millwork partners + install orchestration.

**Out of scope (different models):**  
- Pure big-box “measure and hope” retail with no PM muscle  
- Full gut kitchen GC (plumbing, electrical, framing, floors as the *core* product)  
- Cabinet *manufacturing* plant as the business  
- Solo craftsman who *is* the company (craft job ≠ this page’s ownership math)

**Two honest lanes inside the category:**  
Some systems lean **refresh / reface** (speed, volume, approachable tickets).  
Some lean **new cabinets + counters** (higher ticket, longer millwork cycle, more showroom / design theater).  
Both are “kitchen.” The operating reality is not identical — diligence the mix.

Named systems exist (Kitchen Tune-Up, Bath Tune-Up, Cabinet IQ, Kitchen Solvers, others). Treat them as proof the category franchises — not as the point of this page.

Brian’s teaching frame on the category:

- Cabinets sit in the **high-ticket, sales-driven home services** stack with roofing, fencing, flooring, painting — project tickets often framed in the **$5k / $10k / $30k+** conversation depending on scope  
- Same **sales / marketing / closing KPIs** as other project home services; execution differs (millwork + design), accountability of sellers rhymes ([TsYBbpC5UA4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TsYBbpC5UA4))  
- Hard to hand off early as **semi-absentee**: owner energy for leads and closing doesn’t clone easily when sellers are near-100% commission ([NlTs68n-NvA](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NlTs68n-NvA))  
- Fit lens: if you’re willing to sell (or lead sellers) on big in-home decisions, this stack can fit; if you want route density and recurring hours, look elsewhere

## How money comes in

You sell **projects**, not hours the homeowner sees on a timesheet.

A refresh can land low-to-mid four figures.  
A reface often sits mid-four to low-five.  
A full cabinet replacement (especially with counters) commonly climbs mid-to-high five figures; premium / large kitchens go higher.  
Industry education (not Brian forecasts): many markets discuss refacing roughly **~$4k–$12k+** and replacement roughly **~$12k–$30k+** before counters/layout complexity — local labor and material tier move those bands hard.

Brian’s Cabinet IQ interview (one operator’s disclosed shape, **not** your P&L): average ticket discussed around **~$25k** (dragged by smaller vanity jobs); **$50k–$100k** sales not uncommon on a mid/high-end product story; pay-in-full before order to protect cash; materials + install labor framed around **~50%** of ticket → **~45–50%** gross in that conversation; corporate store examples cited in Item-19-style discussion (multi-million mature location; ~$1M first-year example) — **education only, verify any current FDD yourself**.

Revenue mix usually looks like some blend of:

| Channel | What it feels like |
|--------|---------------------|
| **Refresh / reface** | Faster cycle, more jobs, price-sensitive shoppers, install density matters. |
| **New cabinets ± counters** | Bigger tickets, longer millwork wait, design skill + showroom trust. |
| **Bath / vanity attach** | Smaller average, fills the book, same sellers/crews. |
| **GC / remodeler partner** | They bring clients; you stay niche on cabinets/counters; relationship sales. |
| **Showroom vs mobile-only** | Intent traffic vs go-to-the-house; rent vs reach tradeoff. |

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Close rate on qualified consults** — design confidence + process, not “more leads” alone  
2. **Average job size / attach** — counters, hardware, pantry, second bath, upgrade tier  
3. **Selection discipline** — locked specs before factory order; change orders priced or they eat you  
4. **Millwork reliability** — lead time promises you can keep; freight damage; wrong doors  
5. **Install quality + punch speed** — callbacks in the heart of the home destroy reviews  
6. **Cash terms** — deposit / progress / pay-before-order culture vs financing the factory yourself  
7. **Mix** — all-premium, long-cycle book without a refresh lane can starve the calendar

Gross margin lives in **price − millwork/materials − install labor − sales commission − warranty/rework**.  
Then overhead: design labor or software, showroom (if any), office/PM, insurance, local marketing, trucks, and (if franchised) royalty + brand fund + tech fees.

Industry shape (education only): healthy shops obsess over **sold → ordered → installed → collected** lag, not vanity “pipeline” screenshots. A busy design calendar with nothing shipping is a hobby with rent.

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Housing stock ages. Trends turn. Zillow photos punish dated oak. Empty-nesters refresh before listing. Buyers walk into a kitchen and decide the house in thirty seconds.

When the economy is good, people upgrade finishes and open layouts.  
When the economy is soft, full gut remodels pause first — **refresh / reface / “make it look new without moving the plumbing”** often lasts longer than luxury custom.

That’s the demand story.

The constraint isn’t “kitchens exist.”

It’s **trust + design close rate + millwork partners + install capacity** in *your* territory — on a **discretionary, emotional, long-cycle** purchase.

Unlike emergency plumbing, nobody’s kitchen “fails” at 11pm in a way that forces a same-week close.  
You live in the gap between “we should do something” and “we signed and paid.”

## Micro: what actually drives one company

**Sales + design as one motion**  
This isn’t storm canvass math. It’s consultative: measure, listen, budget tool or good/better/best, overcome “it depends,” get to a number without killing trust. Brian’s Cabinet IQ guest framed the pain of kitchen/bath retailers who shrug “it depends” at a homeowner staring at a **$10k vs $100k** fog — process that gives a fast, honest budget wins.

**Long sales cycle**  
Multiple visits. Spouse alignment. Financing. Material samples that sit on the counter for three weeks. HOA / landlord friction on rentals. Your CRM and follow-up cadence *are* the business.

**Millwork / supplier partners**  
You rarely manufacture. You curate programs: price tiers, lead times, freight, showroom support, defect policy. Partner failure becomes *your* Google review. Inventory-light (order after sell) is common — beautiful for cash **if** deposits are real and selections are locked.

**Lead times & expectation management**  
Weeks from order to delivery is normal; “really quick” in custom can still be **3–8 weeks** depending on vendor (per that same interview’s industry comment). Soft dates + hard homeowner vacations = rage.

**Install orchestration**  
W-2 finish carpenters vs 1099 install specialists. Either way: protect floors, level boxes, scribe fills, soft-close adjusts, counter template coordination. The room is unusable or half-usable — patience is thin.

**Showroom (optional but real)**  
Intent-based category: people search; they don’t impulse-buy a kitchen like a smoothie. Light commercial / non–Grade-A space can work. Showroom still costs rent, samples, and someone to staff it. Mobile-first systems trade that for van + laptop + stronger digital lead gen.

**Owner role (honest version)**  
Early stage: lead flow, design/sales leadership (or being the closer), partner QA, install scheduling, cash, reviews.  
Not “I bought a logo and went fishing.”  
Brian’s anti-fit point applies hard here: **sales-heavy, high-ticket home services** (he names cabinets with roofing/flooring) fight semi-absentee until the sales culture is real.

**Systems**  
Estimating tools, design software, digital paperwork handoff from sales → ops, production calendar, warranty SOP. Hero designers don’t scale past their calendar.

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

**Startup / investment shape (education):**

- Brian’s broad home-services franchise band often lands roughly **~$100k–$200k** (painting, roofing, plumbing, flooring, restoration named together in one teaching clip) — *category* band, not a kitchen-specific FDD.  
- Public 2026 FDD aggregator reads for a large **refresh/remodel** brand in this lane (Kitchen Tune-Up as category proof): Item 7 often cited roughly **~$122k–$199k**; royalty stack commonly discussed around **~6% + ~1%** brand/MAP (+ tech fees in some filings) — **always verify current FDD**; territory fees and showroom choices move the real check.  
- Brian’s Cabinet IQ interview cited all-in roughly **~$189k** for that system at the time (fee + showroom buildout story; vendor help on sample cost) — dated podcast economics, not a forecast.  
- FranServe placement history includes cabinet/kitchen names (e.g. Cabinet IQ) as **category proof** someone is awarding deals — not as your unit economics.

**Operating intuition (industry + interview education, not forecasts):**

- Ticket ladder: refresh/reface often **four figures to low five**; replacement + counters often **mid five**; premium outliers higher  
- Interview shape: ~**$25k** average with vanity drag; **pay in full before order** as a cash discipline example  
- Gross often discussed near **materials + labor ≈ half the ticket** in that niche cabinet+counter conversation — your mix (paint vs custom wood) will disagree  
- Close rates and lead CAC vary wildly by channel; ask for **consult → sold → installed** conversion, not vanity lead counts  
- Cycle time: days for some refresh installs vs **weeks of millwork lag** for custom — capacity planning is a queue of sold-but-not-installed jobs

Diligence questions that matter more than memorizing averages:

- What’s the **refresh vs reface vs replace vs counters** mix last 24 months?  
- Median days from first consult → signed contract → materials ordered → install complete?  
- Deposit / pay-before-order policy — and what happens when the customer changes a door style after the factory cut?  
- Close rate by lead source (Google vs showroom vs referral vs GC)?  
- Sales commission structure? Designer W-2 vs commission-only?  
- % install subcontracted vs W-2? Callback / punch cost as % of revenue?  
- Top three millwork partners: lead time reliability, defect rate, freight damage process?  
- Showroom rent vs mobile — contribution after occupancy?  
- If franchised: royalty + brand fund + tech + required local spend as % of gross; Item 20 outlet churn  
- Warranty length and who pays rework when the factory says “installer error”

What that means in plain English:

The *category* can print attractive project margins because tickets are large and inventory can stay light.  
A *specific office* can drown in unpaid design time, stale pipeline, late trucks, and one viral “my kitchen was destroyed for six weeks” review — while still “crushing it” on Instagram.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

**1. It’s a sales + design business wearing a tool belt**  
If you can’t build a consultative close culture, the millwork catalog doesn’t matter.

**2. Long, emotional sales cycle**  
Spouses, budgets, Pinterest boards, “we’ll decide after the holidays.” Pipeline looks full; cash doesn’t move.

**3. Discretionary demand (not emergency)**  
Unlike a burst pipe, ugly cabinets wait. Soft economy hits this category before true essentials.

**4. Selection chaos / change orders**  
Unlocked finishes after the factory starts = dead margin, dead schedule, dead trust.

**5. Millwork partner dependency**  
Wrong shade, late container, damaged doors, discontinued door style. You own the apology.

**6. Lead-time expectation gaps**  
Homeowner heard “three weeks.” Vendor said “three weeks after final approval.” You said both. Nobody’s happy.

**7. Install quality in the most visible room**  
Gaps, out-of-level runs, chipped edges, soft-close fails. One bad kitchen tours the neighborhood group chat.

**8. Scope creep into full remodel**  
“While you’re here, can you move the sink / add a window / redo the floor?” Saying yes without GC muscle is how niche operators get crushed.

**9. Showroom vs lead-gen cost**  
Empty showroom is expensive wallpaper. Mobile-only with weak digital is an empty calendar. Pick a model and fund it.

**10. Commission sales culture**  
Near-100% commission designers/closers: high upside, high drama, high turnover — and Brian’s point that owner energy is hard to replace.

**11. Owner dependency / anti-semi-absentee**  
Early on, the best closer is often you. Handing “just hire a sales manager” into this model is a common expensive fantasy.

**12. Cash timing traps**  
If you order before money is real — or finance the customer’s indecision — working capital vanishes in plywood and quartz.

**13. Competition from every direction**  
Big-box, independent cabinet shops, handymen who “paint cabinets,” full GCs, national refresh brands, Facebook marketplace “guys.” Differentiation is process + trust + finish quality, not slogans.

**14. Warranty / punch purgatory**  
Callbacks months later. Humidity warps. Customer “remembered” a different stain. Policy or pain.

**15. Seasonality & life events**  
Spring listing season and pre-holiday pushes; summer vacations and January freeze on decisions. Plan the troughs.

**16. Hiring designers who can sell (or sellers who can design)**  
Pure artists who won’t ask for the card. Pure closers who design nightmares. You need both muscles.

**17. Franchise stack (if you go that route)**  
Royalty + rules + territory + required marketing vs playbook, vendor pricing, design support, and brand. Worth it only if the system wins jobs and protects margin — not because the logo looks good on a wrap.

**18. Confusing “sold revenue” with installed, collected profit**  
A signed proposal isn’t cash. An ordered kitchen isn’t a happy review. A big top line with rework and refunds is a trap.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means cabinets / kitchen refresh is a **real high-ticket project business** with a long fuse — not a paint-and-pray side hustle and not a passive royalty coupon.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re wired for consultative, high-ticket sales (or building that team)  
- You’re patient with long cycles and still ruthless about pipeline hygiene  
- You like orchestrating partners and installs more than manufacturing wood yourself  
- You’re okay living in design details, samples, and “the hinge side is wrong” drama  
- Project home services (same stack as painting / flooring / roofing) excite you more than recurring route hours  
- You’ll hold scope: cabinets/refresh excellence beats mediocre full-gut GC cosplay

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want passive / semi-absentee from month one  
- You hate selling and hate hiring sellers  
- You need weekly recurring route revenue to sleep (think pest, lawn, senior care hours)  
- You’re undercapitalized for marketing + the lag from consult to install  
- You want to *be* the finish carpenter as the whole company (craft job ≠ this page’s model)  
- You actually want full kitchen GC complexity (permits, plumbing, electrical as core) — different page, different risk  
- Soft-skill conflict (upset homeowners in their kitchen) drains you dry

Franchise vs independent is a separate call.  
Same category either way: design → close → order → install → warranty.

## Risks (the short list)

- Pipeline full, factory empty, cash thin  
- Partner miss → your reputation  
- Change-order bleed  
- Install callback spiral  
- Discretionary demand dries in a downturn  
- Sales culture collapses when you step back  
- Showroom rent with no intent traffic  
- Royalty drag without lead/design/vendor advantage  
- Thinking “kitchens always sell houses” substitutes for operator skill

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “people hate dated oak.”

You’re buying the right to run a **design-to-install machine** that turns housing stock, trust, and millwork partners into finished kitchens — if you can close emotional jobs, lock selections, survive lead times, and install clean.

Some people should lean refresh/reface for speed and density.  
Some people should lean cabinets + counters and curate a premium lane.  
Some people should partner with GCs and stay niche.  
Some people should walk — and put the same capital into a vehicle with cleaner recurring economics or a shorter sales cycle.

The question isn’t “do kitchens go out of style?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a stressful showroom calendar that only works when you’re the designer, the closer, and the apology department?**

If you want help comparing doors (this model vs painting vs flooring vs others), that’s a conversation.  
Not a pitch deck.
