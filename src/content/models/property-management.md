The owner’s in another state.  
The toilet’s overflowing at 11:40 p.m.  
The application stack has three “maybe” credit stories and one eviction that wasn’t disclosed cleanly.  
Rent is late — again — and the owner wants to know why *you* haven’t fixed a human who won’t pay.

That’s **property management**.

Not “buy rentals and be a landlord.”  
Not flipping houses.  
Not short-term rental co-hosting as the whole company (different channel mix, cleaning ops, and regulation risk — separate model).  
Not a brokerage listing specialty (different license economics — see **senior real estate** when the aging seller is the product).

You’re a **local ops company** whose product is running someone else’s doors: leasing, rent collection, maintenance coordination, owner reporting, compliance theater, and the phone that rings when water hits the floor.

Simple on a whiteboard: more doors → sticky monthly fees → scale.  
Hard in real life — because **door growth vs service quality**, **after-hours**, **trust accounting**, **vendor networks**, **owner/tenant churn**, and **licensing** all show up before the “recurring revenue” brochure does.

---

## What the business actually is

You’re a **residential (or small-multifamily) property manager** — independent or branded — paid to stand between owners and the daily reality of tenants, toilets, and turns.

Typical job: an investor (or tired accidental landlord) owns one or many units and doesn’t want to be the after-hours number. You market the vacancy, screen applicants, collect rent, dispatch vendors, chase notices, reconcile deposits, and send the owner a statement that doesn’t make them fire you.

Brian’s materials put this row on the **250 Franchise Ideas** sheet as **Property Management**, with **Real Property Management**, **Property Management Inc.**, and **All County Property Management** as category-proof names — not as “this page is a brand pitch.” His **Mobile Franchises** newsletter lists property management alongside other go-to-the-customer models. Separately, his landlord-vs-business teaching treats **property management fees** as an *expense line on the investor’s P&L* — which is exactly why this page is the **services company**, not the landlord.

Typical menu (mix varies by market / company):

1. **Full management** — rent collection, owner statements, maintenance coordination, renewals, compliance basics  
2. **Leasing / tenant placement** — list, show, screen, lease; often a separate fee event  
3. **Lease renewals** — keep the good tenant; fee or included depending on agreement  
4. **Make-ready / turns** — punch list, vendors, photos, re-list speed  
5. **Owner acquisition / onboarding** — the sales motion that adds doors  
6. **Optional attach** — in-house maintenance bench, HOA/condo specialty, light commercial, insurance/resident “benefit” packages (disclose ruthlessly)  
7. **Not the same company:** owning the doors yourself (landlord CapEx), STR co-host platforms, or pure brokerage listing work

Typical loop:

1. **Win the owner** — realtor intro, investor meetup, Google, seller-financed tired landlord, franchise/brand lead flow, acquisition of a book  
2. **Onboard the door** — agreement, trust setup, keys/codes, condition photos, reserves policy, who approves what  
3. **Lease or keep leased** — marketing, showings, screening, move-in, deposit handling  
4. **Operate** — rent ACH, late notices, work orders, vendor dispatch, inspections, renewals  
5. **Report / remit** — owner statement; money that isn’t yours leaves the trust account correctly  
6. **Retain or lose** — owner churn on “you’re too expensive / too slow / too much markup”; tenant churn on bad turns and bad screening  
7. **Grow doors without breaking the phone** — the whole game after door ~50–150 depending on systems

**Scope lock:** this is an **ops / services model** — doors under management, sticky fees, tenant ops.

It is **not** landlord / buy-and-hold investing (different capital, leverage, CapEx, and who owns the asset — Brian’s ownership frame keeps **business vs real estate vs investing** as separate doors).  
It is **not** “your tenants’ rent is your revenue” — that’s the *owner’s* top line; yours is fees.  
It is **not** short-term rental co-host / Airbnb management as the identity of the company.  
It is **not** senior real-estate specialty (listings + transition stack).  
It is **not** “set it and forget it passive income with a logo.”

Named proof points exist: national residential PM brands and strong local independents. Treat logos as **category proof** — not as the point of this page, and not as a substitute for a transferable book of doors and clean trust accounting.

Independents, franchisees, and roll-ups can all sit in this category. Same umbrella. Wildly different P&Ls and compliance maturity.

## How money comes in

You sell **management capacity and trust** — not the underlying rent roll (that belongs to the owner).

Pricing / economics shapes you’ll see:

- **Monthly management fee** — commonly educated as roughly **~8–12% of collected rent** (or a flat **~$100–$150+/door** band in some markets); always verify *your* market and agreement  
- **Leasing / placement fee** — often educated as roughly **~50–100% of one month’s rent** when you fill a vacancy  
- **Renewal / lease-extension fees** — smaller, sticky if you keep tenants  
- **Setup / onboarding fees** — new-door friction coverage  
- **Maintenance coordination markup** — disclosed % or flat admin on vendor invoices (ethics live here)  
- **Ancillary / resident programs** — late fees (split rules matter), pet screening, “benefit” packages, tech fees — only where legal and contracted  
- **What you generally do *not* own:** the rent itself, appreciation, depreciation — those are landlord economics

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Doors under management** that stay — not doors you signed and lost in six months  
2. **Revenue per door (RPU / RPD)** — management fee alone is often less than half of a mature book once leasing, renewals, and ancillaries are real  
3. **Average rent × fee %** — low-rent doors can be operationally identical and economically worse  
4. **Vacancy days** — empty doors starve fee % models and create leasing cost; they also burn owner trust  
5. **Owner concentration** — one whale portfolio can make or destroy a month  
6. **Staff leverage** — doors per coordinator / property manager before the phone melts

Gross management fee *looks* simple on a napkin.  
Then after-hours overtime, software, E&O, trust accounting, bad debt chasing, free “owner therapy,” and the make-ready that took three vendor no-shows show up.

This is a **trust + compliance + ops** business.  
Not rental NOI. Not a listing commission. Not a route van (though you’ll live in your truck some weeks).

**Light contrast (same housing stack, different check):**

| Model | Who pays you | What you “sell” | Overhead feel |
|-------|--------------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Landlord / buy-hold** | Tenants (rent) → you keep NOI after debt/ops | Ownership of the asset | CapEx, leverage, vacancy risk on *your* balance sheet |
| **Senior real estate specialty** | Commission on closed deals (via brokerage) | List / buy / close + transition stack | License splits; sparse high-touch files |
| **Home inspection** | Buyer / sometimes deal party | Condition report for a transaction | Agent marketing; solo → multi-inspector |
| **Residential cleaning** | Homeowner / sometimes PM on turns | Recurring cleans / turn cleans | Labor attrition; route density |
| **Property management** | Owner (fees on doors you operate) | Leasing + ops + trust + vendor orchestration | Sticky fees; after-hours; trust accounting; door-growth quality trap |

Brian’s landlord content complains about **management fees, maintenance, missed rent, evictions, and turnovers** eating investor cash flow — that’s the *customer pain* this company exists to absorb (for a fee). Don’t confuse absorbing that pain with *being* the landlord.

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

America has a lot of rental housing — and a lot of owners who don’t want to be on-call facility managers.

Remote investors, tired accidental landlords, small multifamily owners, and people who bought “cash flow” then met a 2 a.m. sewer backup keep the demand for a **professional middle layer** alive.

Every new door that gets financed, inherited, or exhausted into “I can’t do this anymore” is a potential management agreement.

The constraint isn’t “are there rentals?”

It’s **license + trust accounting hygiene + enough quality doors at a fee that funds real service** — without growing so fast the phone and the vendors collapse.

National brands exist. Local boutiques exist. Software exists.  
Same category umbrella. Different operating reality.

## Micro: what actually drives one company

**Doors under management (the unit of scale)**  
You’re not scaling “impressions.” You’re scaling obligations. Each door is a phone line, a deposit, a smoke detector, a renewal calendar, and a vendor relationship. Count doors the way a route company counts stops — then ask what service level those doors actually get.

**Owner sales vs tenant ops (two jobs)**  
Winning doors is a B2B sales motion. Running doors is an ops / CX / compliance motion. Founders who only love “investor lunches” drown in work orders. Founders who only love “fixing toilets” never replace churned owners.

**Trust accounting (non-negotiable)**  
You hold other people’s money: rent in transit, security deposits, sometimes reserves. Commingling with operating cash is how companies and licenses die. Separate accounts, reconciliations, software that matches the bank, and a rule that “temporary borrow from trust” is never temporary.

**After-hours reality**  
Emergencies don’t respect office hours. Either you staff an answering/on-call system with clear emergency definitions, or *you* are the system. Semi-absentee myths die on a holiday weekend flood.

**Vendor network**  
Plumbers, HVAC, electricians, cleaners, carpet, junk, locksmiths, restorers, landscapers — preferred, insured, fast, and honest on pricing. A thin vendor bench turns every work order into a firefight and every owner statement into an argument about markup.

**Screening + fair housing + documentation**  
Bad tenants are expensive. Illegal or inconsistent screening is existential. Criteria written, applied, retained. “We go with our gut” is how you buy a lawsuit.

**Fee honesty / markup disclosure**  
Owners hate surprise markups more than they hate fair disclosed fees. Race-to-6% management with silent vendor padding is how you win doors you will lose — and how regulators and plaintiff’s counsel get interested.

**Software + process**  
Leasing CRM, owner portal, maintenance tickets, accounting, inspection photos. The brand or the indie stack can both work; chaos scales worse than mediocre software.

**Owner role (honest version)**  
Early stage: you *are* leasing, collections, vendor wrangler, and midnight phone.  
Semi-absentee fairy tales are how people buy a territory, a logo, and fifty doors of resentment.  
Scale means coordinators, clear SLAs, and a book that transfers — not “rentals are forever so I’ll fish.”

**Adjacent stack**  
Cleaning turns, inspectors, handymen, restoration, junk, landscaping, pest — you don’t need to own them. You do need named humans who show up — and who don’t train your owners to fire you.

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

**Operating intuition (education — not forecasts):**

- Money event = **fees on doors you keep**, plus leasing/renewal/ancillary events — never confuse *owner rent collected* with *your* revenue  
- Public education bands often put residential management fees around **~8–12% of collected rent** (or flat per-door alternatives) — verify market; don’t memorize a blog  
- Leasing fees often chatter around **~50–100% of one month’s rent**; renewals and setup fees vary widely  
- Operators obsess over **revenue per door / RPU** because fee-% alone understates (or overstates) a real book; category chatter often treats “management fee only” shops as leaving money — and ethics — on the table when ancillaries aren’t disclosed  
- Scale intuition you’ll hear in the trade: very small books can feel like a poorly paid job; mid books hit overhead walls; larger books need systems or they rot — **none of that is your guarantee**  
- Brian’s corpus is **strong** on “don’t confuse landlord investing with operating a business” and **thin** on a dedicated property-management Item 19 / door-level earnings table — do **not** invent one from franchise marketing PDFs

**Startup / investment shape (education — verify current quotes / state requirements):**

- License / broker affiliation where required, E&O, trust account setup, PM software, marketing to owners, working capital for slow door growth  
- Franchise / brand paths (RPM / PMI / All County–style category) add fee + system + sometimes territory math — still not a substitute for local ops competence  
- Buying an existing **book of doors** is a different check (diligence on churn, trust cleanliness, owner concentration, and whether relationships follow the buyer)  
- You’re often **office-light / home-plus-software** early — not a retail buildout. You *are* buying the ability to answer the phone correctly when water hits the floor

Your diligence questions should sound like:

- Doors under management — trailing 24 months adds, losses, net?  
- Revenue per door (all fees) vs management-fee-only?  
- Owner concentration — top 5 owners as % of revenue?  
- Owner churn % and reasons (fee, service, self-manage, sold property)?  
- Average days vacant / leasing conversion?  
- Trust reconciliations — clean, monthly, who signs off? Any past shortages?  
- After-hours SLA — who answers, what’s “emergency,” overtime cost?  
- Vendor bench — insured, preferred pricing, markup policy disclosed in writing?  
- Licensing / brokerage relationship / audit history / complaints?  
- Staffing — doors per coordinator; owner hours still glued to the phone?  
- If buying a book: will owners re-sign; deposits handled correctly; software data exportable?

Same brain you’d use buying any trust + recurring book: concentration, compliance, service capacity, and whether *you* want to live inside other people’s rental emergencies for years.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

This section is the point. Soft marketing skips it. Operators live it.

**1. Door growth vs service quality**  
Every new door is revenue *and* load. Sales-led growth without coordinators, vendors, and after-hours coverage creates the reviews that freeze the next fifty doors. Growing to look big while the phone drops calls is how PM companies earn their reputation.

**2. After-hours / emergency load**  
Floods, no-heat, lockouts, break-ins — real emergencies and “emergencies.” Under-defined policies turn every owner into a 24/7 client and every technician into overtime. Over-defined policies that never dispatch create churn and liability when something was actually urgent.

**3. Trust accounting**  
Deposits and rent that aren’t yours. Three-way reconciliation isn’t optional theater. One “we’ll fix it Monday” borrow from trust can end the company. Software bugs, owner advances, and messy make-ready refunds are where good people get stupid.

**4. Vendor network fragility**  
Your product is often *someone else’s* truck. Preferred plumber retires, HVAC ghosts peak season, cleaner no-shows a turn — and you own the owner’s anger. Building and auditing a bench is unpaid until the day it saves the portfolio.

**5. Owner + tenant churn**  
Owners leave for fee, for a cousin who “will do it for free,” for self-management fantasies, or because you were slow once during a flood. Tenants leave (or get removed) and create leasing cost and vacancy holes. Sticky fees only stick if service and screening hold.

**6. Licensing / brokerage / regulatory stack**  
States vary: PM license, real-estate broker affiliation, trust rules, advertising, fair housing, deposit timelines, entry notice, habitability. “We just manage a few doors casually” is how amateurs meet regulators. Franchise playbooks help; they don’t replace local counsel.

**7. Screening / fair housing / eviction politics**  
Tighten standards and you get accused of discrimination if you apply them unevenly. Loosen them and you buy nonpayment. Evictions are slow, emotional, and jurisdiction-specific. Document everything or don’t play.

**8. Fee compression + race-to-bottom**  
Someone always offers 6% and “free leasing.” They often make it up on silent markups or by not answering the phone. Competing only on headline % trains owners to treat you like a commodity — then punish you when commodity service arrives.

**9. Maintenance markup / ethics optics**  
Legitimate coordination fees, disclosed, with invoice visibility, can be fair. Hidden padding, captive vendors, and “we never show the owner the real invoice” destroy trust and invite claims. This is where sticky revenue becomes sticky liability.

**10. Vacancy + leasing speed**  
Empty doors anger owners and starve % fee models. Bad photos, slow showing response, and weak screening create either long vacancy or expensive wrong tenants. Leasing is a sales muscle inside an ops company — awkward if you only hired “nice people.”

**11. Owner concentration**  
One 80-door client feels like winning until they leave. Portfolio risk is real. So is the power imbalance when a whale demands free after-hours heroics.

**12. Staffing / doors-per-human wall**  
There’s a range where adding doors without adding capable humans quietly destroys NPS. Burned coordinators quit; tribal knowledge walks out; trust reconciliations slip.

**13. Software / process debt**  
Spreadsheets + group texts scale to a point, then fail spectacularly. Migrations mid-book are painful. Owners feel every portal glitch as incompetence.

**14. Liability / habitability / entry mistakes**  
Wrong lockbox, missed carbon monoxide detector, unauthorized entry, ignored mold complaint — E&O and worse. Stay in process; document; don’t play home inspector or lawyer.

**15. Semi-absentee myth**  
“Hire a manager and watch doors compound.” Early reality is owner sales *and* ops fires. Earn leverage with systems and leaders later — don’t buy the myth with a territory map.

**16. Confusing “own rentals” with “own this company”**  
Landlord / syndication / BRRRR is a different door in Brian’s ownership frame. This page is a **services practice** that operates other people’s assets for fees. CapEx, leverage, and who owns the outcome are not the same conversation — even if both say “doors.”

**17. Thinking “everyone needs a manager” fills your calendar**  
Macro rental stock is real. Your company is doors won, doors kept, trust accounts that reconcile, vendors that show, and a fee structure that funds the phone being answered.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means property management is a **real trust + ops + compliance business**, not a passive rent-collection app with a logo.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re willing to live inside licensing / trust rules and treat other people’s money as sacred  
- You can sell owners **and** build an ops machine (or honestly partner the half you hate)  
- You’re calm under after-hours pressure and still willing to hold emergency definitions  
- You like vendor relationships, checklists, and documentation more than Instagram landlord flexing  
- You’re okay with sticky-but-operationally-heavy recurring revenue — not only project spikes  
- You want a real-estate-adjacent vehicle **without** buying the underlying CapEx stack yourself  
- You’re willing to fire bad owners / bad doors when they poison the book

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You want **landlord / investing** returns and asset appreciation as the whole thesis (different door — say so)  
- You want pure listing commissions or senior transition stack work (**senior real estate** / related pages)  
- You want truck-route field service without trust-accounting gravity (**residential cleaning** turns may still be a *vendor* you hire)  
- You hate conflict, collections, or slow legal friction (evictions, habitability arguments)  
- You’re looking for passive / “hire a manager and fish” from month one  
- You’re under-reserved for door ramp and a rough leasing season  
- You’re allergic to compliance detail and think commingling is “just cash flow timing”

Franchise brand vs indie boutique vs buying a book is a separate call.  
Same category either way: doors, fees, trust, vendors, after-hours, churn — plus a phone that doesn’t care about your weekend plans.

## Risks (the short list)

- Door growth outrunning service → churn + reputation damage  
- Trust accounting errors / shortages → license and existential risk  
- After-hours understaffing → liability and owner flight  
- Vendor failure cascading into owner churn  
- Fee compression / undisclosed markup blowups  
- Owner concentration cliffs  
- Fair housing / screening / eviction mistakes  
- Habitability / entry / safety claims  
- Staffing walls and coordinator burnout  
- Buying logo theater instead of a clean, transferable book  
- Confusing practice ownership with landlord real-estate investing

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “America has rentals” or a pastel promise that management fees print while you sleep.

You’re buying the right to stand between an owner’s spreadsheet and a tenant’s 11:40 p.m. reality — and get paid in sticky fees — if you can grow doors without breaking service, keep trust accounting boring, hold a real vendor bench, and stay licensed when the file gets ugly.

Some people should launch lean, win doors slowly, and build process before headcount.  
Some people should buy a tired book and fix trust hygiene + after-hours + vendor follow-through.  
Some people should walk — and put the same capital into landlord investing (if that’s actually the thesis), senior real estate, home inspection, residential cleaning, a trade, or a different vehicle entirely.

The question isn’t “will landlords keep outsourcing headaches?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a calendar full of other people’s toilets that only pays when the doors stay, the trust reconciles, the vendors show, and the fee that clears still funds a phone answered on purpose?**

If you want help comparing doors (this practice vs landlord investing vs senior real estate vs home inspection vs residential cleaning vs others), that’s a conversation.  
Not a pitch deck.
