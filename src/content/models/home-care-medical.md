Dad left the hospital with a wound that still needs dressing.

The discharge planner says “home health.”  
Not a live-in companion.  
Not someone to cook dinner every night.  
A nurse — visits, orders, documentation — then therapy if the plan needs it.

That’s **skilled home health** / medical home care.

Not a nursing home.  
Not continuous companion care (that’s the non-medical agency page).  
Not facility placement fees.

Clinicians go to the patient’s home for **intermittent** skilled services under a plan of care.  
Payers (often Medicare, sometimes Medicaid / Medicare Advantage / commercial) reimburse episodes — not “Mom’s private hours forever.”  
You run clinical leadership, compliance, and a collections machine that moves slower than a family Visa.

Simple on a whiteboard.  
Hard in real life — because the product is regulated clinical care inside someone else’s house, paid by rules you don’t control.

---

## What the business actually is

You’re a **clinical home-health operator**: referrals in, assessments, plan of care, skilled visits out, documentation that survives a survey, claims that survive an audit.

**Scope lock (read this twice):**

| | **This page — skilled / medical home health** | **Non-medical senior care** ([related](/models/in-home-non-medical-senior-care)) |
|--|-----------------------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| **Who shows up** | RNs, LPNs/LVNs, PTs/OTs/SLPs, HHAs under clinical oversight | Companions / personal-care aides |
| **Cadence** | Intermittent visits (episodes / 30-day periods under Medicare PDGM) | Hours that can grow into live-in / near-24/7 |
| **Who pays** | Medicare (and other clinical payers) for qualifying skilled need | Mostly private pay / LTC / some VA–Medicaid waivers |
| **License stack** | State home-health license + Medicare CoPs / survey path when you bill Medicare | State non-medical / personal-care rules (lighter clinical layer) |
| **North-star pain** | Clinical quality + coding + cash timing + nurse supply | Caregiver recruiting + schedule coverage |

Brian’s teaching (and his own partnership) centers **non-medical** first — companions, ADLs, private-pay hours. His newsletter said it plainly: *some* owners expand into medical care, but the **simpler** model is non-medical. Ted’s interview framed skilled as a **later** add when the non-skilled book had cash flow (~**200 hours/week** as *his* milestone talk) so the office could fund a more complex clinical side.

This page is the heavier door: **Medicare-certified (or clinical-payer) skilled home health** as the business — not “we sometimes send a nurse.”

Typical service menu (skilled / intermittent):

1. **Skilled nursing** — wound care, IV / injections, catheter / ostomy, medication teaching, clinical assessment  
2. **Physical / occupational / speech therapy** — post-acute, fall risk, ADLs, swallowing  
3. **Home health aide** — personal care **tied to** the skilled plan (not standalone companion hours)  
4. **Medical social work** (when indicated)  
5. **Care coordination** with physicians, hospitals, SNFs, discharge planners

Typical loop:

1. **Referral** — hospital / SNF discharge, physician, ACO / MA plan, prior patient  
2. **Eligibility / SOC** — homebound / skilled-need rules (Medicare), face-to-face, start of care, OASIS  
3. **Plan of care** — physician-ordered; clinical manager / DON owns quality  
4. **Visits** — scheduled density that clears clinical need *and* payment thresholds (LUPA risk is real)  
5. **Document** — if it isn’t in the record, it didn’t happen for payment or survey  
6. **Bill / collect** — PDGM 30-day periods, adjustments, denials, AR aging  
7. **Outcome / discharge** — goals met, facility, hospice, or readmission — then refill the pipeline

**Not this page:** continuous non-medical companion / live-in agencies ([in-home-non-medical-senior-care](/models/in-home-non-medical-senior-care)).  
**Not this page:** senior placement success fees ([senior-placement-referral](/models/senior-placement-referral)).  
**Not this page:** stairlifts / ramps ([mobility-accessibility](/models/mobility-accessibility)).  
**Not this page:** owning the SNF / AL building.

Some franchise systems sell **dual** books (medical + non-medical under one brand). FranServe deal flow has included names like BrightStar Care and BOOST Home Healthcare as **category proof** — logos are not the point; the operating stack is.

Even a non-medical brand’s “specialize into skilled / nursing services” path (Brian’s Drive file from a franchisor clinical lead) still demands a **managing RN / DON**, state Board of Nursing rules, professional liability, accreditation steps, skilled policy manuals, and 24/7 clinical coverage posture. That’s the tell: medical is not a menu toggle.

## How money comes in

You sell **qualified clinical episodes**, not “as many companion hours as the family will buy.”

Under Medicare’s **Patient-Driven Groupings Model (PDGM)**, payment for home health is largely driven by **patient characteristics** across roughly **30-day periods** — clinical grouping (primary diagnosis), functional impairment (OASIS), comorbidities, and admission source/timing — into hundreds of case-mix groups. Visit volume still matters operationally (especially **LUPA**: low-utilization periods paid per-visit instead of the full case-mix amount), but you are not in the old “more visits = more revenue forever” cartoon.

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Referral quality × admit conversion** — empty census with a certified agency is just overhead  
2. **Case-mix / coding accuracy** — wrong primary diagnosis or weak OASIS → underpayment or audit risk  
3. **Visit planning vs LUPA thresholds** — clinically appropriate frequency that also clears payment math  
4. **Payer mix** — traditional Medicare vs Medicare Advantage vs Medicaid vs commercial; MA can change authorization and rates  
5. **Length of stay / episode discipline** — stay too short or too long without clinical support and margin or compliance suffers  
6. **Collections speed** — payroll is weekly; Medicare remittance is not  
7. **Therapy + nursing mix** — staffing availability constrains what you can safely admit

Gross margin lives in **reimbursement − clinician labor (loaded) − mileage − clinical supplies − QA/coding cost**.  
Then overhead: administrator, **DON / clinical manager**, office, EHR (OASIS submission), EVV where required, professional + general liability, workers’ comp, compliance, marketing to referral sources, bad debt / denial write-offs, and (if franchised) royalty + brand/tech fees.

Cash reality vs non-medical: private-pay companion care often bills families on a short cycle. Skilled Medicare books routinely fund **weeks of payroll before period payment lands**. Working capital is not optional window dressing.

**Light contrast:** non-medical sells **hours × spread**. Skilled sells **episodes × case-mix × collectability**. Same demographic wave. Different ledger.

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

America is getting older. Brian’s newsletter cited seniors **85+** on a path roughly **~7M → ~15M by 2040**. People want to age in place. Hospitals push shorter stays. Families cannot be the night shift forever.

Post-acute care in the home is structural demand — wound care after surgery, new insulin teaching, PT after a fall, nursing oversight when the doctor will not keep them inpatient.

Demand is not the scarce resource.

**Nurses, therapists, clean documentation, and solvent AR are.**

Policy moves the goalposts. CMS’s CY **2026** Home Health PPS rulemaking has been discussed industry-wide as a **net pressure** year on aggregate Medicare payments (market-basket updates offset by PDGM behavioral adjustments — operators model their own case-mix, not headlines). Coding, OASIS quality, and denial management become margin strategy, not back-office trivia.

National chains, regional independents, and dual-model franchises all chase the same discharge lists. Differentiation is clinical reputation with referral sources — not a clever slogan.

## Micro: what actually drives one agency

**Clinical leadership (DON / clinical manager)**  
Medicare Conditions of Participation expect real oversight of patient care and personnel. Survey interviews land here. Hire the wrong “paper DON” and you own a compliance time bomb.

**Nurse & therapy pipeline**  
RN/LPN and therapy shortages are the skilled cousin of the caregiver bottleneck. Premium pay, schedule flexibility, and culture compete with hospitals and travel contracts. No clinicians → you refuse referrals → discharge planners stop calling.

**Referral engine**  
Case managers, hospitalists, SNFs, orthopedic groups, wound clinics, ACOs. Relationship selling with clinical credibility. One bad outcome story travels faster than your Google ad.

**PDGM / coding / QA**  
Primary diagnosis selection, functional scoring, comorbidity capture, face-to-face rules. Agencies that treat coding as “billing’s problem” leave money on the table *and* invite recoupments.

**LUPA & utilization discipline**  
Daily visibility into which periods are below visit thresholds — with clinical judgment, not visit padding. Fraud shortcuts end agencies.

**Survey & accreditation readiness**  
State survey and/or deemed accreditation (e.g. Joint Commission / CHAP / ACHC paths commonly discussed). Personnel files, competencies, infection control, emergency preparedness, QAPI. Unannounced visits are a lifestyle, not an event.

**AR / revenue cycle**  
Denial workflows, ADR responses, MA authorization, secondary billing. A growing census with aging AR is a cash crisis wearing a growth costume.

**Owner role (honest version)**  
Early stage: you are chasing referrals, backing up the DON, living in payroll/AR tension, and answering “why wasn’t the nurse there?” when a family is scared. Semi-absentee fairy tales ignore CoPs and survey reality. Earn a real administrator/DON bench before you disappear.

**Optional dual book**  
Some owners run non-medical hours *and* skilled episodes. That can deepen referral stickiness — and double the compliance surface. Don’t blur the P&Ls or the licenses in your head.

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

Education shape only — **not** a forecast for your market or your deal.

**Startup / certification runway (industry education bands):**

- Independent **Medicare-certified** home health startups are commonly discussed in roughly **~$150k–$350k+** all-in (some guides widen toward **~$165k–$465k** depending on state, CON/moratorium, accreditation, and working-capital buffer)  
- Budget **months** (often framed ~**9–15+**) before meaningful Medicare cash — license, enrollment (CMS-855A), survey/accreditation, first claims  
- Non-medical companion agencies often cite lower/faster starts (Brian’s newsletter ~**$90k–$150k** franchise bands for *non-medical*; another clip ~**$130k–$200k**) — useful as **contrast**, not as skilled budget  
- Franchise medical / dual systems: verify current FDD Item 7; clinical staffing and AR buffer dominate the “fee” line

**What the money buys:**

- Administrator + **DON** salaries before census pays for them  
- Office, EHR / OASIS stack, EVV where required  
- Professional liability appropriate for skilled services (plus GL, workers’ comp, cyber)  
- Policy manuals, survey prep, legal  
- **3–6+ months** payroll/opex runway while periods bill in arrears

**Operating intuition (education):**

- PDGM = case-mix groups × 30-day periods; LUPA thresholds vary by group — model *your* mix  
- CY 2026 HH PPS industry chatter: aggregate Medicare payment **pressure** after adjustments — agency-level impact varies wildly by case-mix  
- Ted’s non-medical milestone talk (~**200 billable hours/week** before funding a skilled add-on) is one operator’s cash-flow gate for *expanding* into clinical — not a universal rule and not a skilled P&L

Your diligence questions should sound like:

- What’s the **payer mix** (Traditional Medicare / MA / Medicaid / commercial) and denial rate by payer?  
- Average **days sales outstanding**? ADR / RAC exposure last 24 months?  
- **LUPA rate** and how clinical managers monitor mid-period?  
- DON tenure? RN / therapy vacancy rate? Travel/contract labor % of clinical cost?  
- Last survey / accreditation findings and open CAPs?  
- Case-mix index trend vs peer benchmarks — coding QA process?  
- Top referral sources — concentration risk if one hospital system shifts preferred providers?  
- If dual-model: are licenses, branding, staffing, and books cleanly separated?  
- Working capital: can this agency survive a 60–90 day remittance stall without owner panic borrowing?  
- If franchised: does the system actually help with clinical playbooks and payer contracting — or only logos?

Same brain you’d use buying any regulated clinical business: census quality, labor, compliance posture, cash conversion — and whether *you* want that responsibility.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

This section is the point. Soft marketing skips it. Operators live it.

**1. Medicare / PDGM complexity**  
Case-mix, OASIS, comorbidities, timing, behavioral adjustments, annual recalibrations. You are in the reimbursement business whether you like it or not.

**2. Nurse and therapy recruiting**  
Harder and more expensive than aide recruiting. Hospitals next door will outbid you on benefits. No bench → refused admits → dead brand with discharge planners.

**3. Compliance & Conditions of Participation**  
Policies, competencies, supervision, infection control, emergency preparedness, QAPI. “We’ll tidy the charts later” is how agencies die.

**4. Survey / accreditation risk**  
Unannounced. Condition-level findings can freeze growth or Medicare billing. Corrective action plans are full-time work.

**5. AR / collections lag**  
Clinicians get paid on the clock. Payers pay on their clock. Undercapitalized owners confuse census growth with cash.

**6. Clinical liability**  
Wounds, meds, falls, missed visits, documentation gaps. Professional liability and incident response are core ops, not insurance paperwork you file once.

**7. LUPA and utilization traps**  
Under-visit → per-visit payment shock. Over-visit without medical necessity → audit risk. Both hurt.

**8. Denial / audit culture**  
ADRs, UPIC, MA prior auth. Revenue cycle is a department, not a side hustle for the office manager.

**9. Referral volatility**  
One hospital preferred-provider shift or a competitor’s nurse navigator can empty your Monday morning.

**10. Dual-book temptation**  
Adding non-medical hours (or adding skilled onto a companion agency) without separate systems, staffing, and licenses creates blur — surveyors and juries love blur.

**11. Owner intensity + key-person risk**  
Lose the DON or the administrator and the CoP stack wobbles. Absentee ownership fantasies collide with clinical accountability.

**12. Policy / rate pressure**  
Payment rules move. CY 2026-style net cuts or weight recalibrations hit thin-margin shops first.

**13. Documentation burden on clinicians**  
Burnout from charting drives turnover, which drives quality risk, which drives referral loss. A loop.

**14. State-by-state gates**  
CON, moratoria, license timelines, nurse practice acts. What worked in one state is not a template.

**15. Confusing “aging demographics” with “your collectible episodes”**  
Same trap as non-medical “market size” vs fillable hours — demand ≠ operable, payable census.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means skilled home health is a **regulated clinical services business** where labor *and* documentation *and* cash timing are the product.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re willing to own **clinical accountability**, not just sales  
- You can recruit and retain licensed clinicians — or partner with someone who can  
- Process, audit trails, and “show me the chart” culture energize you  
- You’re capitalized for slow AR and survey delays  
- Multi-year grind toward a professionalized agency sounds exciting  
- You’ve compared this door to lighter senior models and still want the clinical stack

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You wanted the **non-medical** hour-spread business and thought “medical” was a small upsell  
- You hate compliance, documentation, or dealing with payers  
- You need passive income or a semi-absentee story from day one  
- Thin stomach for survey risk, liability, or family clinical crises  
- You freeze when a nurse calls out and a wound visit is due  
- You’re actually hunting placement fees or companion-care only — different pages

Franchise vs independent is a separate decision.  
Same clinical physics either way. Franchise trades fee/royalty for playbook, brand, and peer network — only worth it if the system helps you win **referrals, clinicians, and clean surveys**, not just a logo on a polo.

Many strong operators **start non-medical** (Brian’s stated preference for simplicity) and only add skilled when cash flow and leadership can carry it (Ted’s framing). Walking past skilled is a valid choice — not a failure of ambition.

## Risks (the short list)

- Can’t staff RNs/therapy → can’t grow (or keep) preferred-provider status  
- Coding / OASIS errors → underpayment or recoupment  
- Failed survey / lost Medicare billing → existential  
- AR blowout → busy census, empty bank account  
- Clinical incident → liability + referral death spiral  
- Payer mix shift (MA penetration, rate cuts) → margin compression without ops change  
- Dual-model confusion → compliance and brand risk  
- Distracted ownership → CoP gaps while you “work on marketing”  
- Franchise stack (if any) → royalties must buy real clinical/ops leverage  
- Confusing demographic tailwinds with your collectible, survey-ready episodes

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “aging population.”

You’re buying the right to deliver **intermittent skilled care in the home** — if you can staff clinicians, survive surveys, code cleanly, and wait for payers without breaking payroll.

Some people should start (or buy) **non-medical** and stay there.  
Some people should buy a tired home-health book and install clinical + revenue-cycle systems.  
Some people should run dual — carefully.  
Some people should walk — and put the capital into a different vehicle.

The question isn’t “do seniors need nurses at home?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a regulated job with survey risk, AR stress, and higher stakes when a visit is missed?**

If you want help comparing doors (this model vs non-medical care, placement, mobility, or something outside senior care), that’s a conversation.
