The warehouse just lost six people to a competitor’s shift premium.  
A plant manager’s overtime is eating the P&L and the safety meeting.  
Somebody’s office manager quit Friday and Monday’s phones still have to ring.

Somebody needs **bodies that show up**, cleared for the site, paid this week — and a local operator who will take the call at 6 a.m. when the line is short.

That’s a **staffing / recruiting agency**.

Not a corporate HR department.  
Not [commercial janitorial](/models/commercial-janitorial) (same *B2B contract + night/day labor* neighborhood — you sell *cleaning hours under your brand*, not *people on someone else’s payroll*).  
Not [home care medical](/models/home-care-medical) (same *people placement* brain — totally different clinical license, Medicare, and survey stack).  
Not “I’ll post jobs on Indeed and call it a company” as the whole *employer-of-record* story.

You’re running a **buy-and-resell labor** business: pay rate × billable hours (plus direct-hire fees) − wage burden (payroll tax, WC, unemployment) − internal sales/recruiter payroll − office − the cash hole when associates get paid **weekly** and clients pay in **30–75 days**.

Simple on a whiteboard.  
Hard in real life — because **markup is not margin**, **payroll float is the product**, **workers’ comp class codes can erase a spread**, **demand is cyclical**, **collections are a second sales job**, and **sales culture** shows up before the lobby looks like a career-fair booth.

Express Employment–class franchise systems, regional indie offices, light-industrial specialists, professional / IT desks, and healthcare staffing shops sell overlapping physics. The logo is optional. The bill/pay spread and the funded payroll are not.

---

## What the business actually is

You’re a **local staffing operator**: an office (or home-to-office ramp) that sells **temporary / contract labor** and often **direct-hire / temp-to-hire** placements to employers — then recruits, screens, and deploys the people who fill those orders.

**Two product shapes (don’t blur them):**

| | **Temporary / contract staffing (primary spine)** | **Direct hire / permanent placement** |
|--|---------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **What you sell** | Billable hours — you (or your back-office partner) are typically the **W-2 employer of record** for the associate | One-time fee for a permanent hire (often % of salary); sometimes temp-to-hire conversion fees |
| **Primary constraint** | **Orders × fill rate × billable hours × loaded markup − float** | **Pipeline × close rate × fee % − recruiter cost** (lumpy cash) |
| **Cash shape** | High sales, thinner GM; weekly payroll vs slower AR | Higher margin per deal; feast/famine without a temp book underneath |
| **Risk feel** | WC, unemployment, employment-law, no-shows, bad clients | Reputation + guarantee periods; less float, less ongoing WC on the placed person |
| **Owner job early** | Outbound B2B sales + recruiting bench | Relationship selling + candidate quality |

Many real shops run **both**. Underwrite the mix honestly — a pure perm desk with no temp book is a different company than a light-industrial temp flywheel with a side door of direct-hire fees.

Typical menu:

1. **Temporary / contract staffing** — the volume spine (light industrial, warehouse/logistics, office/admin, skilled trades, light manufacturing, professional/IT in some books)  
2. **Temp-to-hire / evaluation hire** — client tries the person; conversion fee on the back end  
3. **Direct hire / permanent placement** — fee for a permanent fill  
4. **Specialty verticals** — healthcare, IT, finance/accounting, trades (higher bill rates, different compliance and recruiter scarcity)  
5. **On-site / MSP / VMS attach** (larger accounts) — vendor-managed systems, on-site supervisors; bigger volume, thinner price power  
6. **Payrolling / EOR-only** (some books) — client sourced the person; you run payroll/compliance for a thinner spread — don’t confuse with full recruiting value

Typical loop:

1. **Lead** — cold call / door knock, networking, referral from other B2B owners, prior clients expanding shifts, franchise lead systems (if any), LinkedIn / Google Local for SMBs  
2. **Discover / qualify** — roles, shifts, wage reality, safety/PPE, volume, creditworthiness, who signs the PO  
3. **Price** — bill rate from **loaded** pay cost up (not a round markup fantasy)  
4. **Recruit / screen** — ads, walk-ins, database, background / drug / skills where required  
5. **Dispatch / place** — show-up culture; backup bench for call-outs  
6. **Time / invoice / collect** — weekly pay to associates; Net-30/45/60+ from clients  
7. **Retain / expand** — more departments, sister sites, permanent conversions — or lose the account when quality slips or a cheaper bid wins

**Scope lock:** this page is the **local staffing / recruiting agency** — temp markup economics + optional direct-hire — selling labor solutions to employers.  
It is **not** [commercial janitorial](/models/commercial-janitorial) (you employ cleaners under *your* service contract).  
It is **not** [home-care-medical](/models/home-care-medical) or non-medical senior-care agencies (clinical / companion care under care regs — related *people ops*, different payer and license).  
It is **not** pure offshore / virtual VA staffing / Global Team–class remote talent placement (Brian has publicly mentioned investing in that adjacency — **different** delivery, compliance, and buyer; not this underwriting).  
It is **not** an internal corporate recruiting function or an ATS software company.  
It is **not** “gig marketplace app only” with no employer-of-record responsibility.

Brian’s published corpus treats staffing as a **real, sales-driven B2B category**: on the backlog as **markup on wages; sales culture; cyclical**; in CORE model teaching as a **people / team-based** example next to senior care and tutoring (growth = recruit + retain + schedule people *and* sell customers); in a deep newsletter walkthrough of **Express Employment–class** franchise staffing (buy/resell labor, detailed Item 19 / AAGM education, franchisor as EOR funding 100% of temp payroll, owner as sales driver); in the **Business with Beers** interview with Express franchising leadership (temp spine, bill/pay spread, HQ payroll funding vs 30–45 day client terms, outbound sales DNA, cold-call volume as a real filter); and in B2B sales-rep franchise lists / resale roundups naming Express as category proof. Treat named systems (Express, indie regionals, PE roll-ups, healthcare/IT specialists) as **proof the category professionalizes** — not as a pitch.

## How money comes in

You sell **filled, billable associate hours** (and placement fees that convert into cash) — not “job posts live” and not a networking-event selfie.

Education pricing / spread shape (national operator ranges; metros and specialties sit higher; commodity light industrial sits tighter):

- Brian’s published teaching example (category illustration): buy labor ~**$18**/hr → sell ~**$23**/hr → ~**$5** spread (~**20%** gross margin *before* you confuse yourself) — podcast education often walks **~$18 pay + burden → ~$20 loaded**, bill ~**$27**, ~**$7** gross margin  
- Healthy temp shops after **full burden** often land roughly **~18–28%** gross margin depending on segment (light industrial often tighter; skilled / professional / IT often richer)  
- Direct-hire fees commonly discussed roughly **~15–25%+ of first-year salary** (or flat packages) — lumpy  
- Markup % ≠ margin %: a **50% markup** on pay is only ~**33%** gross margin *before* burden — then FICA / SUTA / FUTA / WC / benefits eat the dream

What actually drives the dollars:

1. **Open orders × fill rate** — empty reqs with a sales machine still burning gas  
2. **Billable hours × bill/pay spread after burden** — the real unit of profit  
3. **Mix** — light industrial volume vs skilled / professional / healthcare; temp vs perm fee attach  
4. **Utilization of internal recruiters / salespeople** — desks that don’t fill don’t print  
5. **Client credit quality × DSO** — growth that outruns collections is how agencies die solvent-looking  
6. **Associate retention / re-placeability** — database that redeploys vs perpetual job-board spend  
7. **Sales capacity** — owners who won’t outbound don’t have a staffing company; they have a waiting room

Gross margin intuition (operator / industry education shape — **not** a forecast):

- Temp: high *sales* dollars, **thin-to-mid** GM after loaded wages — then internal labor, rent, job boards, factoring fees, and bad debt eat what’s left  
- Burden education often adds roughly **~12–22%+** on top of pay rate depending on state, WC class, and benefits  
- Workers’ comp is not a rounding error — wrong class code or a claim spike can erase months of spread  
- Net on a single office is often a **tighter** band than top-line “we did $6M sales” myths — volume rises; float, WC, and sales payroll rise with it

You’re not a job board with a logo.  
You’re a **spread + float + sales culture** company wearing business-casual.

## Macro: why this industry keeps working

Companies still need flexible labor. Hiring permanently for every spike is expensive. Aging workforces, logistics volume, manufacturing swings, and “we need coverage Monday” don’t disappear because LinkedIn exists.

Good times → more orders, overtime, temp-to-hire conversions, specialty desks.  
Tight times → clients cut temps first (cyclical is not a footnote) — then the same clients call back when the line restarts and they won’t over-hire.

Add the sister-model contrast lightly:

[Commercial janitorial](/models/commercial-janitorial) rides **sticky night contracts + thin service margins + night-crew retention**.  
[Home care medical](/models/home-care-medical) rides **clinical episodes + Medicare timing + nurse supply**.  
Staffing rides **wage markup + B2B sales culture + payroll float + WC + cyclical order books**.

Same “I need people who show up” demand emotion. Different invoice, different lawsuit, different cash gap.

That’s the engine. Not one viral “we hired 10,000 people” Reel.

## Micro: what actually drives one office

A single staffing office lives or dies on a few boring things:

**Sales culture (the career-defining line)**  
Brian’s Express–class teaching is blunt: this is a **B2B sales business that happens to be in staffing**. Franchise leadership on his podcast framed ideal owners as outbound-sales DNA — equipment / medical / tech sales backgrounds that actually *prospect* — and filtered hard against people who say “I’m good at sales” but mean retail counter closes. Education talk of **~200 cold calls a week** is a culture tell, not a motivational poster. If you won’t hit the street, network, and ask for the order, the model doesn’t care about your LinkedIn headline.

**Payroll float & funding**  
Associates expect weekly pay. Clients often pay Net-30/45/60+. That gap *is* the business. At scale, education frames put working-capital needs in the **hundreds of thousands** for mid-seven-figure books — or you factor / use a payroll funder and pay for it. Franchise systems that are the **employer of record and fund 100% of temp payroll** (Brian’s published Express framing) change the owner’s cash job dramatically vs an indie that must fund wages themselves. Underwrite *who* pays Friday before you celebrate the logo.

**Workers’ compensation & employment burden**  
Class codes, experience mods, state rules, light-industrial injury risk, warehouse vs office vs healthcare. A cheap bill rate that ignored WC is a slow-motion margin death. Unemployment claims, wage-hour mistakes, and misclassification risk sit next to WC — employment law is ops, not a binder on a shelf.

**Cyclical demand**  
Temps are often the first cut in a slowdown and the first call in a rebound. Client concentration in one plant, one 3PL, or one seasonal vertical turns “diversified agency” into a single-customer story. Plan for soft quarters while fixed office and sales payroll continue.

**Collections & credit**  
Winning the order from a slow-pay or soon-to-fail client is how you fund *their* float. DSO by client beats blended vanity. Early-pay discounts, credit checks, and the courage to fire a bad account matter as much as the opener script.

**Recruiter / sales bench**  
Early: owner is often the primary hunter for **6–12 months** (Brian’s interview framing) until a sales + recruiting team can run without heroics. Losing your only filler recruiter is not “one FTE” — it’s empty Monday shifts and a screaming client.

**Fill quality & show-up rate**  
No-shows, safety incidents, and warm-body fills that fail day two destroy accounts faster than a competitor’s price cut. The database, screening discipline, and backup bench are the product.

**Owner involvement**  
If you’re the only closer *and* the only person who can calm a plant manager at 5 a.m. *and* the only one who understands the aging report, you own a stressful sales job with employment-law side effects. Scale starts when sales + recruiting + back office (or franchisor back office) can run Tuesday without you on every order.

## The numbers (how to read them — not a promise)

Treat public blogs, ASA-class industry summaries, FDD Item 19 writeups, Brian’s published AAGM / sales education bands, and “owner income” ranges as **shape**, not your forecast. Always pull current FDD (if franchised), trailing sales by client, DSO, WC mod, and who funds payroll.

**Capacity / book intuition (category education — not your forecast):**

- Brian’s newsletter education (Express–class Item 19 era he published): average sales framing near **~$6.5M** for owners open **24+ months**; top-decile / top-unit stories in the **tens of millions** of sales (one cited top franchisee near **~$39–40M** in a given year) — *top-line sales, not owner profit*  
- Podcast education: first-year office averages cited near **~$1.3M** gross sales; territories open **5+ years** averaged near **~$6.4M** in the year discussed — verify current FDD  
- AAGM (“Average Annual Franchisee Share of Gross Margin” after loaded payroll costs & royalties) in that newsletter education grew from roughly **~$677k (2020) → ~$954k (2022)** system averages — still **not** your draw  
- Ramp table education in that same piece: mid-single-digit months of AAGM climbing toward **~$10k → ~$20k → ~$30k+/month** bands by roughly months 5 / 12 / 19 in *that* disclosed table — ramp is the unknown; don’t underwrite the best row

**Startup / CapEx education bands (category, wide):**

- Lean indie desk (home + job boards + working capital): often discussed **~$50k–$150k+** — float is the real capital  
- Franchise staffing offices (Express–class education Brian published): roughly **~$140k–$220k** all-in bands (fee, office, tech, training, working capital) depending on year/FDD — **verify the actual Item 7**  
- Indie that self-funds payroll: working capital can **dwarf** the office buildout — model weeks of wages × headcount on assignment  
- Resales appear regularly in Brian’s franchise-resale newsletters (six-figure asking prices in various metros) — price ≠ quality; underwrite clients, DSO, and sales dependency  
- Factoring / bank lines: price the fee into margin; dependency is a risk line

**Spread / float education shape:**

- GM after burden often **~18–28%** temp (segment-dependent); specialty desks can run higher  
- DSO education often **~45–75 days** in operator benchmarks; weekly payroll vs that gap is the float  
- At ~**$5M** sales, education chatter puts float / WC working-capital needs commonly in the **~$400k–$1M** conversation without a funder — illustrative, not your line  
- Confusing **markup** with **margin** is the classic pricing mistake

Your diligence questions should sound like:

- Trailing **sales vs gross margin vs AAGM / owner cash** (don’t stop at top-line)?  
- Mix: light industrial vs office vs skilled vs specialty; temp vs perm fees?  
- Client concentration (top 5 / top 1 as % of sales)?  
- DSO by client; bad-debt history; credit policy?  
- Who is employer of record? Who funds weekly payroll — you, factor, or franchisor?  
- WC experience mod, class mix, claims history?  
- Fill rate, show-up %, time-to-fill by job family?  
- Sales/recruiter count, tenure, comp plan, owner hours still on every hunt?  
- Cyclicality — last downturn sales drop; seasonal peaks?  
- If franchised: royalty on **gross margin** vs sales (Express–class teaching: royalty on GM after loaded costs — structure matters)? Does the system actually fund payroll, insure, invoice, and coach sales — or only a logo?

Same brain you’d use buying any high-volume, thin-spread B2B services shop: credit quality, cash timing, sales bench, and whether *you* want outbound prospecting and employment risk in your week.

## Biggest challenges (say them out loud)

This section is the point. Soft marketing skips it. Operators live it.

**1. Payroll float (the silent killer)**  
You pay Friday. They pay in 45 days — if they pay. Growth that looks like a win on the sales board can bankrupt an undercapitalized indie. Factoring fees and early-pay discounts are real margin taxes. Franchise EOR funding (when real) is a structural advantage — not a free lunch; you still live inside their rules and royalty.

**2. Workers’ compensation & burden creep**  
Wrong class codes, claim spikes, state rate changes, and “we’ll just price like the last guy” erase the spread. Light industrial and warehouse work carry injury reality. WC is a pricing input, not an afterthought binder.

**3. Cyclical demand**  
Temps get cut first. One big plant slows and your billable hours fall while recruiter salaries and rent don’t. Owners who underwrite only the boom year buy a surprise.

**4. Sales dependency / outbound culture**  
This is not inbound “Google Local and wait.” Brian’s published filter — and Express leadership on his podcast — keeps coming back to **outbound B2B sales**. Cold calls, door knocks, networking, follow-up discipline. Retail sales experience ≠ this job. Owners who won’t sell become owners who don’t last.

**5. Collections & client credit**  
Winning volume from a slow-pay or distressed buyer is how you finance someone else’s operations. Credit checks, deposits, and firing accounts feel mean until the aging report teaches you manners.

**6. Markup ≠ margin confusion**  
Pricing off a round markup without burden, OT premium, and WC is how “busy” offices lose money. Train every salesperson on loaded cost.

**7. Fill quality & no-shows**  
A placed body who ghosts Tuesday torches trust. Safety incidents on your W-2 can end accounts and raise mods. Screening shortcuts are expensive.

**8. Recruiter / sales bench fragility**  
One hunter leaves; the book softens. Comp plans that reward volume without margin or retention create zombie fills. Owner-as-only-closer caps the ceiling.

**9. Client concentration**  
One logistics account at 40% of sales feels great until their volume manager changes vendors. Diversification is ops, not a slide.

**10. Commodity price pressure / VMS**  
National MSPs and reverse auctions compress bill rates. Competing only on price trains clients to treat you like a interchangeable vendor — then punish you when commodity quality arrives.

**11. Employment-law / wage-hour / classification**  
Overtime, breaks, joint-employment theories, harassment claims, I-9 / E-Verify discipline. You’re in the people business with lawyers downstream.

**12. Associate supply in tight labor markets**  
When unemployment is low, recruiting cost and wage pressure climb while clients resist bill-rate increases. The dual market (sell clients *and* sell jobs to candidates) never sleeps — CORE teaching’s “people / team-based” point.

**13. Seasonality inside the cycle**  
Holidays, weather, school calendars, inventory peaks. Without a flex staffing plan you fire/rehire your way into chaos (Brian’s broader payroll/seasonal-staffing teaching applies even when the *agency* is the product).

**14. Owner-as-hero hunter**  
If only you can open accounts *and* only you can calm the angry plant *and* only you understand float, you didn’t buy freedom — you bought a high-stress B2B sales job with WC statements.

**15. Franchise / brand fee drag (if any)**  
Royalties on gross margin, brand funds, required vendors, territory rules help only if they fund payroll, shorten sales ramp, and improve fill. A logo does not make 200 cold calls.

**16. Confusing “staffing is a huge industry” headlines with “your collectible gross margin after float, WC, and sales payroll”**  
Same trap as every demand-story industry. Macro vanity ≠ your Monday show-up rate and your aging AR.

None of that means the model is bad.  
It means staffing is a **sales + spread + float** business where empty reqs, slow-pay clients, and rising burden decide whether the office survives the next downturn.

## What it takes (fit / anti-fit)

**This fits you if:**

- You’re wired for **outbound B2B sales** (or will hire and hold a hunter accountable) — and you won’t romanticize “I’m a people person” as a substitute  
- Credit memos, DSO dashboards, and WC class codes energize you more than they nauseate you  
- You’re capitalized for float *or* you’ve underwritten a real payroll-funding / EOR structure (franchise or indie) with eyes open  
- You can recruit recruiters and build a bench — dual-market leadership  
- You’ve compared this door to [commercial-janitorial](/models/commercial-janitorial) (contract service labor) and [home-care-medical](/models/home-care-medical) (clinical placement) and to lighter truck/route models — and still want the staffing stack  
- You’re honest about cyclicality: you can run lean in soft quarters without panic-cutting the sales engine that restarts the book

**This is a bad fit if:**

- You wanted a passive “people always need jobs” story from month one  
- You’re allergic to cold calls, rejection, or collections conversations  
- You’re undercapitalized for weekly payroll while AR ages (indie path) and you assumed the franchise brochure erased float without reading who actually funds it  
- You actually want **janitorial contracts**, **home care**, **offshore VA staffing**, or a **pure software / job-board** play — different underwriting  
- You thought “we’ll add healthcare IT next quarter” was a margin patch, not a new compliance and recruiter specialty  
- You need the trendiest vertical more than timeless fill-and-collect discipline in *your* trade area

Franchise vs independent is a separate call.  
Same physics either way: orders, fills, loaded spread, float, sales culture. A brand is only worth the fee if it funds payroll, invoices, insures, and shortens sales ramp — not because the website has stock photos of hard hats.

Many operators will correctly choose a **lighter** B2B services door or a non-float vehicle first. That’s a valid read, not a lack of ambition.

## Risks (the short list)

- Float / funding gap → growth that kills cash  
- WC claim spike or mispriced class codes → margin wipeout  
- Cyclical order collapse with high fixed sales payroll  
- Owner (or only hunter) stops outbound → pipeline death  
- Slow-pay / bankrupt client → you funded their labor  
- No-shows / safety incidents → account loss + mod damage  
- Client concentration → single-threaded revenue  
- VMS / price war → busy hours, thin spread  
- Employment-law event → legal + reputation spiral  
- Recruiter walk-off with relationships and database habits  
- Royalty / brand drag without funding or sales leverage  
- Buying a “book” that was one salesperson + one plant + one soft AR policy  
- Confusing industry boom headlines with collectible GM after burden

## The ownership point

You’re not buying “everybody needs workers.”

You’re buying the right to run a **billable-hour + placement machine** — if you can sell outbound without apology, price from loaded cost up, fund or structurally avoid the payroll float trap, carry WC and employment risk with discipline, collect before hope becomes policy, and not confuse top-line sales for free money when the cycle turns.

Some people should buy a tired office and fix sales process, credit policy, and fill quality (resale path Brian has pointed candidates toward as *category* inventory — diligence still required).  
Some people should open lean with a funder or franchise EOR stack and prove outbound before a second territory.  
Some people should choose [commercial-janitorial](/models/commercial-janitorial) or [home-care-medical](/models/home-care-medical) or a truck-based service business and never take on weekly associate payroll float.  
Some people should walk — and put the capital into a different vehicle entirely.

The question isn’t “do companies hire temps?”

It’s: **does this give you control of the outcome — or a stressful B2B sales job with WC statements, aging AR, and a personal guarantee when the only hunter who can open accounts texts “I’m done” in a downturn?**

If you want help comparing doors (this model vs other business-services vehicles vs home-service or senior-care paths), that’s a conversation.  
Not a pitch deck.
